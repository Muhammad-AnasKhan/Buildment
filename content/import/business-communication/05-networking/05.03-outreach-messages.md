## Why small requests work better

Think about the person reading your message. They are busy. They get messages from many people. They do not know you. When they open your message, they ask themselves two quick questions: "Who is this?" and "How much will this cost me?"

A big request costs a lot: a long call, a full portfolio review, a job. Saying yes to a stranger for that is hard. A small request costs a minute: one question, one piece of feedback. That is much easier to say yes to, and it can start a real conversation.

## Four parts of a good first message

1. **Who you are,** in one line.
2. **Why them.** Something specific: their post, their talk, their project. This shows you are not sending the same message to everyone.
3. **One small, specific request.** One question or one thing. Not three.
4. **An easy way to say no.** For example, "No problem if you are busy." This removes pressure, and people relax when they do not feel trapped.

Keep the whole message short enough to read on a phone without scrolling. A few sentences is enough.

## Before and after

Here is Bilal's first try, to Meera, an engineering manager at a company he admires:

> Hello dear, I hope you are doing well. I am a software engineer with many skills. I am searching for a job and I would be very grateful if you could help me. Please see my CV attached and tell me if there is any opening in your company. Thank you so much.

The intention is good, but the message is hard to answer. It does not say why he chose Meera. The request is large and vague. She would have to open an attachment from a stranger. And "Hello dear" can sound too personal in English business writing. "Hi Meera" is safer.

Here is his second version:

> Hi Meera, I read your post about how your team reviews pull requests within a day. I am a junior backend developer, and I am trying to set up a similar process for a three-person NGO project.
>
> One question: do your reviewers use a checklist, or does each reviewer decide what to check?
>
> A short answer would really help. No problem if you are too busy. Thanks, Bilal

It is about the same length. But now it has a reason, a real question and an easy exit.

## Match the request to the relationship

Start small. Ask for more only as the relationship grows.

| Stage | A request that fits |
|---|---|
| First message | One question they can answer in a minute |
| After one or two replies | Feedback on one specific thing, such as one project's README |
| After a real exchange | A 15-minute call on one topic |
| When they know your work | A referral, or an introduction to someone else |

If you ask for a call, make it easy. Say how long, what about, and offer two times in their time zone, for example: "Would 15 minutes on Tuesday or Wednesday at 10:00 your time work?"

## Connection notes

On LinkedIn you can usually add a short note when you send a connection request. LinkedIn limits the note's length, so keep it to two or three sentences. You do not need a request at all:

> Hi Marco, I enjoyed your post on testing forms with screen readers. I fixed a similar bug in my own project last month and would like to follow your work. Yazan

## Follow up once, then close the loop

If there is no answer after about a week, send **one** short follow-up. Do not add guilt or pressure.

> Hi Meera, I am sending this again in case it got lost. No pressure at all. Bilal

If there is still no answer, move on. Silence is not an insult.

When someone does help you, **close the loop**. This means you tell them what happened after their help, so the conversation feels complete. It is also one of the best ways to give back:

> Hi Meera, thank you for the checklist idea. We tried it this week and our reviews are faster and calmer. I really appreciate your time. Bilal

<Callout type="tip" title="Templates are a start, not the message">
It is fine to start from a template. But change the "why them" line and the request every time. If your message could be sent to anyone, people can tell.
</Callout>
