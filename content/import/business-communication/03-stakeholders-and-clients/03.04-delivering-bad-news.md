## Wednesday afternoon

The booking page launches next Monday. Claire has already told her students about it in the school newsletter.

On Wednesday, Rami tests what happens when two students book the last seat in a class at the same moment. Both get a confirmation. A class with ten seats could end up with eleven students.

Rami finds the cause. Fixing it properly, and testing the fix, will take about five working days. That means the full fix is ready on Thursday next week, three days after the launch.

## Why early is kinder

It is natural to wait. You hope to fix the problem quietly, or you want to have a perfect plan before you say anything. But bad news gets worse with time:

- **Options disappear.** On Wednesday Claire can move the launch, tell her students, or plan a workaround. On Sunday night she can do almost nothing.
- **Trust drops.** If Claire learns later that Rami knew on Wednesday, she will wonder what else he has not told her.
- **Someone else may tell her first.** A student or Samir could find the problem. Then Rami looks like he was hiding it.

You do not need a complete solution before you write. You need the facts you have, and a time when you will know more.

## The four parts

A good bad-news message has four parts, in this order:

| Part | Answers the question |
|---|---|
| **Facts** | What happened? Short and plain, no blame. |
| **Impact** | What does it change for you: dates, users, cost? |
| **Options** | What could we do? Two or three real choices, each with its cost. |
| **Recommendation** | Which option do I suggest, and why? |

Finish with a **next step**: what you need from the other person, by when, and when you will update them again.

## Rami's message

```text
Subject: Booking page: bug found in testing, launch options

Hi Claire,

While testing today, I found a bug. If two students book
the last seat in a class at the same moment, both get a
confirmation, so a class could have more students than
seats. No real students are affected, because the page is
not live yet. I should have tested this case earlier, and
I am sorry.

Impact: a proper fix, fully tested, takes about five
working days. It will be ready on Thursday next week,
three days after the planned launch on Monday.

Options:
1. Move the launch to Thursday next week. Everything
   works fully on day one.
2. Launch on Monday. Online booking closes when two seats
   are left, and students call Samir for the last two seats.
   The full fix goes live on Thursday next week.
3. Launch on Monday with no change. Samir checks for double
   bookings each morning and contacts any extra students.

My recommendation: option 2. You keep the date from your
newsletter, and no class can be overbooked. The cost is a
few extra calls for Samir until the fix is live, so it
would be good to check with him. I do not recommend
option 3: a student could arrive at a full class.

Could you tell me which option you prefer by tomorrow,
Thursday, at 12:00 your time? I am starting the fix today
either way, and I will send you an update on Friday.

Rami
```

Notice what the message does:

- The **subject line** is honest. Claire knows what the email is about before she opens it.
- The **facts** come first, in plain words. There is no code and no long technical story.
- Rami **owns his part** in one sentence and apologizes once. He does not apologize in every paragraph.
- The **impact** is about Claire's world: the launch date and her students.
- Each **option** has a cost. One of them is honest about being a bad idea.
- The **recommendation** gives a reason, and Claire still makes the final choice.
- The **next step** has a deadline and a time for the next update.

## Tone: calm, honest, brief

**Do not blame.** Even if the problem started with someone else, such as a late design or a broken library, describe what happened, not whose fault it was. Blame makes the reader defend people instead of solving the problem.

**Facts are not excuses.** "I lost two working days to power cuts" is a fact a client can understand. It becomes an excuse only when it replaces a plan. Say the fact once, then move to impact and options.

**Match the channel to the size.** A small delay fits in a chat message. A big problem, such as a missed launch or lost data, deserves a short call, followed by a written summary so nothing is forgotten.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not hide the news">
Avoid soft words that hide the problem, such as "a few small issues" or "slight changes to the timeline". If the date moves, say the new date. Vague news makes people imagine something worse.
</Callout>

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Rasha's app release for a client will be one week late, because a payment provider changed its rules. Which first sentence is best for her message to the client?"

  - "First of all, I am really sorry, I know how important this release is and I feel very bad about it."
  - "The payment provider changed its rules without any warning, so this delay is not on our side."
  - "We have had a few small challenges this week, which may affect the timeline a little bit."
  - "The release will move from 12 May to 19 May, because the payment provider changed its rules."
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "One short apology can be right, but this opening says nothing about what happened. The client still does not know the news."
  - "This starts with blame. Even if it is true, the client cares more about the new date than about whose fault it was."
  - "This hides the news. 'A few small challenges' and 'a little bit' make the client guess, and guessing makes people worry more."
  - "Right. It gives the fact, the new date and the cause in one plain sentence. The client knows the news at once."
```
