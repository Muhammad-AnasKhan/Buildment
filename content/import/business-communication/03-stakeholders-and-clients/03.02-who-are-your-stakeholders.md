## Rami's one-size update

At the end of each week, Rami sends one long update to Claire, Dina and Samir. It lists every task, every bug and every technical detail. He thinks this is the safest way: everyone gets everything.

By week three, Claire has stopped opening the updates. Dina only reads the first line. Samir does not understand most of the words.

## Four kinds of stakeholders

Start by listing everyone the project touches. It helps to think in four groups:

| Group | Who | Rami's project |
|---|---|---|
| **Decide** | Approves, pays, can stop the project | Claire, the school owner |
| **Build** | Does part of the work with you | Dina, the designer |
| **Use** | Uses the product every day | Samir, and the students |
| **Affected** | Not involved, but feels the result | The teachers, whose class lists change |

The people in the "decide" group are usually easy to remember, because they are in your inbox. The others are easy to forget. They often matter most for whether the project actually works.

<Callout type="warning" title="Watch for quiet stakeholders">
Some people never join a call, but your work changes their day: a receptionist, a support agent, a teacher, or the next developer who will maintain your code. Ask your client: "Who else will use this or be affected by it?"
</Callout>

## What does each person need to hear?

For each person on your list, answer three short questions:

1. **What do they care about?** Money and dates? Design quality? Saving time at work?
2. **What do they need from you?** Decisions to make, files to prepare, changes to learn?
3. **How often, and how?** A weekly email, a quick chat message, or a short call?

Here is Rami's stakeholder map after he did this:

| Person | Cares about | Needs from Rami |
|---|---|---|
| Claire | Launch date, cost, happy students | Weekly summary, decisions to make |
| Dina | Her designs built correctly | Questions on screens, early |
| Samir | Less work at the front desk | A preview and a short guide |
| Students | Booking in under a minute | A simple page that works on phones |

The students do not get messages from Rami. But they are still on the map, because their needs shape his decisions.

## Same news, different messages

When something changes, the news is the same for everyone, but the message is not. Imagine Rami decides to build the cancellation feature next week instead of this week. He writes three short messages:

```text
To Claire:
The cancellation feature will be ready next Thursday, not
this Thursday. The launch date does not change. No action
needed from you.

To Dina:
I am starting the cancellation screen next Monday. Could you
send the final version of that screen by Friday?

To Samir:
Next week you will be able to see cancelled bookings in the
admin page. I will send you a two-minute guide when it is ready.
```

Each message starts with what that person cares about, and ends with what they need to do, if anything. Each one is short enough to read on a phone.

## How much detail?

A simple rule: **the closer someone is to the work, the more detail they need.** Dina needs to know which screen and which button. Claire needs to know whether the date or the cost changes. Samir needs to know what will be different in his day.

If someone wants more detail, they will ask. That is much better than a long message nobody reads.
