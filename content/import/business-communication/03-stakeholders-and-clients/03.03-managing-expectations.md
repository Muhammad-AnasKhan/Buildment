## Where Rami's trouble started

Look again at Rami's week three. Claire changed the colors twice, which made the designs late. She asked for a waiting list as "one small extra". Samir expected to see cancelled bookings.

None of these people did anything wrong. Each one had a picture in their head of what the project included and how it would go. Nobody had written that picture down, so each person's picture was a little different.

The fix is simple, but it must happen at the start: agree three things, and put them in writing.

## Agree three things at the start

**1. Scope: what is in, and what is out.** List what you will deliver. Then list what you will **not** deliver. The "not included" list feels awkward to write, but it prevents most arguments later.

**2. Timing: dates, and what each date depends on.** Give dates for each milestone (a checkpoint where a part of the work is finished). Say what you need from others to keep each date, such as designs by a certain day.

**3. Communication: how and when you will talk.** How often you will send updates, which channel to use, how fast you will usually reply, and what happens if you lose power or internet for a day.

Here is the summary Rami should have sent after the first call:

```text
Subject: Booking page: what we agreed

Hi Claire,

Thank you for the call. Here is what we agreed:

Included: class calendar, booking form, confirmation email,
admin page for Samir to see and cancel bookings.
Not included: online payments, waiting lists, mobile app.
We can discuss these as a second phase.

Dates: designs from Dina by 10 March, test version by
24 March, launch on 7 April. The launch date depends on the
designs arriving on time.

Updates: every Thursday by email. For urgent questions,
WhatsApp. I usually reply within a few hours on working
days. If a power cut keeps me offline for more than a day,
I will tell you by phone or SMS as soon as I can.

Please reply if anything here is different from what you
expected.

Rami
```

The last line matters. It invites Claire to correct the picture now, while changing it is cheap.

## When a new request arrives

Even with a clear scope, clients ask for more. When small extras keep growing the work without anyone agreeing to a new date or price, this is called **scope creep**.


A good answer to a new request has two steps. First, say you will check the impact, and give a time when you will reply. Then come back with options, for example:

| Option | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Add it, move the date** | Waiting list included, launch one week later |
| **Swap it** | Waiting list in, another feature out, same date |
| **Later** | Launch on time, waiting list in a second phase |

Then let the client choose. It is their project and their money. Your job is to make the cost of each choice clear.

<Callout type="tip" title="Not a no, a choice">
You are not refusing the client. You are saying: "Yes, and here is what it costs." Most clients respect this, because it helps them decide.
</Callout>

## When things change, update early

Plans change. Designs arrive late, a library does not work as expected, your power is off for two days. The rule is: **tell people as soon as you know a date is at risk, not on the day it is due.**

If Rami had written to Claire on the day Dina's designs were late, Claire could have planned around it. If he waits until the launch date, she has no time left to adjust. The news is the same, but now it is a surprise.

Early updates can feel uncomfortable, especially when you do not have a solution yet. The next chapter shows you how to write them well.
