## One project, four messages

This is the main task of the course. You will write four pieces about the project you chose in chapter 7.1, check each one, and submit them together for mentor review.

You do not need to finish in one sitting. If the power goes or your connection drops, write in a phone notes app or on paper, then type it in when you can.

Each piece below has a short checklist. The right column shows the module and chapter that taught each point, so you can go back if one feels unclear.

## Step 1: Write a fact sheet

Before you write any message, write down five lines of facts. All four pieces use them, so they stay the same from the first email to the interview. Lamis, a freelance web developer, wrote this for her project:

```text
Project: volunteer sign-up web app for Bright Steps, an education NGO
Client: Ingrid, program manager, based in Berlin
Scope: sign-up form, volunteer list, email reminders. Not: payments, mobile app
Dates: start 3 March, first demo 17 March, launch 31 March
Updates: email every Thursday. I work 9:00 to 15:00 Gaza time
```

Lamis has one more fact. She is often offline in the afternoon, so she plans her important work for the morning. If a message arrives while she is offline, she answers it the next morning.

## Piece 1: The kickoff email

Send the kickoff email at the very start, usually right after the first call or after the contract is agreed. It puts the agreement in writing, so you and the client can both check it later. Aim for 100 to 180 words.

| Check | Taught in |
|---|---|
| Subject names the project and the purpose | 2.2 Emails that get answered |
| The first two lines say why you are writing | 2.2 Emails that get answered |
| Scope: what is included, and what is not | 3.3 Managing expectations |
| Dates for the first deliverable and the end | 3.3 Managing expectations |
| How and when you will send updates, with your hours | 3.3 Managing expectations |
| One clear next step for the reader, with a date | 2.2 Emails that get answered |
| Short sentences, polite and plain words | 1.2 Clear and polite |

## Piece 2: The weekly status update

Imagine it is week two of the project. Write the update you would send on the day you promised in your kickoff email. Aim for 60 to 120 words. Here is Lamis's:

> **Subject:** Bright Steps app: update, week 2
>
> Hi Ingrid,
>
> **Done:** The sign-up form works and saves volunteers to the list.
> **Next:** Email reminders and search, ready for the demo on 17 March.
> **Blockers:** I still need the reminder email text. Could you send it by Monday?
> **Status:** On track for the demo.
>
> Best, Lamis

| Check | Taught in |
|---|---|
| Done, next and blockers, clearly labeled | 2.3 Status updates and standups |
| Readable in under a minute: short lines, no long story | 2.3 Status updates and standups |
| Each blocker says what you need, from whom, by when | 2.3 Status updates and standups |
| Dates match your kickoff email, or the change is clear | 3.3 Managing expectations |
| One line on overall status: on track, at risk or off track | 2.3 Status updates and standups |

## Piece 3: The bad-news message

Use the problem you named in chapter 7.1, or another realistic one: a delay, a bug after launch, a service that stopped working, or a new request that does not fit the scope. Write it on the day you would first know, not on the deadline. Aim for 120 to 200 words.

You already wrote one bad-news message in the module 3 checklist, so use the same four parts: facts, impact, options and a recommendation. What is new here is that this message belongs to a project. Your client already has your kickoff email and your updates. So start from what you agreed: name the date or scope you promised, and say exactly what changes. If your module 3 message was about this same project, you can improve it with your mentor's feedback instead of starting again.

| Check | Taught in |
|---|---|
| Starts from what your kickoff email agreed, and says what changes | 3.3 Managing expectations |
| Sent as soon as you know, not on the deadline day | 3.4 Delivering bad news |
| The news is in the subject or the first two lines | 3.4 Delivering bad news |
| Facts: what happened, with no blame or excuses | 3.4 Delivering bad news |
| Impact: what it means for dates, cost or users | 3.4 Delivering bad news |
| Two or three options, and the one you recommend | 3.4 Delivering bad news |
| Written for the right reader, with the right detail | 3.2 Who are your stakeholders |
| What you need from them, and when they hear from you next | 3.4 Delivering bad news |

<Callout type="warning" title="Apologize once, promise carefully">
If part of the problem is your fault, say so in one sentence. A long apology takes space from what the reader needs: a way forward. Avoid promises you cannot keep, like "this will never happen again". A broken promise becomes your next bad-news message.
</Callout>

## Piece 4: The interview answer

Now imagine the project ended months ago. You are in an interview for a remote role, and the interviewer asks: "Tell me about a time a project did not go as planned." Answer with the story of your pack: the same problem as piece 3, told with the STAR method from module 6. Aim for 150 to 250 words, about one to two minutes when you say it aloud.

| Check | Taught in |
|---|---|
| Situation in one or two sentences: project, client, your role | 6.3 Behavioral questions |
| Task: what you were responsible for | 6.3 Behavioral questions |
| Action: what you did, with "I", in a few concrete steps | 6.3 Behavioral questions |
| Result: a fact or number, and what you learned | 6.3 Behavioral questions |
| Same problem, dates and facts as your bad-news message | 7.2 Step 1: your fact sheet |

Your best evidence is in the other three pieces. You shared the problem early, with options. Say that in the action part: it shows an interviewer how you communicate, not just what you built.

Which part of STAR needs the most work?"
```quiz
options:
  - "Situation: the client, the project and the deadline"
  - "Task: what she was responsible for on the team"

  - "The situation is clear in one sentence: an NGO client, a donation form and four weeks."
  - "Her task is clear. She was the only developer, so the form was her responsibility."

  - "The result is strong: a short delay, a launch, and a number that shows the impact."
```

## Step 2: Read it as one story

Before you submit, read all four pieces together, as your mentor will.

- Are the names, dates and scope the same in every piece?
- Does the bad-news message refer to what the kickoff email promised?
- Does the interview answer tell the same story, from the other side?
- Did you read each piece aloud once and cut words you would not say?
- Did you remove private details and change names where needed?

<Callout type="info" title="What your mentor looks for">
Your mentor reads your pack like a client or team lead would. They check whether each piece is clear, polite and complete, and whether the four pieces fit together. Expect comments and questions, not a score.
</Callout>
