## From practice to your own project

In six modules you practiced one kind of message at a time: a polite email, a standup update, a delay message, an outreach note, an interview answer. Each practice was useful because it was small and safe.

Real work does not come one message at a time. One project brings many messages, and they all connect. The client remembers the date in your first email. Your status update is measured against it. When something goes wrong, your message about it is read next to everything you promised before.

In this capstone you write four pieces about **one** project:

| Piece | When it happens in a real project |
|---|---|
| 1. Kickoff email | Day one: you agree what, when and how |
| 2. Weekly status update | Week two or three: you report progress |
| 3. Bad-news message | Something goes wrong: a delay or a problem |
| 4. Interview answer | Months later: you tell the story to an employer |

The four pieces tell one story: the same client, the same dates, the same problem. That is the hard part, and also the useful part. A mentor will read your pack and give you feedback, just like a team lead would read your messages on a real project.

## Why this is worth your time

Many fellows will work with clients and teams they never meet in person. Those people judge you by your messages. A clear kickoff, a short update and an honest delay message build trust faster than any CV line.

You also keep the pack. Next time you start a real project, you can copy its shape and change the facts. And the interview answer is one you can use, almost word for word, in a real interview.

## Choosing a good project

Dalia is a junior full-stack developer. She studies software engineering and freelances part time. Her first idea for the capstone was "a to-do app I built in one evening for a tutorial". It is real, but there was no client, no deadline and nothing to report. Her second idea was "everything I did in my internship". It is too big for four messages.

Her third idea worked: the registration form and dashboard she built for an NGO's summer coding camp. It had a client, a five-week timeline, a designer she worked with, and a real problem: one week before launch, the client asked for a new feature that was not in the plan.

The project can be **real** or **realistic**. If you have not had a client yet, use a university team project, volunteer work, or a made-up client based on a real kind of job, such as a booking page for a small gym. Tell your mentor which one it is.

A good capstone project has these five things:

1. **Someone you deliver to.** A client, a team lead, an NGO, a professor or your team.
2. **A few weeks of work.** At least two or three weeks, so a weekly update makes sense.
3. **Something that can go wrong.** A delay, a bug, a new request, lost access, or a power or internet problem.
4. **Work you can describe with "I".** You did it, or you would do it in the realistic version.
5. **Details you can share safely.** You can change names and leave out private data.

| Good fits | Weak fits |
|---|---|
| A website for a local business, built over a month | A one-evening tutorial app with no user |
| A university team project with a supervisor | Everything you did in your last job |
| A sign-up form for an NGO event | A project you only watched others build |
| A made-up client: a booking page for a gym | A project full of secrets you cannot change |

<Callout type="warning" title="Protect other people's privacy">
Your mentor will read your pack. Do not include passwords, private client data, prices you promised to keep secret, or real email addresses. Change names if you need to, for example "Client A".
</Callout>
