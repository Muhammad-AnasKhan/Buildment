## Karim's email that waited a week

Karim is a freelance backend developer. He is building an online booking system for Emma, who runs a small guesthouse in Edinburgh. To finish the payment feature, he needs Emma to create an account with a payment company and send him the test keys.

Here is the email he sent:

```text
Subject: Hello

Dear Emma,

I hope you are well. This week I worked on the booking
calendar and fixed the bug with the dates. I also started
the payment part. The payment company has a test mode,
which is useful for developers. I read their documents
and the setup looks fine. To continue I would need the
test keys from your account if possible.

Best regards,
Karim
```

Emma read it on her phone between two guests at the front desk. She saw "Hello", read the first lines about the calendar, thought "good, it is going well", and closed it. Karim waited six days.

Emma is not lazy. She just did not see the request. It was hidden in the last lines, and nothing told her it was urgent.

## Three parts of an email that gets answered

### 1. A subject that says what the email is about

The subject is the first thing people see in a full inbox. A good subject tells the reader the topic and, if needed, the action and the date. "Hello", "Question" or "Update" tell them nothing.

| Weak subject | Clear subject |
|---|---|
| Hello | Need payment test keys by Thursday |
| Question | Question: which logo should the website use? |
| Update | Booking system: week 3 progress |
| Urgent!!! | Site down since 09:00: please check hosting email |

### 2. The request first

Put your request in the first one or two sentences. Then give the short reason or background. Many professionals call this "bottom line up front": the main point comes first, and the details come after.

Your reader can stop reading after two lines and still know what you need. If they want the background, it is there.

### 3. A specific next step

End with a clear call to action: who does what, and by when. Make it easy to say yes. If the task is unclear for the reader, give them the steps or offer to help.

- Weak: "Let me know your thoughts."
- Clear: "Could you send the test keys by Thursday? If it helps, I can join a 10-minute call and guide you through it."

## Karim's email, rewritten

```text
Subject: Need payment test keys by Thursday

Hi Emma,

Could you send me the test keys from your payment account
by Thursday? I need them to finish and test the payment
feature this week.

How to find them:
1. Log in to your payment company account.
2. Open the Developers page.
3. Copy the two "test" keys and send them to me.

Please send only the test keys, not the live ones.

Other news: the booking calendar is finished and the date
bug is fixed.

If Thursday is hard for you, tell me and we can plan a
short call instead.

Thanks,
Karim
```

The rewrite is not much shorter. It is ordered for the reader: request, reason, steps, and the extra news at the end. Emma answered the same evening.

<Callout type="tip" title="One email, one topic">
If you need two unrelated things, send two emails with two clear subjects. A reader may answer one request and forget the second one hidden below it.
</Callout>

## When there is still no answer

Sometimes a clear email still gets no reply. People are busy, and emails get lost. A **follow-up** is a short, polite second message. Send it after two or three working days, as a reply to your first email, so the details stay together:

> Hi Emma, just following up on the test keys below. Thursday is still my target to finish payments. Is that still possible for you?

Do not complain about the wait. Repeat the request and the date, and keep it friendly.
