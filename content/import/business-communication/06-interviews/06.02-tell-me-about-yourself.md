## The question that sounds easy

"So, tell me about yourself." Many interviews start this way. It sounds like small talk (light, friendly chat), but it is not. The interviewer wants a short summary of who you are as a professional, and a reason to keep listening.

Most people answer badly because they did not prepare. Some tell their whole life story. Some read their CV (the document that lists your education and work) line by line. Some say one sentence and stop. All three waste the best minute of the interview.

## Three parts: present, past, why this role

A simple structure keeps your answer short and clear. Speak about **one to two minutes**, which is about 120 to 250 words.

| Part | What to say | Length |
|---|---|---|
| **Present** | What you do now, your main skills, one recent piece of work | 2 or 3 sentences |
| **Past** | How you got here: study, first projects, one important step | 2 or 3 sentences |
| **Why this role** | Why this company and this role fit what you want to do next | 1 or 2 sentences |

Start with the present because it matters most to the interviewer. The past explains how you got there. The last part connects your story to their job, so the answer ends on them, not on you.

## A full example

Here is Hamza's answer. Read it aloud: it takes about one minute.

> "I am a backend developer, and I mostly work with Node.js and PostgreSQL. Right now I am finishing my computer science degree, and I work part time as a freelancer. My latest project is an API for a small online bookshop. It handles orders and payments for about 200 customers a month.
>
> I started coding at university three years ago. In my second year I joined a fellowship for developers, where I worked in a remote team with mentors in Europe. That is where I learned to write clear pull requests and to work across time zones.
>
> I applied for this role because your team builds tools for small shops, and that is the work I enjoy most. I also want to grow by working with senior engineers on a product with real users."

Notice what Hamza does:

- He gives **facts**, not adjectives. "An API for 200 customers a month" says more than "I am hardworking and passionate."
- He mentions **remote work skills**: pull requests and time zones.
- He ends with **their company**, so the interviewer can easily ask a next question.

## Make it yours

Change the answer for each role. The present and past parts stay mostly the same. The "why this role" part must be new each time, so read about the company first.

Do not memorize every word. Memorized answers sound like reading, and if you forget one word you may lose the rest. Instead, remember the three parts and two or three key facts for each. Then practice out loud, with a timer, until it feels natural.

<Callout type="tip" title="What to leave out">
Leave out your age, family, religion and politics. They do not help the interviewer judge your work. You also do not have to talk about hard personal circumstances. If you choose to, keep it to one sentence and link it to a strength, for example: "I plan my work around power cuts, so I always keep a backup and deliver on time."
</Callout>

## Common questions

**Q: What if I have no work experience yet?**
A: Use what you have: university projects, freelance tasks, volunteering, a fellowship or a personal app. A real project with real users, even 20 friends, is a strong present.

**Q: Should I say where I live?**
A: You can say it in a few words if it helps, for example to show your time zone and working hours. Keep the focus on your work.
