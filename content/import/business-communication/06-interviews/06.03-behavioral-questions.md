## Questions about the past

Many interview questions start with "Tell me about a time when..." For example:

- "Tell me about a time you solved a difficult problem."
- "Tell me about a time you made a mistake."
- "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate."

These are called **behavioral questions**. The idea behind them is simple: what you did in the past is good evidence of what you will do in the future. So the interviewer does not want your opinion about work. They want a real story.

## The STAR method

STAR is a common interview method for telling a work story. Many recruiters and career guides recommend it, because it gives the interviewer what they need in a clear order.

| Part | What it answers | Share of your answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | Where and when? What was the problem? | Short, about one fifth |
| **Task** | What was your job or goal in it? | Short, one or two sentences |
| **Action** | What did **you** do, step by step? | The longest part, more than half |
| **Result** | What changed? What did you learn? | Short, with a number if you have one |

The most common mistake is spending too long on the situation. The interviewer does not need every detail of the client or the project. They need enough to understand the problem, and then they want to hear what you did.

## A full example

Here is Nour's full answer. She is a junior web developer, and this story comes from volunteer work. It takes about two minutes to say.

> **Situation:** "Last year I volunteered as the only developer for a small NGO that runs learning sessions for children. Teachers recorded attendance in an online form, but the internet in the learning centers was weak, so many entries were lost. The NGO needed this data for its monthly report to a donor."
>
> **Task:** "My job was to make attendance recording reliable before the new term started, in three weeks, with no budget for paid tools."
>
> **Action:** "First, I had short calls with three teachers to see how they used the form. I found that it failed whenever the connection dropped while sending. So I built a simple web app that saves each entry on the phone first and sends it when the connection comes back. I tested it with two teachers for one week and fixed the two bugs they found. Then I wrote a one-page guide in Arabic and English and showed all the teachers how to use it in a 20 minute call."
>
> **Result:** "In the first month, 12 teachers used the app and recorded about 1,800 attendance entries. The coordinator reported no lost entries, and the NGO sent its donor report on time. I also learned to talk to users before I write code: the problem was not the form, it was the connection."

Look at what makes it strong:

- **Situation and Task** take only four sentences together.
- **Action** is the longest part. Each sentence starts with "I" and a clear verb: had calls, found, built, tested, wrote.
- **Result** has numbers (12 teachers, 1,800 entries) and one lesson learned.

## Say "I", not only "we"

Most real work happens in teams, and that is fine. But the interviewer is hiring you, not your team. If every sentence says "we", they cannot tell what you did.

Name the team in the situation, then use "I" in the action: "Our team of four was building a checkout page. I was responsible for the payment form. I found the bug..." This is honest, and it shows your part clearly.

## Real stories only

Never invent a story or make your part bigger than it was. Interviewers ask **follow-up questions**, such as "What exactly did you build?" or "What would you do differently?" Invented details are easy to spot, and trust is hard to win back. A small, true story is always better than a big, made-up one.

Stories that went wrong are fine too, especially for questions about mistakes. In the result, say what you learned and what you now do differently.

## Build a story bank

You do not need a new story for every question. Four or five good stories, told with STAR, can answer most behavioral questions. This is your **story bank**. Your stories can come from work, freelancing, university projects, volunteering or a fellowship.

| Common question | A story that could answer it |
|---|---|
| A time you solved a hard problem | A bug you tracked down, or a slow page you made faster |
| A time you made a mistake | A missed deadline, and what you changed afterwards |
| A time you worked with a difficult person | A client who changed the scope, or a teammate you disagreed with |
| A time you learned something fast | A new tool or language you learned for a project |
| A time you worked under pressure | A deadline during power or internet cuts, and how you planned |

```quiz
  - "Task"
  - "Action"
  - "Result"
correct: 2
explanations:
  - "The situation describes the problem and the background. This sentence describes something Karim did."
  - "The task is Karim's job or goal. This sentence describes the step he took to reach it."
  - "Right. It says what Karim did, with a clear verb: 'I wrote a script'. That is the action."
  - "The result is what changed afterwards, such as fewer failed backups. This sentence is still about what he did."
```
