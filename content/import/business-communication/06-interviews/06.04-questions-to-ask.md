## Your turn to ask

Near the end of most interviews, the interviewer says: "Do you have any questions for us?" Many candidates are tired or nervous by then, so they say "No, I think you covered everything." That is a missed chance.

An interview goes both ways. The company is deciding if you fit the role. You are also deciding if the role fits you: the work, the team, the hours, the support you will get. Good questions help you decide, and they show the interviewer how you think.

## Three areas to ask about

Prepare four or five questions before the call, and ask two or three of them. Some may already be answered during the interview, so it helps to have extras. Choose from three areas:

| Area | Example questions |
|---|---|
| **The role** | What would success look like in the first three months? What is the biggest challenge in this role? |
| **The team** | Who would I work with most? How does the team give feedback or review code? |
| **How work is done** | How many hours does the team overlap? What is done in meetings, and what in writing? |

For remote roles, the third area is very important. The answers tell you how your days will feel. A team that writes decisions down and works well asynchronously (not at the same time) is easier to join from another time zone.

You can also ask about onboarding: "What would my first two weeks look like?" This is a useful question for any new joiner, and it shows you want to start well.

<Callout type="tip" title="Listen and ask a follow-up">
Do not just read your list of questions. Listen to each answer and react to it. If Laura says "Our biggest challenge is slow releases," you can ask: "What is slowing them down right now?" A real conversation leaves a stronger impression than a list.
</Callout>

## Questions to avoid

Some questions make a weak impression, even when they are polite:

- **Questions the website answers,** such as "What does your company do?" It suggests you did not prepare.
- **"Did I get the job?"** The interviewer usually cannot answer yet. Ask about the next steps instead: "What are the next steps in the process?"
- **Only questions about time off or benefits** as your first questions. These matter, but leading with them can suggest the work itself interests you less.

Questions about pay, contract type and how payment works are fair and important, especially when you work from another country. Many companies discuss them with the recruiter, often in the first call or when they make an offer. If nobody brings them up, ask the recruiter politely before you accept an offer.

## After the call

Send a short thank-you email within one day. It is polite, and it reminds the interviewer of you. Mention one specific thing from the conversation:

```text
Subject: Thank you for today's interview

Hi Laura,

Thank you for your time today. I enjoyed hearing about your plan
to speed up releases, and it made me even more interested in the role.

I look forward to hearing about the next steps.

Best regards,
Aisha
```
