## A message full of short forms

Khaled starts his first remote job as a junior developer at a small startup. On his first morning, his team lead, Priya, writes to him in the team chat:

> FYI, the client changed the scope again. Can you give me an ETA on the login deliverable by EOD? If you have a blocker, tell me and we can sync tomorrow.

Khaled knows English well. He still is not sure what Priya wants from him. The message uses seven business terms in three sentences. This is normal in many teams, and it is easy to learn.

## Ten terms you will see every week

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | The work a project includes, and what it does not | "Dark mode is not in the scope of this project." |
| **Deliverable** | A finished piece of work you hand over | "The first deliverable is the home page design." |
| **ETA** | Estimated time of arrival: when you expect to finish | "What is the ETA for the bug fix?" |
| **Blocker** | A problem that stops your work until it is solved | "My blocker is that I still have no server access." |
| **Follow up** | To check back later on something you discussed | "I will follow up with the client on Monday." |
| **Stakeholder** | A person affected by your work or interested in it | "The NGO director is a key stakeholder for this app." |
| **EOD** | End of day: by the end of the working day | "I will send the report by EOD." |
| **ASAP** | As soon as possible | "Please fix the payment bug ASAP. Customers cannot pay." |
| **FYI** | For your information: you only need to know this | "FYI, the office is closed on Friday." |
| **Sync** | A short talk or call to share updates and agree | "Can we have a quick sync about the API tomorrow?" |

Two small grammar notes. "Follow up" (two words) is the action: "I will follow up." "Follow-up" (with a hyphen) is the thing: "I sent a follow-up." And "sync" can be a noun ("a quick sync") or a verb ("let's sync tomorrow").

Now Khaled can read Priya's message in plain English:

> For your information, the client changed what the project includes. Before the end of today, can you tell me when you expect to finish the login feature? If something stops your work, tell me and we can talk tomorrow.

## Three terms that need care

**EOD across time zones.** "End of day" depends on whose day it is. When you work with people in other time zones, write a time and a zone: "by 5 pm your time" or "by 15:00 UTC". UTC is a world standard time that does not change with the seasons. When you receive "EOD", confirm the time if you are not sure.

**ASAP is not a time.** Everyone's "as soon as possible" is different, and "ASAP" can also sound pushy (like too much pressure). When you write it, add a time and a reason: "Could you fix this by noon? Customers cannot pay." When you receive it, it is fine to ask: "Is tomorrow morning OK, or do you need it today?"

**Scope protects you.** "Out of scope" is a polite, professional way to say "this was not part of our agreement". It is not rude. It lets you offer the extra work as a separate task, with its own time and price. You will practice this with clients in module 3.

<Callout type="tip" title="FYI means no reply needed, not no reading needed">
An FYI message usually does not ask you to do anything. But read it carefully. A change in a date or a plan can still affect your own work.
</Callout>

## Using the terms yourself

Do not fill every message with short forms. A client outside tech, or the staff of a small NGO, may not know them. A simple rule: use them with teammates who use them, and spell them out for new clients and non-technical readers.

And if you do not know a term, ask. "Sorry, what do you mean by 'sync' here: a call, or a chat message?" Asking is professional. Guessing wrong and missing a deadline is not.

  - "FYI does not ask for a plan. If Priya wanted one, she would ask for it directly."
