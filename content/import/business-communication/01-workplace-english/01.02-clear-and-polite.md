## Clear first

A clear message is easy to understand the first time. Your reader may also speak English as a second language. They may read on a phone, or at the end of a long day. Help them with three habits:

- **Short sentences.** One idea per sentence. If a sentence has three commas, split it.
- **Simple words.** "Use" instead of "utilize". "Start" instead of "commence". "Help" instead of "facilitate".
- **Specific details.** Say what, by when, and why. Words like "soon" and "some changes" make the reader guess.

Here is a message a developer sent to a client after a call:

> I wanted to let you know that regarding the changes that you requested in our last call, some of them have been completed already but there are some others that I will need more information about before I can proceed with them.

And here is the same message, made clear:

> I finished three of the five changes from our call. I have one question about each of the other two. Could you answer them by Wednesday?

The second version is shorter, it gives numbers and a date, and it is still polite.

## Polite in English

This part surprises many people. In many languages, including Arabic, a direct request is polite when it comes with a warm greeting and a respectful way of addressing the person. That is a real and good kind of politeness.

English, especially in international workplaces, often does it differently. It turns orders into questions, and it adds small "softening" words. So a direct sentence that is normal in your language can sound like an order when you translate it word for word.

Neither way is wrong. The two languages use different tools. At work in English, it helps to know the English tools.

## Phrases that can sound rude by accident

| Can sound rude | Try instead |
|---|---|
| Send me the file. | Could you send me the file, please? |
| I need it today. | Could I have it by 4 pm today? I need it for the demo. |
| Why didn't you reply? | Just checking that you saw my message from Monday. |
| You are wrong. | I think there may be a mistake here. Could we check it together? |
| What? I don't understand. | Sorry, could you explain what you mean by "release"? |
| Do it again. | Could you take another look at this part? |
| Hurry up. | Is there any chance this could be ready by Thursday? |
| OK. | Thanks, that works for me. |

The left column is not bad English. It is grammatically correct. It just carries a tone that the writer usually did not mean.

## Your polite toolkit

- **Ask, do not order.** Start requests with "Could you...?", "Would you be able to...?" or "Can you...?"
- **Please and thank you.** "Please" helps, but "Please send the file" can still feel like an order. A question is softer.
- **Softeners.** Words like "I think", "it seems" and "a little" make a problem or a disagreement easier to hear: "It seems the page is a little slow on phones."
- **A reason.** "...because the client demo is at 3 pm." A reason makes a request feel fair, not like an order.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not soften so much that the request disappears">
"I was just wondering if maybe, when you have time, you could possibly look at the file?" is polite, but the reader cannot tell what you need or when. Clear and polite: "Could you review the file by Thursday? I want to send it to the client on Friday." Also avoid ALL CAPITAL LETTERS and marks like "???" or "!!!". In English they read as shouting or anger.
</Callout>

## Putting it together

A simple pattern works for most requests: a greeting, the request as a question, a reason or a deadline, and thanks.

> Hi Daniel, could you send me the product photos by Wednesday? I need them to finish the shop page. Thanks!

It takes ten more seconds to write than "Send me the photos." It can save days of slow replies.
