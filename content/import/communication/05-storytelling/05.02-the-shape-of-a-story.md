## A story that goes nowhere

Ahmed is in a job interview with a software company in the Netherlands. The interviewer asks: "Tell me about a project you are proud of."

Ahmed starts with the tools his team used. Then he describes the team, the client, the weekly meetings and the hosting. After three minutes, he has not said what went wrong, what he did, or what changed. The interviewer politely stops him and moves to the next question.

Everything Ahmed said was true. But it was not a story. A story needs something to change.

## Five parts

A simple shape works for almost every work story. Each part answers one question for the listener.

| Part | The question it answers | Length |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | Where were you, and what was going on? | One sentence |
| **Problem** | What went wrong, or what was at risk? | One or two sentences |
| **What you did** | Which actions did you take yourself? | Two or three sentences |
| **Result** | What changed? Can you give a number? | One sentence |
| **Lesson** | What did you learn, or what does this show? | One sentence |

Here is a story from Samah, a freelance developer, with each part marked:

> **Situation:** Last spring I built a booking app for a small driving school.
> **Problem:** After launch, about a third of students missed their lessons. The reminder messages were going out at 3 a.m., because the server used a different time zone from the school.
> **What I did:** I found the bug in the logs, fixed the time zone, and added a test so it could not come back. I also asked the school which reminder time suited their students.
> **Result:** Within a month, missed lessons fell to about one in ten.
> **Lesson:** Now I check time zones in every app that sends messages.

It takes less than a minute to say. It has a person, a problem, clear actions, a number and a lesson. When you tell it, you do not say the labels. They are only here to show the shape.

## Get to the problem quickly

```predict
explanation: "The second opening names the problem in the first sentence. It makes the listener ask 'why?', and that question keeps them listening."
```

The problem is the engine of a story. Until the listener knows what went wrong, they do not know why they are listening. So keep the situation to one sentence and get to the problem fast. You can add background later if someone asks.

## Common mistakes

- **Too much situation.** Like Ahmed, you spend most of the time on tools and team, and the story starts too late.
- **"We" all the way through.** The team matters, but in an interview the listener wants to know what **you** did. Say "we" for the team and "I" for your own part.
- **A vague result.** "It went well" tells the listener nothing. Give a number or a clear change: "errors fell by half", "the client signed a second contract".
- **No lesson.** Without it, the listener wonders why you told the story.
- **Making it bigger than it was.** Keep it true. A small true story is stronger than a big story you cannot defend when someone asks a follow-up question.

<Callout type="tip" title="You choose what to share">
Some of your strongest stories may come from hard times: power cuts, lost work, studying while working. You never have to share personal hardship at work. If you choose to, keep the focus on what you did and what you learned.
</Callout>

## How long should it be?

Most work stories should take under a minute to say: about 80 to 120 words. Add one small detail that makes it real, such as a name, a number or a time of day ("3 a.m."). Then say it out loud once and time yourself. If it runs over a minute, cut the situation first.
