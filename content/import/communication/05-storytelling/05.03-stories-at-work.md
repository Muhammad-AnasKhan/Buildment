## A good story, the wrong moment

Dalia works remotely for a startup in Berlin. In the Monday call, the team lead asks: "Will the release be ready on Friday?"

Dalia tells a story about how she learned Docker last month: the tutorials, the errors, the night it finally worked. It is a good story. But the team lead asked about Friday, and after two minutes he still does not know the answer.

The story was not wrong. It was not **relevant**: it did not answer what the listener needed at that moment.

## Start from the point, then choose the story

In module 1 you learned to start with the point. Stories follow the same rule. Before you tell a story, ask yourself two questions:

1. **What do I want this person to remember or do?**
2. **Which true story proves that point best?**

If no story proves your point, do not tell one. A short, clear answer is always better than a story that wanders.

## Three common moments

| Moment | What the listener wants | Story length |
|---|---|---|
| Update to a team or client | The status, and what it means for them | Two or three sentences, after the point |
| Pitch or demo | A reason to care about the problem | One person's problem, about 30 seconds |
| Job interview | Evidence of how you work | About one to two minutes |

### In an update

Give the point first. Then use a very short story as evidence. Here is a message from Faris to a client about a delay:

> The new search will ship on Thursday, one day late. Here is why: during testing, my teammate searched for a customer's name in Arabic and got no results. The search did not handle Arabic letters correctly. I fixed it and added tests for Arabic and English names. One extra day now saves your support team many complaints later.

The client gets the answer in the first line. The story explains the delay in a way they will remember and can repeat to their own manager.

### In a pitch or a demo

In module 4 you learned to start a demo by showing the problem. A story about one real person is a strong way to do that. Yara opens her pitch to a local startup program like this:

> Last winter my cousin's school closed for weeks. His teacher sent homework on WhatsApp, and forty students sent photos of their answers back to one phone. Nothing was organized, and many answers were lost. We built a simple app that collects homework by class and works on slow connections.

One person and one clear problem make the listener care before they see a single screen.

### In a job interview

Many interviewers ask **behavioral questions**: questions about what you did in the past, often starting with "Tell me about a time when...". The idea is that how you acted before shows how you will act again.

STAR is a common interview method: **Situation, Task, Action, Result**. It is the same idea as the five-part shape from chapter 5.2. Our shape adds a lesson at the end, which shows the interviewer that you learn from experience.

Before an interview, prepare a small **story bank**: four or five true stories you can tell well. Good ones to have ready:

- A mistake you made, and how you fixed it
- A disagreement with a teammate or client, and how you handled it
- Something new you had to learn fast
- A tight deadline, or a problem outside your control, such as a power cut
- A time you helped someone else succeed

One story can answer several questions. You only change which part you stress.

## Keep it short

- Cut the situation to one sentence.
- Use one real detail, not five.
- Stop after the lesson. Do not add a second story.
- Say it out loud and time it before the real moment.

<Callout type="warning" title="Protect other people">
Work stories often include clients and teammates. Do not share private details, and do not name a client in public without their permission. "A clinic I worked for" is enough.
</Callout>
