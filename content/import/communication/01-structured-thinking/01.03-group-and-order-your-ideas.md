## Nine points, no shape

Noor is a developer at a health NGO. Every Friday she sends an update to her manager, Tom. This week she writes down everything she can remember:

> - Fixed the login bug on Android
> - The Arabic translation of the forms is not finished
> - Next week I will start the reports page
> - I need access to the new server
> - Wrote tests for the patient form
> - Meeting with the clinic staff on Tuesday
> - The Arabic font looks wrong on old phones
> - Updated the user guide
> - I need a decision on the report format

Every point is true and useful. But Tom has to read all nine to find the two things he must do. If he skims, he may miss them.

## Why a few groups work better

People remember a few groups much more easily than a long, flat list. A label also does work for the reader: it says what kind of point comes next, so they know whether to read it carefully or skim it.

Here is Noor's update with the point first and three groups:

> Hi Tom, I need two things from you this week: access to the new server, and a decision on the report format.
>
> **Need from you**
> - Access to the new server, so I can test with real data
> - A decision on the report format (PDF or Excel), so I can start the reports page
>
> **Done this week**
> - Fixed the login bug on Android
> - Wrote tests for the patient form
> - Updated the user guide
>
> **Next week**
> - Finish the Arabic translation and fix the font on old phones
> - Start the reports page
> - Meeting with the clinic staff on Tuesday

The facts did not change. Only the shape did.

## How to group your points

1. **List** every point first, without judging them.
2. **Look** for what points share: the same kind, the same time, or the same person who must act.
3. **Make two to four groups** and give each one a short label.
4. **Check** that each point fits in only one group, and that nothing important is missing.

If you end up with five or more groups, join some of them or cut the weakest points. If everything fits in one group, you do not need labels at all.

## Common ways to group

| Group by | Example labels | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Status | Done, in progress, blocked | Weekly updates |
| Time | Before, now, next | Plans and project history |
| Who acts | Need from you, I will do, for your information | Messages to managers and clients |
| Kind of reason | Cost, time, risk | Recommendations |
| Option | Option A, option B | Comparing two choices |

"For your information" means "you do not need to act, but you should know this". You will often see it shortened to **FYI**.

## Put the groups in order

Groups also need an order. Three simple rules cover most messages:

- **Action first.** Right after the main point, put the group the reader must act on.
- **Most important next.** Then go from the most important group to the least.
- **Time order for steps.** If the groups are steps or stages, put them in the order they happen.

Choose one order and keep it. Then tell the reader how many groups are coming: "Two reasons: cost and time." The number helps them follow you and notice if something is missing.

**A short example.** Ibrahim recommends a tool to his startup's founder:

> I recommend we use a ready-made booking tool for the first version, instead of building our own. Three reasons: time, cost and risk.
>
> **Time:** we can launch in two weeks, not two months.
>
> **Cost:** the monthly fee is less than one week of developer time.
>
> **Risk:** if it does not fit, we can still build our own later.

The reader gets the recommendation in the first line, sees that three reasons are coming, and can check each one.

## Common questions

**Q: I have heard the word MECE. What is it?**
A: It stands for "mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive", and it is linked to Barbara Minto's work at McKinsey. In plain English: no point appears in two groups, and no important point is left out. It is the same check as step 4 above.

**Q: Do I need labels in a short chat message?**
A: Not always. In a short message, a sentence like "Two things: the logo and the colors" does the same job as labels.
