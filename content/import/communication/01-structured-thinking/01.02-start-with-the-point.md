## Story order and answer order

When we write, we usually follow **story order**: what happened first, what we tried next, what went wrong, and finally what we need. That is the order in which we lived it, so it feels natural.

The reader needs **answer order**: what you need or what you found, then why, then the details. They did not live your day. They want to know quickly whether they must act, and how.

This idea has a short name in English: **BLUF**, "bottom line up front". The "bottom line" is the final result, the part that matters most. The term comes from US military writing, where orders must be understood fast. Barbara Minto, who taught writing to consultants at the firm McKinsey, describes the same idea in her book *The Pyramid Principle*: start with the answer, then support it.

## What counts as "the point"

The point depends on why you are writing.

| Why you are writing | Your first sentence gives |
|---|---|
| You need something | The request, and when you need it |
| You are answering a question | The answer: yes, no, a number, a date |
| You are giving news or an update | The result or the change |
| You are suggesting something | Your recommendation |

## Find your point in three steps

1. Before you write, finish this sentence for yourself: "After reading this, I want the reader to know or do..."
2. Make that your first sentence.
3. Add only the reasons and details the reader needs to act on it.

If you already have a draft, look at its last sentence. The point is often hiding there, because we reach it at the end of our story. Move it to the top.

## Before and after

**Answering a question.** Rania, a product manager, asks in the team chat: "Can we launch on Monday?"

> **Before:** So we fixed most of the bugs from the test session. Ahmad is still working on the export feature, and the PDF part is slow on large files. QA also found two small issues on mobile, which I fixed this morning. If we leave out the export, everything else is ready.

> **After:** Yes, we can launch Monday if we leave out the export feature. Everything else is tested and ready. The export is still slow on large files, and Ahmad needs about one more week for it.

**Asking for something.** Nadia is a freelance web developer writing to her client.

> **Before:** Hi Daniel, hope you are well. I finished the home page and the about page. For the services page I started with the layout from your sketch. I still need the text for the three services, and also the photos you mentioned last week. For now I am using placeholder text.

> **After:** Hi Daniel, could you send the text and photos for the three services by Wednesday? I need them to finish the services page. The home and about pages are done and ready for your review.

**Giving bad news.** Khaled builds a survey app for an NGO.

> **Before:** Hi Maria, this week I worked on the SMS reminders. On Tuesday the SMS provider changed how their service works, so our code stopped working. I read their new documentation and started updating our code. It is more work than I expected.

> **After:** Hi Maria, the survey app will be ready on 14 May, one week later than planned. The SMS provider changed its service, and our reminder code needs four more days of work. If 14 May is too late for the field team, I can deliver on the original date without SMS reminders.

Notice that each "after" message is the same length or shorter. Nothing important was lost. The parts were only put in a different order, and the extra story was cut.

<Callout type="tip" title="Point first is not rude">
You can still greet the reader and say thank you. With bad news, one kind sentence before the point is fine, such as "Sorry, I have some bad news about the deadline." Keep it to one sentence, then give the point.
</Callout>

The same rule works for email subject lines. "Update" tells the reader nothing. "Decision needed by Thursday: new or shared price component?" tells them what to do before they open the email.

## Common questions

**Q: What if I do not know the answer yet?**
A: Then that is your point. Say so first, and say when you will know: "I do not know yet why the payment failed. I will have an answer by 3 pm your time."

**Q: Starting with a request feels too direct for me. Is that a problem?**
A: In many cultures, people start with greetings and context, and that is not wrong. In international remote teams, busy readers usually prefer the point early. A short greeting, then the point, keeps both politeness and clarity.
