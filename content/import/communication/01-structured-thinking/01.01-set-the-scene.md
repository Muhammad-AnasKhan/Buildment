## A message that cost a day

Yousef is a frontend developer in Gaza. He works for a startup whose team lead, Megan, lives in Toronto, seven hours behind him. At the end of his day, he sends her this message:

> Hi Megan, I started on the checkout page today. First I looked at the design file and noticed the mobile layout is different from the desktop one. Then I checked the old code and found that the price component is used in three places. I tried to change it but it broke the cart page, so I reverted. I think there are two ways to do this. One is to make a new component and the other is to change the old one carefully and test all three pages. The first is faster but we will have duplicate code. What do you think?

It is still morning in Toronto. Megan reads it between two meetings, on her phone. She reads the first lines, sees a story about design files and old code, and is not sure what Yousef needs. She replies: "Thanks for the update! Is anything blocking you?"

By the time her reply arrives, it is late evening in Gaza and the power is off in Yousef's area. He answers the next morning, while Megan is asleep. She finally sees the real question when she wakes up. One quick decision cost a full working day.

## What went wrong

Yousef did good work. He found a real problem and thought of two solutions. The problem was the **order** of his message. He wrote it in the order things happened to him. His question came at the very end, and it was hidden in the word "this".

Megan needed a different order: the decision first, then the options, then the details. Here is the same message, written that way:

> Hi Megan, I need one decision from you: should I build a new price component or change the shared one? A new one is faster (done tomorrow) but duplicates code. Changing the shared one is cleaner but needs testing on three pages (two more days). I suggest the new one for now. Details below if you need them.

She could answer that in ten seconds.

## Why this matters for your career

In a remote team, your writing is often the only part of your work that people see. Your team lead, your client or a hiring manager judges your thinking by your messages, updates and short talks. When the time difference is large, every unclear message can cost a full day.

Clear structure is not about perfect English. Short, simple sentences in the right order work better than long, beautiful ones in the wrong order. Structure is a skill, and you can practice it.

## Key words

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Main point** | The one thing you most want the reader to know or do |
| **Request** | Something you ask the reader to do, decide or send |
| **Supporting points** | The reasons, facts and details that explain the main point |
| **Structure** | The order and grouping of the parts of a message |
| **Skim** | To read quickly, looking only at the first lines and headings |
