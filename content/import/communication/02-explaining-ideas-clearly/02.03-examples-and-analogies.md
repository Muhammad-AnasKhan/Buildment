## Abstract words do not stick

Rami is a mobile developer. He tells his client, a woman who runs a small clothes shop: "The new version will improve performance." She is happy. A week later she writes: "The app is still slow on my old phone. You said you fixed it."

Rami meant the product list now loads faster on a good connection. She heard "the whole app will feel fast on my phone". Both of them used the same word, **performance**, and each filled it with a different picture.

Words like *performance*, *security*, *scalable*, *optimized* and *user experience* sound clear. But they are **abstract**: the listener cannot see them, so they fill them with their own picture. Usually that picture is not yours.

## Make it concrete

A concrete example uses things the listener can picture: a number, a name, a screen, a real case. Compare:

| Abstract | Concrete |
|---|---|
| "The app is faster." | "The order page opens in 2 seconds instead of 8 on mobile data." |
| "We improved security." | "A stolen password is not enough to log in. The thief also needs your phone." |
| "The map has a rate limit." | "The map service answers 100 requests a minute. At lunch we send 300." |
| "The design is not responsive." | "On a phone, the Pay button is cut off at the right edge of the screen." |

The best examples come from the listener's own world: their app, their customers, their data. "Remember the order from Ahmad last Tuesday that showed up twice? That is the problem I fixed." Now the client does not have to imagine anything. She already saw it.

## Use a familiar comparison

Sometimes there is no example the listener has seen. Then an analogy helps. You link the new idea to something they already know well.

| Technical idea | Comparison | Where it stops |
|---|---|---|
| Cache | Keeping the books you use most on your desk | The copy on your desk can be out of date |
| Database index | The index at the back of a book | Each new page means updating the index too |
| Version control | Saving every draft of an essay, with a note | It can also combine two people's changes |
| API | A waiter who carries your order to the kitchen | It does only what the request says, no more |

Choose comparisons from the listener's life. For a shop owner, a cache is like keeping the best-selling items at the front counter instead of in the storeroom. For a teacher, a database index is like the class list that tells you which page each student's grades are on.

## When an analogy misleads

Every analogy breaks somewhere. A lock and encryption are alike in one way: both keep strangers out. They are different in another way: there is no locksmith for strong encryption. The danger is that the listener keeps following the comparison past the point where it is true.

Watch for two common problems:

- **The listener carries it too far.** Like the locksmith question, they use the comparison to guess things you never said.
- **It makes the work sound smaller than it is.** "Adding online payment is like adding a new item to the menu" sounds like one hour of work. In reality it means a payment provider, security checks and testing with real money.

The fix is simple: say where the comparison stops.

> It is like a lock, but with one big difference: there is no locksmith. If the key is lost, the files are lost. So we will keep a copy of the key in a safe place.

<Callout type="tip" title="Use the analogy to start, then return to the real thing">
An analogy opens the door. After it, give one concrete fact about the real system. If the listener is going to make a decision, check that the decision would also be right in reality, not only in the comparison.
</Callout>
