## The director's question

Dina is a mobile developer for an NGO that runs a health clinic. Last week she turned on a new login step for the clinic's patient records app. Now staff must type a six-digit code from their phone after their password. Some staff are unhappy. Dr. Salma, the clinic director, sends Dina a voice message: "Why did we add this? People are complaining. What do I tell them?"

Dina's first draft of a reply looks like this:

> We enabled 2FA with TOTP via an authenticator app, so auth now requires a second factor in addition to the password. It mitigates credential stuffing.

It is correct. But Dr. Salma would understand almost none of it, and she still would not know what to tell her staff.

## A four-step recipe

When you explain a technical idea to someone outside tech, use these four steps:

1. **Why it matters to them.** Start with the thing they care about: their customers, their money, their time, their safety.
2. **What it is, in one plain sentence.** No jargon. If you must use one technical word, explain it once.
3. **One example or comparison.** Make it concrete, as you practiced in chapter 2.3.
4. **What it means for them.** What changes, what they should do, or what happens next.

Here is Dina's new reply, one step per sentence:

> This protects patient records if a staff password is stolen. Now, logging in needs two things: the password, and a six-digit code that appears on the staff member's own phone. So if someone steals a password, for example through a fake email, they still cannot get in without that phone. For staff, it adds about ten seconds to each login. Anyone who changes their phone should tell me first, so they do not get locked out.

Dr. Salma can now answer her staff, and she knows the one thing they must do. After you explain, check that the listener understood. For example, ask: "What will you tell the staff?" In module 3 you will practice the other side of this check: summarizing back what you heard.

## Swap the jargon

Most jargon has a plain version. Keep a small list of your own swaps.

| Jargon | Plain words |
|---|---|
| Deploy | Put the new version live for users |
| Bug | A mistake in the code |
| Server | The computer that runs the app for all users |
| Database | Where the app keeps its information |
| API | A way for two programs to ask each other for data |
| Latency | The delay before something answers |
| Downtime | Time when the app is not working |

Sometimes the listener needs the technical word, because they will see it again. For example, "two-step verification" appears on the login screen. Then say it once, explain it once, and use the same word every time. Do not switch between "2FA", "two-step" and "the code thing". Three names sound like three different things.

## Keep it short

Aim for four to six sentences, or less than one minute when spoken. If the listener wants more, they will ask. It is easier to add detail than to win back someone's attention.

Two quick tests:

- **Read it aloud.** If you need a breath in the middle of a sentence, split the sentence.
- **Try it on someone outside tech.** A family member or a friend is perfect. Watch where they frown or ask "what does that mean?". That is where you lost them.

<Callout type="tip" title="Simple does not mean less true">
A simple explanation leaves out details. It must not say things that are false. If a detail changes what the listener should do, keep it in.
</Callout>
