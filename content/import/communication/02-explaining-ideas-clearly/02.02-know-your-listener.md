## One problem, three listeners

Hana is a frontend developer at a small startup. Her team built an app that helps an NGO track food parcel deliveries. This week, field workers say the app takes minutes to open.

Hana finds the cause. Each time the app opens, it downloads every delivery record since January. On a weak mobile connection, that takes a long time. Her fix: download only the last 30 days, and load older records only when someone asks for them.

Today she has to explain this three times:

- **Oliver**, her team lead in London, two hours behind her.
- **Aya**, the NGO's program manager, who plans deliveries and reports to donors.
- **Mahmoud**, a field worker who uses the app on his phone every day.

| Listener | Already knows | Needs from Hana |
|---|---|---|
| Oliver | The code, the database, how the app is built | The cause, the fix and any risk in the fix |
| Aya | How deliveries work, very little about code | When it will be fast, and whether data is lost |
| Mahmoud | Every screen of the app, not how it works | What changes for him, and what to do until then |

## Three explanations, all correct

To Oliver, Hana can use the team's own words:

> We fetch every record on load with no pagination. I'm limiting it to 30 days and lazy loading the rest. Main risk: offline mode expects the full list, so I'm testing that first.

To Aya, she leaves out the code and answers Aya's questions:

> The app is slow because it downloads every delivery since January each time it opens. From Thursday it will download only the last month, so it should open in a few seconds. No data is lost. Older deliveries load when you ask for them.

To Mahmoud, she talks about his day:

> From Thursday the app will open much faster. To see a delivery older than one month, tap "Show older". Until then, open the app once on Wi-Fi in the morning and keep it open.

None of these is "the right explanation". Each one is right for one listener. If Hana sent Oliver's version to Aya, Aya would understand nothing. If she sent Mahmoud's version to Oliver, he could not check her fix.

## Three questions before you explain

Before you write or speak, take ten seconds and answer three questions.

1. **What do they already know?** Think about the words they use and the things they have seen. Do not guess from a job title alone. A doctor knows a lot about patient privacy, but maybe nothing about databases.
2. **What do they need to do with it?** Are they going to decide, approve, act, tell other people, or just stay informed? This decides what goes first and what you can leave out.
3. **How will they receive it?** A chat message read on a phone between meetings needs fewer words than an email read at a desk. A call with a weak connection needs short, simple sentences.

## When you do not know, ask

Sometimes you cannot answer the first two questions. Then ask one short question before you explain:

> Would the short version help, or do you want the technical details too?

> Will you share this with the donors? Then I will keep it free of technical words.

One quick question can save you a long explanation that misses. You will practice asking good questions in module 3.

<Callout type="warning" title="Plain is not the same as childish">
Non-technical does not mean not smart. Aya manages hundreds of deliveries a week. Use plain words, but do not talk down to her. "Talking down" means speaking to someone as if they are less intelligent than you, for example by explaining things they already know, slowly and many times.
</Callout>

## Common questions

**Q: What if I explain to a group with mixed knowledge?**
A: Start with the plain version that everyone needs. Then add a short part for the technical people, for example "For the developers: ...", or send them the details separately.

**Q: Is it not faster to write one explanation for everyone?**
A: It is faster for you. It is usually slower for the team, because people who did not understand will ask, or will act on a wrong idea.
