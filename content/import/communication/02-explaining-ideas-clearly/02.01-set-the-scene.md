## A bug, a client and a confusing message

Basel is a backend developer. He works freelance from Gaza for Claire, who runs a small online bakery in France. On Monday morning Claire writes to him:

> We got two orders from the same customer for the same cake. Then it happened again with another customer. Did they pay twice? What is going on?

Basel finds the problem in an hour. He is relieved, and he replies quickly:

> Hi Claire, found it. The payment webhook was timing out, so the provider retried it, and since we didn't store an idempotency key the handler created duplicate orders. Added the key, should be fine now.

Every word of this is true. But Claire does not know what a webhook is, or an idempotency key. She still cannot answer the questions that matter to her. Did anyone pay twice? Which orders are copies? Can it happen again?

She replies: "Thanks, I think? Can we have a call?" Now a problem that Basel fixed in one hour costs both of them another hour.

## Why this happens

Basel is not careless. He has the **curse of knowledge**. He has worked with webhooks for years, so the words feel normal to him. He cannot easily "un-know" them and read his message the way Claire reads it.

A well-known experiment shows how strong this effect is. In 1990, a Stanford student named Elizabeth Newton asked people to tap the rhythm of a famous song, such as "Happy Birthday", on a table. A listener had to guess the song. The tappers expected listeners to guess about half of the songs. In fact, listeners guessed only 3 songs out of 120.

The tappers could hear the music in their heads. The listeners only heard knocking. When you explain your own work, you are the tapper. The idea is clear in your head, so it feels clear in your words.

## The same facts, explained for Claire

Here is a reply Basel could have sent:

> Hi Claire, it is fixed, and no one paid twice. When a customer pays, the payment company sends our shop a message. Sometimes our shop answered too slowly, so the payment company sent the message again, and our shop made a second order. Five orders this week are copies. I listed them below, so please bake each one only once. I changed the code so a repeated message can never create a new order.

It is a little longer, but Claire needs no call. Every sentence answers one of her questions, in words she already uses.

## Why this matters for your career

In remote work, most explanations happen in writing or on short calls. A client or team lead cannot lean over your desk and look at your screen. If they cannot follow you, they lose time. They may also start to feel that working with you is hard, even when your work is excellent.

Developers who explain technical things in plain words get more responsibility. They are the ones clients call back, and the ones team leads trust to talk to customers. The good news: this is a skill, and you can practice it.

## Key words

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Listener** | The person you explain to, whether they hear you or read your message |
| **Jargon** | Special words used in one job or field, which others usually do not know |
| **Abstract** | General, hard to picture, like "performance" or "security" |
| **Concrete** | Real and specific, like a name, a number or a case you can picture |
| **Example** | One real case that shows what you mean |
| **Analogy** | A comparison with something familiar, to explain something new |
