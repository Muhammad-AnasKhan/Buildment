## From practice to your own voice

In five modules you practiced with other people's work: messages that hid the main point, a developer explaining a bug to a client, a misunderstood requirement, a demo that went wrong, a story that went on too long. Those examples were safe practice, because they were not about your own work.

Now you use the same tools on something that is yours. You will prepare a **five-minute talk or project demo** about your own work or study. Then you either record it or write the full script, and a mentor gives you feedback.

Why five minutes? Because that is how long you often get in real work. A demo slot in a weekly team call. The first minutes of a call with a new client. The answer to "Tell me about a project you are proud of" in an interview. If you can be clear in five minutes, you can be clear almost anywhere.

<Callout type="info" title="What the mentor does not judge">
Your mentor does not grade your accent, your English level or how professional your video looks. They look at clarity: Is the point easy to find? Is the talk easy to follow? Would the listener understand it and know what to do next?
</Callout>

## Your toolbox

You already have every tool you need. Here is where each one came from.

| Module | Tool you can use in your talk |
|---|---|
| 1 | Start with the point, then group ideas into two to four parts |
| 2 | Know your listener, use examples and careful analogies |
| 3 | Ask clarifying questions, summarize back what you heard |
| 4 | Talk plan: point, three parts, ending. Demo plan. Handling questions |
| 5 | Story shape: situation, problem, what you did, result, lesson |

You do not need every tool. A good talk uses the few that fit your topic and your listener.

## Choosing a good topic

Rawan studies software engineering and works part time as a junior tester for a startup in Berlin. Her first idea for the capstone was "Everything about software testing". It is real, but it is far too big for five minutes. Her second idea was "My favorite keyboard shortcut". It fits in five minutes, but there is not much to say.

Her third idea worked: "How I set up automatic tests that caught three bugs before our last release". Her listener is her team lead and two new teammates. Her main point is: "Ten minutes of setup per feature saves hours of bug fixing later." It is real, it is hers, it has one clear point, and it fits.

A good capstone topic has these five things:

1. **It is yours.** You built it, did it, studied it or decided it.
2. **It has one main point.** You can say it in one sentence.
3. **It has a real listener.** You can name them: a client, a team lead, an interviewer, a new teammate or your classmates.
4. **It fits in five minutes.** It is one project, one lesson or one idea, not your whole career.
5. **You can share it.** It has no secrets or private data.

| Good fits | Too small or too big |
|---|---|
| A demo of your graduation project for a possible client | Your favorite programming language and why |
| How you fixed a slow page, for a non-technical manager | Everything about artificial intelligence |
| What you learned from your first freelance project | Your whole life story |
| A tool you built for an NGO, for its volunteers | One button color you changed |

## Talk or demo?

Both are welcome. Choose the one that fits your topic.

| A talk fits when | A demo fits when |
|---|---|
| You explain an idea, a decision or a lesson | You built something people can see working |
| There is nothing on screen to show | Seeing it work is the main point |
| You want to practice speaking without slides | You want to practice showing and talking together |

<Callout type="tip" title="No camera or fast internet? That is fine">
You can submit an audio-only recording, or the full written script with no recording at all. Both are accepted in the same way as a video. Chapter 6.3 explains each option.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Protect other people's privacy">
Your mentor will see or hear your talk. Do not show passwords, private client data, real customer names or anything you promised to keep secret. Use test data in a demo, and change names if you need to, for example "Client A".
</Callout>
