## The reply in your head

Ahmad is a developer in Gaza. His team lead, Rana, works from Berlin. In the morning call, Rana explains a change: "From next sprint, users from our partner company will sign in with their work account instead of email and password."

Ahmad built the current login page. After ten seconds he stops listening. In his head, he is planning which library to use and how to explain why his old code was fine.

He misses the next sentence: "Everyone else keeps the normal email login." That afternoon, he starts removing the email login for all users.

Ahmad did not have a hearing problem. He had a reply problem. He was talking to himself while Rana was talking to him.

## Listen to understand, not to reply

The psychologists Carl Rogers and Richard Farson wrote about **active listening** in the 1950s. Their idea is simple: the goal of listening is to understand the speaker, not to win or to answer quickly.

In practice, active listening is three habits.

**1. Give full attention.** Close other tabs and mute notifications during an important call. Look at the speaker or at your notes. Write keywords, not full sentences, so you do not fall behind.

**2. Listen to the end.** You will notice yourself planning a reply. That is normal. When it happens, write one word to remember your idea, and bring your attention back to the speaker. When they stop, wait one second before you speak. Sometimes they add the most important part.

**3. Summarize and check.** Before you reply or start work, say the main point back in your own words, and ask if you got it right.

The third habit is the one most people forget. It is also the one that saves the most time.

## Summarize, then check

A good summary is short. It keeps the main point and the details that change the work, such as a date, a number or a limit. Use this simple pattern:

> "Let me check I understood. You need ___, by ___, because ___. Did I get that right?"

Here is what Ahmad could have said:

> "So from next sprint, partner users sign in with their work account, and everyone else keeps email and password. Did I get that right?"

Rana would have answered "Yes, exactly" in two seconds. Or she would have corrected him. Both answers are good news. A correction after a twenty-second summary is much cheaper than a correction after a week of work.

Three tips:

- **Use your own words.** Repeating the exact sentence does not show that you understood it.
- **Do not add your solution yet.** First agree on the problem, then talk about fixes.
- **A summary is not agreement.** You can summarize a view you disagree with. It shows respect, and then you can give your own view.

## Listening on weak connections and in a second language

Remote calls are not perfect. These habits help:

- **Say it when audio cuts.** "Sorry, your audio cut after 'the deadline'. Could you repeat that part?" Ask about the part you missed, not the whole thing.
- **Ask about one word.** If you do not know a word or abbreviation, ask: "What does MVP mean here?" Most people are happy to explain.
- **Send a written summary after important calls.** "Summary of today's call: ... Please correct me if I missed anything." This helps teammates in other time zones too.
- **Read the whole message.** Listening includes reading. Read a long chat message to the end before you reply.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not pretend you understood">
Saying "yes, got it" when you did not understand feels polite in the moment. Later it costs you and the team much more. A short question is always more professional than a week of wrong work.
</Callout>

## Common questions

**Q: Will summarizing make calls longer?**
A: A summary takes about twenty seconds. It often makes the whole project shorter, because mistakes are caught before work starts.

**Q: What if I need the speaker to repeat something many times?**
A: Ask about the exact part you missed, and ask them to write the key facts, such as a date or a number, in the chat.

**Q: Is it rude to summarize what my manager said?**
A: No. Most managers like it, because it shows you care about getting the work right. You can start with "Let me check I understood".
