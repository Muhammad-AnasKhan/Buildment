## "Make it faster"

Asmaa is a frontend developer. She works remotely for a startup in Toronto, about seven hours behind Gaza. The product manager, Kevin, sends her a chat message at the end of his day, when it is already night in Gaza:

> "Users are complaining about the sign-up page. Can you make it faster?"

Asmaa could start right away. She could make the images smaller and the code lighter. But "faster" can mean two very different things. Maybe the page loads slowly. Or maybe the page loads fine, but sign-up has too many steps and takes users too long to finish.

Asmaa reads it the next morning, while Kevin is asleep. If she guesses wrong, she loses a day or more. She can send one message, and Kevin will read it when he wakes up.

## Closed and open questions

A **closed question** can be answered with yes, no or one word. "Is it urgent?" "Yes." You learn very little. Closed questions are useful at the end, to confirm a fact: "So the deadline is Friday?"

An **open question** needs a fuller answer. Open questions usually start with **what**, **how**, **which**, **who** or **when**. They invite the other person to tell you what they know.

| Closed | Open |
|---|---|
| "Is the design final?" | "Which parts of the design might still change?" |
| "Do you want tests?" | "What should the tests cover first?" |
| "Is it urgent?" | "When do you need it, and what depends on it?" |
| "Did you understand the task?" | "How are you planning to start the task?" |

Be careful with **why**. "Why did you choose this library?" can sound like blame, especially to a manager or a client. "What led to this choice?" asks the same thing more gently.

## Specific is better than general

"Any more details?" and "Can you explain more?" are open questions, but they are too general. The other person has to guess what you need. Often they repeat what they already said.

A specific question names the exact part you are unsure about. When you can, offer the options you see, as Asmaa did: "slow loading, or too many steps?" This makes the question quick to answer, even for a busy person.

## Ask early, and ask well across time zones

The cheapest moment to ask a question is before you start. After you have built something, every answer that changes the plan also throws away work.

Across time zones, one round of question and answer can take a full day. So plan your questions:

- **Send them together.** Number them in one message, not five messages over the day.
- **Put the most important question first.** The reader may only answer the first one.
- **Say what you will do until you hear back.** Then your work does not stop while they sleep.

Here is Asmaa's message:

> Hi Kevin, two questions before I start on the sign-up page:
> 1. What are users complaining about: slow loading, or too many steps?
> 2. Do most complaints come from phones or from computers?
>
> Until I hear from you, I will measure how fast the page loads on a mid-range phone, because that is useful either way.

## How many questions?

You do not need to ask everything. Ask the questions whose answers would change what you do. A simple test: **"If the answer were different, would I do the work differently?"** If not, the question can wait.

For a new task, the questions that most often change the work are about four things:

| Area | Example question |
|---|---|
| Who | "Who will use this, and on which devices?" |
| Done | "What does finished look like for you?" |
| When | "When do you need it, and what depends on it?" |
| Limits | "Is there anything I must not change, or a budget limit?" |

## Practice: write your questions

A team lead at an NGO sends you this message: "Can you write a short guide for new volunteers on how to use our app? We need it soon."

```openquestion
prompt: "Write the reply you would send. Include two or three open, specific clarifying questions, numbered, with the most important first. Then add one sentence saying what you will do until you hear back."
required: true
minWords: 40

modelAnswer: "Hi Samira, happy to write the guide. Three quick questions first: 1. Which tasks must new volunteers be able to do in the app in their first week? 2. Will they read the guide on their phones, or print it? 3. What date do you need it by, and is there an event it is for? Until I hear back, I will start an outline for signing in and logging hours, since every volunteer needs those."
```
