## What a demo is for

A demo is not a tour of every screen. The listener wants to answer three questions:

1. **What problem does this solve, and for whom?**
2. **Does it work?** They want to see it, not hear about it.
3. **Can I trust the person who built it?** One clear decision you made, and why, answers this better than a list of tools.

So a good demo has three parts: **the problem, the result and one key decision.** It follows the same shape as the talk in chapter 4.2. The point comes first, and the result is shown, not described.

## The three parts

| Part | What you do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **The problem** | Say who has the problem, in one or two sentences | "Patients call the clinic to book. Lines are busy." |
| **The result** | Show the happy path working, from start to end | Book a real test appointment, step by step |
| **One key decision** | Explain one choice and its trade-off | "I used SMS reminders, not app alerts. Not all patients use apps." |

Notice that the key decision is about the user, not about your favorite tool. A non-technical client cares that patients get reminders. A technical interviewer may also want to hear the trade-off: what you gave up, and why it was worth it.

## Prepare the demo, not only the app

Most demo problems come from small things nobody checked. Before the call:

- **Choose one path** and write the clicks down in order: open, search, pick a time, confirm.
- **Prepare test data.** Use a test user with a realistic name and real-looking data, so you are not typing during the demo. Never show real customer data.
- **Clean your screen.** Close other tabs and chats, and turn off notifications. Make the text larger so people on phones can read it.
- **Practice the whole demo once, out loud, with a timer.** This is when you find the broken button.

## When the internet or the power fails

For many of us, a demo can be cut by a power cut, a weak signal or a slow server, often at the worst moment. You cannot control that. You can control how ready you are.

### Build your backups the day before

| Backup | How to prepare it | When it saves you |
|---|---|---|
| **A recorded demo** | Record your screen doing the happy path. Keep the file on your device | The app or the connection fails during the call |
| **Screenshots** | Take one image per step, in order, in one folder or one slide file | Your connection is too weak to share video |
| **A local copy** | Run the app on your own computer with test data, not only online | The online server or the internet is down |
| **A shared link, sent early** | Send the recording or screenshots to the client before the call | You lose power and cannot rejoin at all |
| **Power and data** | Charge your laptop, phone and power bank. Have mobile data as a second connection | The power or home internet goes out |

Two small habits help a lot. First, turn off your camera during the screen share if the connection is weak: video uses a lot of data. Second, save the recording on your device, not only in the cloud. If the internet is down, a cloud link will not open.

### What to say when it breaks

Stay calm and keep it short. People remember how you handle a problem more than the problem itself. Prepare a sentence like this:

> "My connection just dropped, so I will switch to the recording I made yesterday. It shows the same booking flow. I can run it live again at the end if we have time."

If you lose power completely, send a short message as soon as you can, with the backup link:

> "Sorry Marta, the power went out here. The demo recording is in the link I sent this morning, at minute 1:20. I can join again in about 30 minutes, or we can find a new time."

<Callout type="tip" title="Tell the client early">
If the electricity schedule in your area makes a cut likely during the call, tell the client at the start: "If I drop, I will message you right away, and the recording is in your inbox." This turns a surprise into a plan.
</Callout>
