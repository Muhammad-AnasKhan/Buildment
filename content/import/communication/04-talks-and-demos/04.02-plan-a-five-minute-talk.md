## Five minutes is not much

Many people speak about 120 to 150 words in a minute. So a five-minute talk is only about 600 to 750 words. That is shorter than this chapter.

This is why most short talks fail in the same way: the speaker tries to say everything. They give the history of the project, every feature, every problem and every tool. The listener gets lost, and the main point is hidden in the middle.

A good short talk does the opposite. It says **one** main thing, supports it with a few parts, and stops on time. Everything else waits for the Q&A or a follow-up message.

## A simple structure

You already know "start with the point" from module 1. A short talk uses the same idea, with a clear ending added.

| Part | What it does | Time |
|---|---|---|
| **The point** | One or two sentences: the main message or result | About 30 seconds |
| **Part 1** | The first supporting part: often the problem | About 1 minute |
| **Part 2** | The second part: what you did or found | About 1 to 1.5 minutes |
| **Part 3** | The third part: the result, proof or what it means | About 1 minute |
| **The ending** | Repeat the point, then one clear next step or request | About 30 seconds |

This adds up to about four and a half minutes. Keep the extra time as a buffer: speakers almost always run longer than they planned.

Why three parts? Three is easy for a listener to hold in mind. Two can work for very short talks. Five or more usually means you are listing, not explaining.

## Lama's outline

Here is Lama's outline. Notice that it is short lines, not full paragraphs.

```text
POINT: Pages now load in half the time on slow mobile data.

1. Problem: users in areas with weak internet waited 9 seconds
   for the home page. Some gave up.
2. What I did: smaller images, load the list 20 items at a time,
   cache the menu so it works offline.
3. Result: 4 seconds on a slow connection test. Support messages
   about "app not opening" dropped this month.

ENDING: Half the load time, with three small changes.
Request: please test it on your own phone this week and
tell me if any page is still slow.
```

She writes the opening and ending sentences in full, because those are the moments when nerves are strongest. The middle is short notes, so she talks to the team instead of reading.

<Callout type="tip" title="End with something the listener can do">
"Thank you, that's all" wastes your last 30 seconds. Repeat the point, then give a next step: a request, a decision you need, or what happens next.
</Callout>

## Practice out loud, with a timer

An outline on paper is not a talk yet. You only learn how long it takes by saying it.

- **Say it out loud once** and time it with your phone. Most first tries are too long.
- **Cut, do not speed up.** If you are at seven minutes, remove a part or an example. Speaking faster makes you harder to follow, especially for listeners who speak English as a second language.
- **Say it a second time.** It will already feel easier. If you can, record yourself on your phone and listen once.

<Callout type="warning" title="The common mistake">
Many speakers add a long introduction about themselves and the background. In a five-minute talk, keep the introduction to one sentence, or leave it out if the listeners already know you.
</Callout>

## Common questions

**Q: Should I write a full script?**
A: For a first talk in a second language, writing the opening and ending in full helps a lot. For the middle, short notes are better, so you do not read word by word.

**Q: Do I need slides?**
A: No. Many short talks work well with no slides, or with a few screenshots. If you use slides, keep few words on each one, so people listen to you instead of reading.
