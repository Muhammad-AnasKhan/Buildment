## Why questions feel hard

Many people feel calm during their prepared talk and nervous during the questions. The talk is planned. The questions are not. In a second language, you also worry that you will not understand the question, or will not find the words fast enough.

Here is a helpful way to see it: a question means the listener cared enough to think about your work. Most questions are not attacks. They are people trying to understand, or checking if they can trust the work.

You do not need to be perfect. You need a simple routine that you can use every time.

## A routine for every question

| Step | What you do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Listen to the end** | Let the person finish. Do not plan your reply while they talk | You answer the real question, not the one you guessed |
| **2. Pause and check** | Take a breath. If unsure, repeat the question in your own words | It gives you time, and catches a misunderstanding early |
| **3. Answer briefly** | Point first, one reason or example, then stop | Short answers leave time for other people's questions |
| **4. Check they understood** | Ask: "Does that answer your question?" | They can ask for more if they need it |

Step 1 and step 2 come straight from module 3. Active listening and summarizing back are even more useful under pressure, because nerves make us guess.

<Callout type="tip" title="It is fine to ask them to repeat">
On a bad connection or with a strong accent, you can say: "Sorry, the audio cut out. Could you say that again, or type it in the chat?" Professionals ask this all the time. Guessing is worse.
</Callout>

## When you do not know

Ahmed is demoing a ticket booking app to a bus company. The company's IT manager asks: "Can this handle 10,000 users at the same time?" Ahmed has only tested it with about 50 users.

A good "I do not know" answer has three parts:

1. **What you do know:** "I have tested it with about 50 users at once, and it was fast."
2. **What you will do:** "I will run a bigger load test." (A load test checks how a system works with many users at the same time.)
3. **When:** "I will send you the results by Thursday."

Then write the promise down right after the call, and keep it. The follow-up message is part of the answer.

## Other common situations

| Situation | What you can say |
|---|---|
| A long question with several parts | "There are two questions there. First, the cost. Second, the timeline." |
| A question you will cover later | "Good question. I show that in the next two minutes. If it is not clear, ask me again." |
| A question far from the topic | "That is a bigger topic. Can we talk about it after the call?" |
| A critical question | "That is a fair concern. Here is what I did about it, and what is still open." |

With a critical question, do not defend yourself with emotion and do not argue. Thank them, answer the real concern with facts, and be honest about what is not finished. Listeners often trust a speaker more after a calm answer to a hard question.

## Common questions

**Q: What if nobody asks a question?**
A: That is normal on many calls. Wait a few seconds, then say: "If questions come up later, send me a message." You can also prepare one question yourself: "People often ask me about X. Here is the short answer."

**Q: Can I prepare for the questions?**
A: Yes. Before the talk, write down the three questions you are most afraid of, and a short answer for each. You will often be asked at least one of them.
