This is the gate for module 28, the last module before the capstone. It asks for something different from modules 20 to 27. There you wrote a design on paper at your own pace. Here you run a round against a clock and then report how it went. Tick each box only when it is true, answer the learning log in your own words, and then submit the mock round at the end. A mentor reads it, so write it the way you would explain a round to a teammate who was not in the room.

## Module 28 checklist

- [ ] I can name the six boxes of a 45-minute round, give each one a time, and say what I will have said or drawn by the end of it
- [ ] I can ask four short questions that fix the scope, and I know to state an assumption when the interviewer says "you decide"
- [ ] I can turn a monthly or daily number into a per-second average and a peak, saying each step of the sum with its unit
- [ ] I can name the number that drives a design, use it to pick one deep dive instead of five shallow ones, and draw the main path first with a one-line job for every box
- [ ] I can check the clock at minutes 10, 20 and 35, and I know what to cut, out loud, if I am behind
- [ ] I can answer "what if traffic grows 10 times?" in four moves: restate, compute, find the first break, fix and price
- [ ] I can find the part that breaks first by dividing each part's load by its plan, and say why a database is harder to fix than app servers
- [ ] I can answer pushback with a number and agree when the number says the interviewer is right; when I find a flaw in my own design, I name it, then show it is in scope or change the design
- [ ] I can say a trade-off as a choice, what it buys, a cost with a number or a name, and a switch point
- [ ] I can name the six common mistakes and the fix for each
- [ ] I ran at least one timed prompt from chapter 28.3 and scored myself on the five rows from chapter 28.1
- [ ] I submitted the mock round below

## Learning log

1. **Your clock:** In your timed round, which box ran over, and what did you cut? How will you notice it sooner next time?
2. **Your driver:** For the prompt you chose, which number drove the design, and which deep dive did it point to?
3. **Your probe:** Write the four moves for "what if traffic grows 10 times?" on your prompt, with the numbers.
4. **Your cost line:** Write one trade-off as choice, cost, number and switch. Where does the number come from?
5. **Your weak spot:** Which of the six common mistakes do you make most often, and what is your fix?
6. **Your quiz:** Which question in the chapter 28.6 quiz did you miss first, and what did it teach you?

## Your gate answer: one mock round

Pick one prompt from the practice bank in chapter 28.3. Choose the case study you found hardest, because that is where a timed round teaches the most. Then change one assumption so that the numbers are yours: double the users, halve the budget, or change who uses the service. This step matters. If you copy a model answer, you are practising memory. If you change an assumption, you have to redo the sums, and that is what an interviewer is testing.

Set a timer for 45 minutes, or 20 minutes for the short round, and use the boxes from chapter 28.2. Talk through the whole loop out loud: scope, numbers, sketch, one deep dive, one trade-off, one failure, and a wrap-up. Look at the clock at the three checks. Do not open the model answer until the timer ends. When it does, score yourself on the five rows from chapter 28.1, and then compare with the model answer.

Now write up the round in about 300 words, or record yourself saying it in 5 to 6 minutes and paste the link. Either is fine, and a written account should stay under 350 words. Cover these parts in order. Say the scope and the number that drives the design. Give the sketch in one line per box. Name the deep dive and say why the numbers chose it. State one trade-off as choice, cost with a number, and switch point. Name one failure and what the user sees. Then answer the follow-up "what if traffic grows 10 times?" in four moves. End with one line on what you cut or where you ran over.

Scoring yourself works best with the five rows from chapter 28.1 and a pencil. For each row, write one line of evidence from your own round: the number you said, the choice you compared, the failure you named, the sum you showed, the sentence that was clear. If you cannot find a line of evidence for a row, that row is where your next practice round should aim. Be honest, because the point of the score is to choose what to practise next, not to impress anyone.

A weak account lists boxes and says "cache for speed". A strong account starts from numbers and lets them decide which boxes to draw. If you run short of words, cut the sketch, not the numbers or the trade-off. A recording should show only your work, not your surroundings. Say the numbers aloud and show the page or board where you wrote them. You do not need to be fluent. Many of our fellows speak English as a second language, and the mentor reads for your reasoning, not for your accent.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is copying the model answer's numbers instead of redoing the sums with your changed assumption. The second is a deep dive that is really a list of five topics. The third is a cost line with no number or name, such as "some extra complexity". A mentor reads for method, not for one right design. Two fellows can choose differently and both pass, as long as each starts from the numbers, ties the dive to the driving number, names a cost they can check, and says when they would change their mind.

A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as "what if the cache restarts empty?" or "why not one database?". Answer by editing and resubmitting. This is the same pressure as a real follow-up, with more time to think. Count that time in your plan.
