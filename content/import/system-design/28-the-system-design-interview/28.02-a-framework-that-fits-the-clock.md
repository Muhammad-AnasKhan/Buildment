Forty-five minutes is short. In module 1 you learned the design loop: clarify, estimate, sketch, deep dive, trade-offs, failure. An interview is the same loop with a clock. This chapter gives each step a time box and a job, so you know where the minutes go before the round starts.

## Six boxes fill 45 minutes

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m28-interview-stepper" />

Step through the round. Each box has a job and a time: clarify 5 minutes, estimate 5, sketch 10, deep dive 15, trade-offs and failure 7, wrap-up 3. At the sketch step, press the button to draw 8 minutes longer. The deep dive falls to 7 minutes. Time spent drawing comes out of the part that shows depth.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what each box must produce">

Each box ends when you have said or drawn one thing. If you can name that thing, you know when to move on.

| Box | Minutes | By the end you have said or drawn | Where you learned it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clarify | 0 to 5 | What it does, what it does not do, and two quality targets (fast, reliable) | Chapter 1.2 |
| Estimate | 5 to 10 | Creates a second, reads a second at the peak, storage over the years, and the number that drives the design | Chapter 2.3 |
| Sketch | 10 to 20 | The path of each main action from client to storage and back, with one line on what each box does | Chapters 1.3 and 1.4 |
| Deep dive | 20 to 35 | One hard part: the options, your pick, and why the numbers chose it | Modules 20 to 27 |
| Trade-offs and failure | 35 to 42 | One choice with its cost and switch point, and one failing part with what the user sees | Chapters 1.4, 16 and 19 |
| Wrap-up | 42 to 45 | Three sentences: what you built, the number that drove it, what you would do with more time | This chapter |

These minutes are a course plan, not a rule from any company. For a one-hour round, stretch every box by a third. For a short round of 20 minutes, use 2, 3, 5, 6, 3 and 1. What stays the same is the order and the job of each box.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the deep dive gets the most time">

Sketches look alike. A link shortener, a news feed and a chat app all have clients, stateless servers, a cache and a store. Two candidates can draw the same sketch and still show very different skill. The difference appears in the deep dive, where you compare options with numbers, and in the trade-off step, where you show judgment about cost.

That is why the plan protects 15 minutes for the dive and 7 for trade-offs and failure, and holds the sketch to 10. Sketching feels productive because the board fills up. But a finished sketch earns little more than a half-finished one, while a half-finished deep dive earns little at all.

There is a second reason. The deep dive is the only box where the interviewer can push you with follow-ups (chapter 28.3). If you have not left yourself time for it, you cannot show how you handle pressure.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say** (in the first minute): "I will spend about 5 minutes on scope, 5 on numbers, then sketch the main paths. Then I would like to go deep on one part, and I will keep 10 minutes at the end for trade-offs and failure. Tell me if you would like a different split."

**Expect:** "Fine. How long do you need for the sketch?" **Answer:** "About 10 minutes. If I run over, I will say so and cut detail, not the deep dive."

Saying the plan out loud does two jobs. It shows structure, and it lets the interviewer steer you before you spend minutes on the wrong thing.

</GoDeeper>

## Ask four short questions first

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m28-clarify-aloud" />

Ask before you draw. Who uses it? How many? What is out of scope? How fast and how reliable? Four short questions fix the scope, and the answers go on the board where the interviewer can see them. If the interviewer says "you decide", pick an assumption, say it out loud, and move on.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: good questions and weak ones">

A good question is one whose answer changes the design. "How many users, and how often do they read compared with write?" changes the whole design. "Should there be a dark mode?" changes nothing you will draw. Chapter 1.2 gave you three kinds of requirement: what the system does, how well it does it, and what limits you. Aim one or two questions at each.

Some interviewers will say "you decide". That is an invitation, not a refusal. State a number as an assumption: "I will assume 500 million new links a month. Tell me if that is far off." You keep moving, and you show that you know the number is a guess (chapter 2.3: say your assumptions out loud).

Watch the clock. If clarifying passes 7 minutes, you are interviewing the interviewer. Write the scope as two short lists on the board, "does" and "does not (for now)", and stop.

</GoDeeper>

## Say each sum out loud, with units

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m28-estimate-aloud" />

Say each step of the sum with its unit, and write the result on the board. 500 million links a month is about 193 a second. Times 100 opens is about 19,300 a second, and times 5 for the busiest moment is about 96,500. Then say which number drives the design: here, opens.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: round on purpose and keep the unit">

The interviewer wants to see your method, not a calculator's answer (chapter 2.3). Round early so the arithmetic is quick, and keep a unit next to every number so a mistake shows up.

| Fact | Use this | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Seconds in a day | 86,400, or about 100,000 | 10 million a day is about 100 a second |
| Seconds in a month | 2.6 million | 30 x 86,400 = 2,592,000 |
| Seconds in a year | 31.5 million, or about 30 million | 365 x 86,400 = 31,536,000 |
| Busiest second | 3 to 5 times the average | State the factor you chose |
| Bytes and bits | 8 bits in a byte | Network speed is in bits, storage in bytes |

The most common slip is mixing megabytes and megabits. Say the unit every time. A second common slip is stopping at the average. The design must survive the peak, so always give both.

If you are unsure of a number, call it an assumption and move on. Never state a guess as a fact.

</GoDeeper>

## Every box answers a number

<MermaidDiagram title="From a number to a box" chart={`flowchart LR
  N1["100,000 opens a second"] --> B1["A cache and many app servers"]
  N2["9 TB over five years"] --> B2["Data split over 5 partitions"]
  N3["Every click counted"] --> B3["A queue, after the reply"]
  N4["Links never lost"] --> B4["Copies of the data"]
`} legend="Each box on the board answers a number you said out loud. These are the Wasla numbers and boxes from module 20. A box with no number behind it is a guess." />

Draw only boxes that a number asks for. 100,000 opens a second asks for a cache and many app servers. 9 TB asks for partitions. A box with no number behind it is a guess, and the interviewer will ask why it is there. Draw the main path first and say in one line what each box does.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: sketch the path, not the zoo">

Start with one request. Draw a client, then follow the request to storage and back, naming what each box does and what it sends. Do the same for the second main action (for Wasla, creating a link, and opening one). Only then add boxes that a number asked for.

Avoid the zoo: a load balancer, an API gateway, a service mesh, a CDN and a queue drawn on every board because they are on every board. Each extra box needs a number and costs time. If you cannot say why a box is there, take it out.

Say which boxes keep no data (stateless, chapter 4.3), because you scale them by adding copies. Say which keep data, because you scale them by splitting (chapter 9.2) or copying (chapter 8.1). That one habit answers many follow-ups before they are asked.

</GoDeeper>

## Let the biggest number pick the dive

<MermaidDiagram title="From the driving number to the deep dive" chart={`flowchart LR
  R["The number that drives it"]
  R -->|"opens a second"| A["Read path and fan-out (20, 21)"]
  R -->|"open connections"| B["Gateways and message order (22)"]
  R -->|"searches and suggestions"| C["Sharded index and trie (23)"]
  R -->|"location updates"| D["In-memory map by cell (24)"]
  R -->|"contention on seats"| E["Holds and the ledger (25)"]
  R -->|"one request a second per site"| F["The URL frontier (26)"]
  R -->|"bits a second out"| G["CDN and segments (27)"]
`} legend="Each case study in modules 20 to 27 had a number that drove its design. That number points to the deep dive. The numbers in brackets are the module." />

Your estimate already tells you where to dive. Wasla: reads, so the read path. The chat app: open connections, so the gateways. The ticket drop: contention, so seat holds. Pick one dive, say why this number chose it, and spend 15 minutes there. Five shallow dives show less than one deep one.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: when two numbers compete">

Sometimes two numbers both look big. The ticket drop in module 25 had 80,000 requests a second at the peak, which is contention, and only about 170 payments a second, which is small but must be exact. Which one do you dive into?

Choose the one where a wrong answer breaks a rule you promised to keep. In the ticket drop, the rules were "never sell a seat twice" and "never lose money", so both deserve a dive. Then say so, and offer the interviewer a choice with a recommendation: "I think seat holds are the hard part. Shall I go there, or would you rather see the money side?"

If the interviewer redirects you, follow them. A redirect is a gift: it tells you exactly where the score is. Do not insist on the dive you planned.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say:** "The number that drives this design is opens a second, about 100,000 at peak. So I would like to go deep on the read path: the cache, the status code and what happens when the cache is empty. Does that match what you want to see?"

**Expect:** "Actually, I would like to hear how you make the codes." **Answer:** "Sure." Then switch at once, and use the same shape: the options, the pick, the number that chose it.

</GoDeeper>

## End with one cost and one failure

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m28-close-the-round" />

Name one choice, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would switch. Then say what breaks and what the user sees. "I chose a 302. It buys every click for counting. It costs one more hop. I would switch if we stopped counting." Then: "If the cache restarts empty, the database sees 9 times its plan, so I spread the cache."

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a cost line needs a number or a name">

A weak cost line says "some complexity". A strong one says "an extra database read of about 2 ms on a miss, and a cache to keep warm". The test is simple: could the interviewer check your line? If it holds a number or a name, they can.

Use the same shape every time, from the course's authoring standards: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, the alternative, and when to switch. Four of those six are enough in a round: the choice, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would switch.

For failure, pick the failure that hurts most, not the most exotic one. Say what the user sees, then what the system does. Chapter 16 covers resilience patterns and chapter 19 covers disaster recovery, and each case study in modules 20 to 27 ended with one failure you can reuse.

</GoDeeper>

## If you fall behind, say so

<MermaidDiagram title="Check the clock three times" chart={`flowchart TD
  A{"Minute 10: scope and numbers on the board?"}
  A -->|"yes"| B{"Minute 20: one path drawn end to end?"}
  A -->|"no"| A2["Say it: I am behind. I will assume the rest."]
  A2 --> B
  B -->|"yes"| C{"Minute 35: one deep dive done?"}
  B -->|"no"| B2["Draw the main path only. List the rest as later."]
  B2 --> C
  C -->|"yes"| D["Minutes 35 to 42: one cost, one failure"]
  C -->|"no"| C2["Stop the dive. Give the answer in two sentences."]
  C2 --> D
`} legend="Look at the clock at minutes 10, 20 and 35. If a check fails, say so out loud and cut something small. Then carry on." />

Check the clock at minutes 10, 20 and 35. If you are behind, say so and cut: assume the rest of the scope, draw the main path only, or end the dive in two sentences. Interviewers forgive a cut that you announce. A silent run-over is much harder to forgive.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: three ways to recover">

**Behind at minute 10.** Say: "I am taking longer on scope than planned. I will assume the rest and check it against the numbers." Then write the assumptions as a short list and move to the estimate.

**Behind at minute 20.** Draw the main path only. Say: "I will leave the second path for later and come back if there is time." Then go to the deep dive. The interviewer may pick up the second path as a follow-up, which is fine.

**Behind at minute 35.** End the dive with the answer first: "The pick is counter blocks in base62, because the codes are public and a counter never clashes." Then give the reason in one more sentence. Move on.

If you go blank, think out loud. "I am not sure which part to dive into. The biggest number is opens, so I will start there." Saying your reasoning is better than silence, because it lets the interviewer help.

</GoDeeper>
