```quiz
type: mcq
question: "It is minute 12 of a 45-minute round. You are still on scope, and the interviewer has not heard one number. What is the strongest thing to do?"
options:
  - "Say you are behind, state your assumptions in one line each, and start the estimate"
  - "Keep asking questions until the scope is completely certain, before you give any numbers"
  - "Start drawing the boxes now, and give the numbers at the end if there is some time left"
  - "Ask the interviewer to give you every number you need, so that you can save some time"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. Naming the delay and fixing the scope with stated assumptions costs one minute. It protects the deep dive, and the assumptions can still be corrected."
  - "Scope is never completely certain. Each extra question at minute 12 takes minutes from the deep dive, which is where the score is."
  - "Boxes drawn before the numbers are guesses. The numbers decide which boxes are needed, and an answer at the end comes too late to change them."
  - "Interviewers expect you to assume a number and say it. Handing the job back shows no estimating skill. Say it as an assumption and ask them to correct you."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "The interviewer says: 4 million daily users, each sending 30 messages a day. Which spoken estimate is strongest?"
options:
  - "About 12 million messages a day, so about 140 a second, and about 420 at the peak of 3 times"
  - "About 120 million a day, which is a lot, so I would plan for a huge cluster of servers"
  - "About 120 million a day, so about 1,400 a second, and 4,200 at a peak of 3 times"
  - "About 1,400 a second at the peak, from 120 million divided by 86,400 seconds in a day"
correct: 2
explanations:
  - "4 million x 30 is 120 million, not 12 million. Every later number is 10 times too low, so the design would be too small."
  - "The 120 million is right, but the estimate stops there. 'A huge cluster' gives the interviewer nothing to check, and no per-second number."
  - "Right. 4 million x 30 is 120 million a day. Divided by about 86,400 seconds that is about 1,400 a second on average, and about 4,200 at a peak 3 times higher."
  - "120 million divided by 86,400 is the average, not the peak. Calling it the peak leaves the design too small for the busiest second."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "At peak, a design's parts run at these shares of their plan: app servers 50%, cache 60%, database 80%. Traffic grows 1.5 times and nothing is added. Which part passes its plan first?"
options:
  - "The cache, at 90% of its plan"
  - "The database, at 120% of its plan"
  - "The app servers, at 75% of their plan"
  - "All three at once, since traffic grew by 50%"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "The cache reaches 90% (60% x 1.5). That is high, but it is still inside its plan. Another part passes first."
  - "Right. 80% x 1.5 is 120%, the only part over its plan. This is the first-break method: scale each load, then compare it with the plan."
  - "The app servers reach 75% (50% x 1.5). They are well inside their plan, and they are also the easiest part to scale by adding copies."
  - "Every load grows 1.5 times, but they start at different shares of their plan. Only the one that started highest crosses 100% first."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "In a news feed design with fan-out on write, the interviewer asks what happens when an account with 20 million followers posts. You had not covered it. Which answer is strongest?"
options:
  - "My queue absorbs any burst of writes, so the design already handles it without changes"
  - "That account is rare, so I would leave it out of the plan and not worry about it"
  - "I did not plan for that, so I would have to throw the design away and start over"
  - "That is 20 million writes, so I would pull such accounts and merge at read time"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "A queue holds a burst but does not shorten it. 20 million writes would still keep the fan-out workers busy for minutes, and the feed would be late."
  - "A rare account can be the one that breaks the design. Leaving it out of the plan ignores the case the interviewer just pointed at."
  - "Starting over is not needed. The gap is small and has a known fix, so naming the flaw and changing one part of the design is the stronger move."
  - "Right. It names the cost with a number and gives a fix: pull the few huge accounts and merge their posts when a feed opens. The rest of the design stays."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "You must close your trade-off for choosing a 302 redirect. Which closing is strongest?"
options:
  - "I chose a 302 because it is the standard choice, and most teams pick it for good reasons that I trust."
  - "I chose a 302 to count each click. It costs one more hop. I would switch to a 301 if we stopped counting."
  - "I chose a 302, which costs one more request on every click, and that cost is small enough that we can ignore it."
  - "I chose a 302 and a cache, and I am sure that both will keep our pages fast under any load we ever meet."
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "This is a claim with no reason from the numbers. 'Standard' tells the interviewer you copied it, and it names no cost and no switch point."
  - "Right. It gives the reason (counting each click), the cost (one more hop) and the switch point (stop counting). Those three parts let the interviewer check the choice."
  - "It names a cost but then waves it away with no number. 'Small enough to ignore' cannot be checked, and it gives no condition for changing the choice."
  - "This promises speed under any load, which no design can do. It names no cost and no switch point, and 'sure' is not evidence."
```
