After your design, the interviewer pushes. "What if traffic grows 10 times?" "What breaks first?" "Why not one big database?" These follow-ups are not traps. They test whether your numbers are real. A good answer has four moves: restate the question, compute, find the first break, then fix it and price the fix.

## A follow-up retests your numbers

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m28-redo-the-maths" />

"What if traffic grows 10 times?" Do not answer with a feeling. Multiply the numbers on the board by 10. Divide each part's load by what it can take, and see which one fails. Then fix it and say the price. Four moves: restate, compute, find the first break, fix and price.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the four moves">

**1. Restate.** "Ten times is 1 million opens a second and 10,000 creates a second." This confirms you heard the question and gives you a moment to think.

**2. Compute.** Scale each number on the board, then divide each part's load by its plan. The plan is the load you chose for that part, usually about half its limit (chapter 2.5). A part at 100% of plan is fine. A part at 880% is not.

**3. Find the first break.** Two things matter: how far over plan a part is, and how hard it is to fix. Stateless app servers (chapter 4.3) are easy: add copies. A database is harder, because new nodes need data moved to them (chapter 9.5). So the database is usually the answer to "what breaks first", even when the app servers are also over plan.

**4. Fix and price.** Give one fix and its cost with a number. Do not list five fixes. The interviewer will ask for the next one if they want it.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say:** "Ten times is 1 million opens a second. The app servers are stateless, so that is 200 servers instead of 20. The database is the hard part: at a 90% hit rate it sees 110,000 operations a second, 8.8 times its plan on 5 partitions. I would raise the hit rate to 99%, which cuts that to 20,000, and go to 8 partitions. The cost is a bigger cache and 3 more database nodes."

**Expect:** "Which of those would you do first?" **Answer:** "The cache. It is the cheapest to add, and it takes the most load off the database."

</GoDeeper>

## Step through six follow-ups

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m28-probe-stepper" />

Step through six probes on Wasla's design from module 20: today, 10 times the traffic, the fix, an empty cache, a hot link and the cost line. Each step shows the numbers behind the answer. In the fix step, try 5, 8 and 10 partitions and watch each one's share of its plan change.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the six answers, with the sums">

All numbers are Wasla's from module 20 and are course assumptions: 100,000 opens and 1,000 creates a second at peak, a 90% hit rate, an app server planned at 5,000 opens a second, a database node planned at 2,500 operations a second.

| Probe | The sums | The answer in one breath |
|---|---|---|
| Today | Misses: 100,000 x 10% = 10,000. Plus 1,000 creates = 11,000. On 5 partitions, 2,200 each = 88% of plan | "Every part is inside its plan." |
| 10 times traffic | 1,000,000 opens, 10,000 creates. Apps: 1,000,000 / 5,000 = 200. Database: 100,000 + 10,000 = 110,000, so 22,000 on each of 5 = 8.8 times plan | "Apps are easy to add. The database is 8.8 times over." |
| The fix | Hit rate 99%: 10,000 + 10,000 = 20,000. On 5 partitions, 4,000 each = 160% of plan. On 8, 2,500 = 100%. On 10, 2,000 = 80% | "A bigger cache and 8 partitions. That is 3 more nodes and data to move." |
| An empty cache | All opens miss: 1,000,000 + 10,000 = 1,010,000. On 8 partitions, 126,250 each = 50.5 times plan. With the cache over 10 nodes, one restart empties a tenth: 9,000 + 100,000 + 10,000 = 119,000, so about 6 times plan | "Spread the cache so one restart loses a slice. Merge duplicate misses and limit unknown codes (chapter 20.4)." |
| A hot link | One link is 1 open in 10: 100,000 reads a second on one cache node. A copy kept 5 seconds in each of 200 app servers: 200 / 5 = 40 reads a second | "Keep the hottest codes in app memory for a few seconds (chapter 5.6)." |
| The cost line | Fix 1: a bigger cache. Fix 2: 3 more database nodes. Fix 3: a short-lived copy in each app server | "It costs cache memory, 3 nodes and some data movement. I would stop at 99% if memory gets too expensive." |

The 5-second copy is an example value. A few seconds is the idea from chapter 20.4. Like every simulation, this one ignores things: cache nodes have limits of their own, and real traffic is not smooth. Use it to reason about shape.

</GoDeeper>

## Bring a number to a disagreement

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m28-pushback-evidence" />

The interviewer asks: why not one database? Do the sum. 101,000 operations a second against a node planned for 2,500 is 40 times over. Then ask what number would change their mind. A view you can check beats a view you only repeat. If the numbers favour the interviewer, agree and say so.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: three kinds of pushback">

**A challenge to a choice.** "Why not X?" Give the number that separates X from your choice, and say when X would win. "One database is simpler, and it is right up to about 2,500 operations a second. At Wasla's peak it is 40 times over."

**A hint.** "Have you thought about Y?" Treat this as a gift. Thank them, say how Y changes the design, and fold it in. An interviewer who gives a hint is trying to help you reach a good answer.

**A change in the rules.** "Now there is no budget for more servers." Redo the sum with the new rule and say which choice changes. This tests whether your design is a chain of reasons or a fixed picture.

Two habits keep you steady. Do not change your answer only because the interviewer frowned: if the numbers support you, hold. And do not defend a choice only because it is yours: if the numbers do not, change it and say why. Evidence beats opinion in both directions.

</GoDeeper>

## When you are wrong, say so

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m28-own-the-flaw" />

"Can I guess other people's codes?" With counter codes, yes. Own it in one sentence, then pick: it is inside the scope, because Wasla's links are public, or it is a flaw, so private links get random codes. Do not defend a flaw, and do not collapse. Say what changes, and keep going.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the shape of owning a flaw, and saying 'I do not know'">

Use three short steps. **Name it:** "Yes, with counters a person can guess the next code." **Say the effect:** "They could open someone else's link." **Choose scope or fix:** "In our scope links are public, so that is fine. For private pastes I would use random codes, at the cost of a lookup to avoid a clash" (chapter 20.3).

Sometimes you really do not know. Say what you do know, and say how you would find out. "I have not used that queue. I know it keeps order only inside one partition, and I would check how it handles a retry before I rely on it." That answer shows honest judgment.

Never invent a number. If you must guess, call it an assumption and say how you would check it. A wrong number said with confidence costs more than a right "I am not sure", because the interviewer then doubts every other number you said.

</GoDeeper>

## Strong answers name a cost and a switch

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m28-answer-sorter" />

Sort eight candidate sentences. A claim only says what you chose. A better answer names what it costs. The strongest also gives a number and says when you would switch. Practise saying every choice in that shape, and the follow-ups get easier because you have already answered them.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the trade-off sentence">

The shape comes from the course's authoring standards: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, the alternative, and when to switch. In a round, four parts are enough:

"I chose **A** because of **this number**. It buys **this**. It costs **that**, about **so much**. I would switch to **B** if **this changes**."

Here it is in three modules. Module 20: "A 302 keeps every click for counting. It costs a trip per click. I would send a short max-age if we stopped counting." Module 21: "I push posts below 100,000 followers. A post from 20 million followers would be 20 million writes. I would move the line if write capacity changes." Module 25: "I authorize first and capture later. A fan who loses the seat is never charged. It costs two provider calls per booking. I would charge at once only if the provider cannot split the steps."

If you cannot think of a cost, you have not found the trade-off yet. Every real choice gives something up.

</GoDeeper>

## Six common mistakes and their fixes

<MermaidDiagram title="Six common mistakes, six fixes" chart={`mindmap

    Skipping the sums

    Drawing a zoo

    Naming no cost

Most weak rounds fail in the same six ways. They draw before they ask. They skip the sums. They draw every box they know. They dive into five things for three minutes each. They name no cost. They go silent while thinking. Each mistake has a simple fix, and you have now practised all six.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the interviewer sees, and the fix">

| Mistake | What the interviewer sees | Fix | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drawing in minute 1 | A design for a problem nobody agreed on | Ask four short questions | Chapter 28.2 |
| Skipping the sums | Boxes with no reason | Say each step with its unit | Chapter 2.3 |
| Drawing a zoo | Habit, not thought | One box per number | Chapter 28.2 |
| Five shallow dives | No evidence of depth | One dive, 15 minutes | Chapter 28.2 |
| Naming no cost | No judgment | Choice, cost, number, switch | This chapter |
| Going silent | No way to help or score | Think out loud | This chapter |

Two more are worth knowing. **Reciting a memorised design.** If you copy the Wasla design without checking the numbers, a cache and a queue appear that nothing in the estimate asks for. The module 20 gate warned about exactly this. **Ignoring a hint.** If the interviewer asks "what about X?" twice, X is where the score is. Go there.

</GoDeeper>

Practise on the eight case studies. Set a timer for 45 minutes, or 20 for a short round. Say your answer out loud, not just in your head. Then compare it with the model answer in the folds, and open the linked chapters for anything you missed.

<GoDeeper title="How to run a timed round">

Pick a prompt. Start a 45-minute timer, or a 20-minute timer for a short round. Use a sheet of paper or a whiteboard, and talk. Recording your voice on a phone is useful but optional, and you must never need one: say it to yourself if that is all you have.

Use the boxes from chapter 28.2. Full round: 5, 5, 10, 15, 7 and 3 minutes. Short round: 2, 3, 5, 6, 3 and 1 minutes. Look at the clock at the three checks, and cut out loud if you are behind.

When the timer ends, do not read the model answer first. Score yourself on the five rows from chapter 28.1: numbers, design choices, failure, evidence and communication. Then read the model answer. Where it has a number you did not say, or a cost you did not name, open the chapter it links to.

Each prompt below is one sentence an interviewer might say. The model answer is the outline of a strong round, not a script. Every number comes from the case-study module named in the entry and is a course assumption. A real interviewer may give you different numbers, and then you redo the sums.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Practice bank 1: modules 20 to 23">

**1. "Design a URL shortener."** (Module 20) *Numbers by minute 10:* creates, opens and storage. 500 million new links a month and 100 opens per link make about 193 creates and 19,300 opens a second, about 96,500 at the peak, and 9 TB over five years. Opens drive it. *Sketch:* a create path (rate limit, code, put if absent, 201) and an open path (cache, then store, 302), split because creates are about 1% of traffic. *Deep dive:* the read path: a 90% hit rate leaves about 11,000 database operations a second, which 5 partitions share. Or the codes: 7 characters of base62 from counter blocks. *Defend:* a 302 over a 301. It keeps every click for counting and the power to stop a link, at the cost of a trip per click. *Failure:* the cache restarts empty and the database sees about 9 times its plan. Spread the cache, merge duplicate misses, limit unknown codes. *Study:* 20.1 to 20.4.

**2. "Design a news feed."** (Module 21) *Numbers:* 50 million daily users who open the feed 10 times and post 0.2 times a day make about 17,400 opens and 350 posts a second at the peak, so reads outnumber writes 50 to 1. Building every feed on read would be about 2.6 million lookups a second. *Sketch:* a post store, follows kept in both directions, and a timeline of post ids per active reader. Publish saves the post and queues an event, and workers add the id to follower timelines. *Deep dive:* fan-out on write or on read. Push by default, but pull accounts above about 100,000 followers, because one post from a 20-million-follower account would hold the fan-out fleet for over two minutes. *Defend:* the hybrid line, set from write capacity and the share of opens that meet a slow lookup. It costs a merge on read for big accounts and a line to retune. *Failure:* the timeline store loses data. Rebuild by pull, active readers first, since rebuilding all 50 million at once is about 7.5 billion lookups. *Study:* 21.1 to 21.4.

**3. "Design a chat app with online status and alerts."** (Module 22) *Numbers:* 10 million daily users sending 40 messages each make about 4,630 messages a second, 14,000 at the peak, and about 44 TB a year. Open connections drive it: about 3 million at the peak, so about 60 gateways at 50,000 lines each. *Sketch:* phones keep a WebSocket to a gateway tier. A stateless chat service numbers each message per conversation, saves it, and only then acknowledges. A registry maps user to gateway, and an offline user gets a push. *Deep dive:* connections at scale (a registry with a short time to live, delivery counted at the phone's acknowledgement, gateways drained with backoff and jitter) or ordering (a counter per conversation, and a client id so a retry is safe). *Defend:* save before you acknowledge. It costs about a millisecond and means no message the sender saw as sent is lost. *Failure:* the registry is lost. Lookups miss, so messages are saved and pushed while gateways register again, and pushes are limited per person. *Study:* 22.1 to 22.4.

**4. "Design search with autocomplete for an online shop."** (Module 23) *Numbers:* 50 million shoppers a day, 4 searches each and 5 suggestion calls per search make about 7,000 searches and 35,000 suggestion calls a second at the peak. The index is about 80 GB, so 8 shards. Two systems: a sharded index for search and a small precomputed structure for suggestions. *Deep dive:* the inverted index (rare words score higher; one copy of each shard answers and a coordinator merges; with 8 shards about 1 search in 13 is as slow as a shard's worst 1%) or typeahead (a trie in memory with the best 3 queries at each node, rebuilt every 10 minutes, with a cache for the shortest prefixes). *Defend:* suggestions from a snapshot, not the live index. It costs up to 10 minutes of staleness, so add a small live list of trending queries if that hurts. *Failure:* suggestions time out. The app drops them after 100 ms and search still works. *Study:* 23.1 to 23.3.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Practice bank 2: modules 24 to 27">

**5. "Design nearby drivers and ride matching."** (Module 24) *Numbers:* 1 million drivers reporting every 4 seconds make 250,000 location updates a second. 200,000 riders refreshing every 5 seconds make 40,000 searches a second. There are about 580 ride requests a second at the peak, and the latest places are only 100 MB. The writes are the load. *Sketch:* cut the map into cells. Keep an in-memory map from cell to drivers, sharded by cell prefix, and send history to a log. A search reads the rider's cell and its neighbours, then measures the true distance. *Deep dive:* location updates at scale, or matching: claim a driver with a compare-and-set from free to held with a 10 second expiry, and offer to one driver at a time. *Defend:* not writing every update to a database, because a place is out of date in 4 seconds. A restart empties the map until drivers report again, about 4 seconds. Add a standby if that hurts. *Failure:* a hot cell such as a stadium at night. Split it and cache the map view for 2 seconds. *Study:* 24.1 to 24.4.

**6. "Design a ticket drop for a concert, with payments."** (Module 25) *Numbers:* 400,000 fans, half of them in the first 10 seconds, make about 80,000 requests a second at the peak for 20,000 seats, so 9 in 10 holds must fail. Payments are only about 167 a second, or about 330 provider calls. Two problems: contention on the seats and exactness of the money. *Deep dive:* holds (one conditional update with an expiry, no lock queue, and a waiting room sized with Little's law) or the ledger (append-only double entries in whole cents, a unique source id so a repeated webhook posts once, and a reversal instead of an edit). *Defend:* authorize the card, confirm the seat, then capture. A fan who loses the seat is never charged. It costs two provider calls per booking and an authorization that can expire. Charge at once only if the provider cannot split the steps. *Failure:* duplicate, late or missing webhooks. Save each under its event id, move payment states only forward, poll payments stuck pending, and reconcile every night. *Study:* 25.1 to 25.4.

**7. "Design a web crawler."** (Module 26) *Numbers:* 2 billion pages a month make about 772 pages a second, about 600 Mbit/s in, and about 50 TB a month stored after compression. Politeness drives it: at one request a second per site, about 772 different sites must have work waiting at once. *Sketch:* seeds go into a URL frontier. Stateless fetchers use DNS and robots caches. A parser sends text to storage and links through a filter and a seen check. The frontier is partitioned by host. *Deep dive:* the frontier (per-host queues, a time when each host may be called again, and priority) or duplicates (clean the URL, check a Bloom filter, hash the page text). *Defend:* a Bloom filter for seen URLs. For 20 billion URLs at a 1% error it takes about 24 GB, against 160 GB for an exact set. It costs about 1% of new pages skipped. Check important URLs in an exact table, or rebuild with new hash seeds. *Failure:* a crawler trap such as an endless calendar. Use a page budget per host and a depth limit. *Study:* 26.1 to 26.4.

**8. "Design a video platform."** (Module 27) *Numbers:* a billion views a day of 3 minutes each make about 11,600 views a second, about 2.1 million people watching at once, and about 4.2 Tbit/s sent on average and 12.5 at the peak. Bandwidth drives it. *Sketch:* for upload, a signed address, chunks sent straight to object storage, an event on a queue and workers that make several versions. For play, a cached watch page, and a playlist and short pieces from a CDN. View events go to sharded counters, off the play path. *Deep dive:* delivery with a CDN: a 95% hit rate leaves under 1 Tbit/s at the origin at the peak, instead of 12.5. Or uploads and transcoding. *Defend:* a signed upload address instead of streaming files through the API, which would put about 40 Gbit/s of file bytes through it at the peak. It costs a later check of the file in the pipeline. *Failure:* a new popular video is not in the CDN yet, so a burst of misses reaches the origin. Merge duplicate misses at the edge. *Study:* 27.1 and 27.2, then 27.3 to 27.5 when you reach them.

</GoDeeper>

## Quick check

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "The interviewer says traffic will triple. Wasla's peak is 100,000 opens and 1,000 creates a second. The hit rate stays at 90%, and a database node is planned for 2,500 operations a second. How many partitions does the database need?"
options:
  - "About 14, from 33,000 operations a second"
  - "About 5, the same as before the change"
  - "About 12, from 30,000 operations a second"
  - "About 40, from 99,000 operations a second"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. Opens triple to 300,000, so 30,000 miss. Creates triple to 3,000. The total is 33,000, and 33,000 divided by 2,500 is 13.2, so 14 partitions."
  - "Five partitions would each carry 6,600 operations a second, which is 2.6 times the plan. Tripling the traffic triples the database load."
  - "This forgets the creates. The 30,000 misses are right, but the 3,000 creates also reach the database, so the total is 33,000."
  - "This applies the factor of 3 twice. The load after tripling is 33,000 operations a second, not 3 times that."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "You propose a cache. The interviewer says: 'I do not think you need one.' Your numbers: 101,000 database operations a second without it, and a node planned for 2,500. Which reply is strongest?"
options:
  - "Caches are standard in every design like this one, so I would rather keep it in place."
  - "Fair point. I will drop it and run 40 database nodes instead of using a cache at all."
  - "Without it the database is 40 times over its plan. What load would change your mind?"
  - "I am confident the cache is needed, so can we move on to the next part of the design now?"
correct: 2
explanations:
  - "This defends the choice by habit. It gives the interviewer no number to check, and it does not answer why this design needs a cache."
  - "Agreeing so fast is a weak reply. 40 nodes cost far more than a cache and 5 partitions, and you have not weighed that cost or said it."
  - "Right. It brings the number that separates the two designs, 101,000 against 2,500, and asks what would change the interviewer's mind. It stays open to being wrong."
  - "Confidence is not evidence. Moving on without an answer leaves the question open, and the interviewer will return to it."
```
