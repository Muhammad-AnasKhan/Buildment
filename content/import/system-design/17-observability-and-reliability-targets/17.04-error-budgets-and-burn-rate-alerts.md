An SLO of 99.9% means that 1 request in 1,000 may fail. That small allowance is useful: it tells you how much trouble you can afford, and how fast you are using it up.

## The budget is what the SLO lets fail

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m17-budget-spend" />

An SLO below 100% leaves room for failure. That room is the error budget. A 99.9% target over 30 days allows 43 bad minutes. A 10 minute problem on day 6 spends 10 of them. A 25 minute bad deploy on day 19 spends 25 more. By day 19 only 8 minutes are left.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the budget as a number">

The <GlossaryTerm term="error budget" definition="The amount of failure an SLO allows in its window: one minus the target. A 99.9% SLO leaves a budget of 0.1% of requests, or 43.2 minutes of full downtime in 30 days." /> is one minus the target. For 99.9% that is 0.001. Over 30 days (43,200 minutes) it is 43.2 minutes of full downtime, or 1,000 failed requests in every million.

The Google SRE book gives a second example. With a 99.999% target the budget is 0.001%. A problem that fails 0.0002% of requests spends 20% of the quarter's budget, because 0.0002 divided by 0.001 is 0.2 [S96]. A small failure rate on a very strict target is already a large share of the budget.

The picture counts bad minutes, which is the easy view. A request-based SLO spends its budget in failed requests, so an outage in the busy hour costs more than the same outage at night. The two incidents are course numbers.

The budget is not a target to reach. Spending none of it means the SLO is too loose or the team is too careful. Spending all of it means trouble. A healthy service uses part of the budget on planned risk, such as releases, experiments and maintenance, and keeps the rest for surprises.

</GoDeeper>

## Burn rate: how fast you spend it

<MermaidDiagram title="From error rate to time left" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Error rate now: 1.44%"] --> B["Allowed by a 99.9% SLO: 0.1%"]
  B --> C["Burn rate = 1.44 / 0.1 = 14.4"]
  C --> D["30-day budget lasts 30 / 14.4 = about 2 days"]
`} legend="Burn rate is the error rate divided by the error rate the SLO allows." />

Burn rate compares how fast you are failing with how fast the SLO allows. A burn rate of 1 uses exactly the whole budget by the end of the window. A burn rate of 14.4 uses it in 30 divided by 14.4, about 2 days. The bigger the number, the sooner you must act.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: burn rate arithmetic">

The formula is: burn rate equals the error rate divided by (1 minus the SLO). It tells you how many times faster than allowed you are failing. Turn it around to get an alert threshold: the error rate to alert on is the burn rate times (1 minus the SLO). For a 99.9% SLO and a burn rate of 14.4 the error rate is 14.4 times 0.001, which is 1.44%. {/* source needed: Google SRE Workbook, "Alerting on SLOs", definition of burn rate and the threshold formula */}

| Burn rate | Error rate (99.9% SLO) | 30-day budget lasts |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.1% | 30 days |
| 6 | 0.6% | 5 days |
| 14.4 | 1.44% | 2.1 days (50 hours) |
| 50 | 5% | 14.4 hours |
| 1,000 | 100% (total outage) | 43.2 minutes |

The last row matches the 43.2 minutes of the first beat: a total outage burns 1,000 times faster than allowed. The same table shows how much budget an alert has already let go by when it fires. A burn of 14.4 for 1 hour spends 14.4 divided by 720 hours, which is 2% of a 30-day budget. A burn of 6 for 6 hours spends 5%. A burn of 1 for 3 days spends 10%. {/* source needed: Google SRE Workbook, "Alerting on SLOs", table of budget consumed per window */}

In code, the SLI-to-burn step is two lines, and you write it in chapter 17.7:

```js
const burnRate = (sli, slo) => (1 - sli) / (1 - slo);
burnRate(0.9856, 0.999); // 14.4 (about, in floating point)
```

## Replay an incident, see who is paged

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m17-burn-replay" />

Pick an incident and switch the alerts on or off, then drag the time rail through the incident. The naive alert pages within a minute for every kind of trouble, even a 3 minute blip. The burn-rate alerts stay quiet for the blip, page for the outage, and catch the slow leak that the fast alert misses.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what each alert did in the replay">

The model is simple on purpose: a steady baseline of 0.05% errors, then an incident at a fixed error rate, and no random noise. The SLO is 99.9%. The three alerts are: naive (the error rate over 5 minutes is at or above 0.1%), fast (1 hour and 5 minutes both at 14.4 times the allowed rate) and slow (6 hours and 30 minutes both at 6 times).

| Incident | Naive | Fast | Slow |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 minute blip at 8% | Pages at once | Never | Never |
| 30 minute outage at 5% | Pages at once, clears at 35 min | Pages at minute 17, clears at 34, 2.0% of the budget gone | Never (the 6 hour burn peaks at 4.6) |
| 5 hour leak at 1.2% | Pages at once | Never (the 1 hour burn peaks at 12.0) | Pages at minute 173, 4.8% of the budget gone |

The table has three lessons. First, the naive alert is fastest, and it is useless: it fires for the blip as well, and at 5 requests a second random noise makes it fire about 27 times a day for nothing (you measure this in challenge 16). Second, the fast alert catches the outage in 17 minutes, but a leak that burns at 12 never crosses its line of 14.4. Third, the slow alert exists for exactly that leak: it takes nearly three hours to notice, but it does notice. No single threshold does all three jobs.

Challenge 16 uses random traffic and a blip before the leak, so its page times differ a little (minute 17 for the outage and minute 142 for the leak). The shape is the same.

</GoDeeper>

## Two windows stop false pages

<MermaidDiagram title="The multiwindow burn-rate rule" chart={`flowchart LR
  L["Long window, 1 hour: burn at least 14.4? Big enough to matter"] --> B{"Both yes?"}
  S["Short window, 5 minutes: burn at least 14.4? Still happening now"] --> B
  B -->|"yes"| P["Page a person"]
  B -->|"no"| N["No page"]
`} legend="A rule fires only when both windows agree. The same shape works for the 6 hour and 30 minute rule at a burn of 6." />

One window is not enough. A long window alone keeps ringing for an hour after the fix. A short window alone pages for a 3 minute blip. Together they ask two questions: is it big enough to matter, and is it still happening? Both must say yes.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the table most teams start from">

The Google SRE Workbook recommends several rules at once for a 99.9% SLO over 30 days. {/* source needed: Google SRE Workbook, "Alerting on SLOs", multiwindow, multi-burn-rate table */}

| Severity | Long window | Short window | Burn rate | Budget spent when it fires |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Page | 1 hour | 5 minutes | 14.4 | 2% |
| Page | 6 hours | 30 minutes | 6 | 5% |
| Ticket | 3 days | 6 hours | 1 | 10% |

The short window is about one twelfth of the long one. It makes the alert reset soon after the fix: when the problem ends, the short window is clean in 5 minutes even though the long window still remembers it. The last row is a ticket, not a page: a burn of 1 means the budget will be gone exactly at the end of the window. Someone should look this week, but nobody needs waking.

Here is the rule in code. The version you write in chapter 17.7 does the same with real log entries:

```js
// burns: { long, short } for each rule. A rule fires when both are at least its burn number.
const fires = (b, rule) => b.long >= rule.burn && b.short >= rule.burn;
```

**Low traffic.** With one request a minute at night, one failed request is a short-window burn of 200 and a long-window burn of about 17. Both pass 14.4, so a single error pages someone. Add a minimum request count before an alert may fire, or use a probe that sends steady traffic.

</GoDeeper>

## Page a human only if they must act

<MermaidDiagram title="Should this alert page a person?" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["An alert fires"] --> B{"Are users hurt now, or soon?"}
  B -->|"no"| T["A dashboard or a ticket. Not a page"]
  B -->|"yes"| C{"Can a person do something useful right now?"}
  C -->|"no"| X["Fix the alert, or automate the fix"]
  C -->|"yes"| P["Page the on-call person"]
`} legend="A page is for an event that is hurting users and needs a human to think." />

A page wakes a person at 3 am, so it must be worth it. Ask two questions. Are users hurt now, or soon? Can a person do something useful right now? If either answer is no, send a ticket or fix the alert. Every page should need thinking, not just a restart.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: symptoms, causes and alert fatigue">

The SRE book gives the rule: every page should be actionable, and every page response should need intelligence. If a robot could do the response, the robot should do it. Pages should be about a new problem, not one the person has seen ten times [S97]. The same chapter says to spend more effort catching symptoms than causes [S97].

A burn-rate alert is a symptom alert: it fires because users are failing, and it is the same whether the cause is a bad deploy, a full disk or a card provider. "CPU is at 90%" is a cause alert. Sometimes it is a real warning, but often the CPU is at 90% and users are fine. Pages on causes train people to ignore pages.

**Alert fatigue** is what happens when most pages need no action. People stop trusting the pager, answer slowly and sleep badly, and one real page gets lost in the noise. In challenge 16 the naive alert opens 30 pages in a day and 27 of them have no incident behind them. The burn-rate alert opens 2 pages, both for real incidents.

**What a good page carries.** A link to the dashboard for that SLO, the burn rate, the time it started, and a runbook: a short written guide of what to check first. Where a person does not answer in a few minutes, the page escalates to a second person.

</GoDeeper>

## A spent budget changes what the team does

<MermaidDiagram title="An error budget policy" chart={`flowchart LR
  Q{"Budget left?"} -->|"yes"| A["Ship features. Accept some risk"]
  Q -->|"no"| B["Freeze risky releases. Fix reliability until budget returns"]
`} legend="The budget turns an argument between speed and safety into a rule both sides agreed on beforehand." />

The budget is also a rule for the team. While some budget is left, ship. When it is spent, stop risky releases and fix reliability until it recovers. This turns an argument between speed and safety into a number both sides agreed on beforehand.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: writing and using a budget policy">

In the SRE book's words, releases continue while error budget remains and halt when it is spent [S96]. A written policy makes that real. It says who may pause releases (usually the service owners together with the product lead), what is paused (new features and risky changes, not fixes for the reliability problem itself), what exceptions exist (security fixes), and what ends the pause (budget back above zero, or a review that finds the cause).

A policy only works if it was agreed before the bad month. If the first time anyone mentions it is the day the budget is gone, it looks like a punishment. Agreed early, it is a shared tool: product teams know that a reliable service buys them room to move fast.

**Two failure modes.** The SLO is set so tight that the budget is always empty and the policy is ignored. Or it is so loose that the budget is never touched and nobody learns from it. If a service never uses its budget, consider raising the target or taking more risk. If it always overspends, fix the service or lower the target honestly.

**In AWS.** Amazon CloudWatch can alarm on a metric, and its metric math can combine metrics into a ratio such as errors over requests. A burn-rate alert is built from that ratio over two windows. {/* source needed: CloudWatch alarms and metric math documentation */}

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

How to say it: "The SLO leaves an error budget, which is one minus the target. I alert on burn rate, which is how many times faster than allowed we are spending it. I use a long and a short window together: the long one says the problem is big enough, the short one says it is still happening, so we do not page for a blip and the page stops soon after a fix. Slow burns open a ticket, not a page."

The follow-up you will get: "What do you do when the budget is spent?" A good answer describes a policy agreed before: pause risky releases, keep fixing reliability, and resume when the budget recovers.

</GoDeeper>
