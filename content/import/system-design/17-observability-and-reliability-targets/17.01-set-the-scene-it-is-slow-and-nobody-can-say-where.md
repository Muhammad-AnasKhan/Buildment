Souq Express is a made-up food delivery app. It is Friday, 19:40, the busiest hour of the week, and support's inbox fills with the same message. Look at the picture and ask yourself two everyday questions: what would you try first, and what do you wish the team had in front of them?

## Slow, and nobody can say where

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m17-blind-outage" />

Customers say the app is slow. Support passes it on. The on-call team opens the one graph they have: the CPU is at 35%, which looks fine. They search the log files of three servers and find 9,400 lines and no answer. The system may not be broken at all. The team simply cannot see inside it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the team is missing">

The team cannot answer three plain questions. The first is: is it really slow, for how many customers, and since when? The second is: which part of the system is slow, the app, the menu, the payments or something outside? The third is: what exactly happened in that part? Each question needs a different kind of evidence, and the team has only one graph and a pile of text.

In this made-up story a developer finds the cause 52 minutes later, by luck: she searches the logs for the word "timeout" and sees that the card provider has been answering slowly. Nothing was wrong with the team's skill. They had no way to look. The 52 minutes, the 112 messages and the other numbers in the picture are course numbers.

A second problem is hidden in the story. Nobody agreed beforehand what "slow" means. Is 2 seconds slow? Is it slow for one customer in a hundred, or for half of them? Without a target, even a perfect graph would start an argument. You will fix that in chapters 17.3 and 17.4.

Souq Express, its customers and the 19:40 outage are a made-up composite for this course. Real systems fail in the same way: an old and well-known example is Meta's outage of 4 October 2021, where recovery was slow partly because the internal tools the engineers needed depended on the same network that had failed [S136]. A team that cannot see inside its system, or cannot reach its own tools, loses time while customers wait.

</GoDeeper>

## Five tools, one question each

Three kinds of signal show what the system is doing. A target says how slow is too slow. An alert wakes a person only when it matters. A dashboard shows the answer at a glance. A calm routine handles the incident and then learns from it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what you already have, and what this module adds">

You already hold the numbers. In chapter 2.4 you learned that an average hides the slowest requests and that a percentile such as p99 does not. In chapter 2.6 you turned nines into minutes of downtime and met the error budget for the first time. Modules 4 and 16 gave you health checks, timeouts and retries. All of them need a way to tell, while the system runs, whether they work.

This module adds the missing view. In 17.2 you meet logs, metrics and traces and learn which question each one answers. In 17.3 you write an SLI and an SLO and see what an SLA adds. In 17.4 you turn the budget into alerts that fire for real problems and stay quiet for noise. In 17.5 you build dashboards with the RED and USE methods. In 17.6 you run an incident and write a postmortem.

Then you build. In 17.7 you write the code that turns request logs into an SLI, a burn rate and a yes or no answer to "should this wake a person?". In challenge 16 you add traces and metrics to a three-service app and replay a day of traffic through your own alert. At the gate you write SLOs for your capstone idea.
