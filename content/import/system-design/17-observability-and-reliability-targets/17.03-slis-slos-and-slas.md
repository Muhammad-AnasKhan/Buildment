"The app should be fast and reliable" is a wish, not a target. This chapter turns the wish into a number you can measure and a line you can miss.

## Measure, target, contract

<MermaidDiagram title="SLI, SLO and SLA" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["SLI: what we measure. Last month, 99.93% of checkouts worked"] -->|"compare with"| B["SLO: the target we set. At least 99.9%"]
  B -->|"a customer contract adds a cost"| C["SLA: the promise. Below 99.5%, we give a credit"]
`} legend="The percentages are examples. An SLI is a measurement, an SLO is a goal for it, and an SLA is a contract with a consequence." />

Three words that sound alike do three jobs. An SLI is a number you measure. An SLO is the target you set for that number. An SLA is a contract that says what the provider owes the customer when a target is missed. Most teams need SLIs and SLOs first. Only some need an SLA.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the three definitions">

The Google SRE book gives the standard wording. A service level indicator (SLI) is "a carefully defined quantitative measure" of some part of the service you give. A service level objective (SLO) is "a target value or range of values" for an SLI. A service level agreement (SLA) is a contract with users that "includes consequences" if the service misses what it promises [S95].

The quick test is the consequence. If nothing happens when the number is missed except that the team feels bad, it is an SLO. If money, a refund, a credit or a lawsuit follows, it is an SLA. Teams often write "SLA" for everything, which leads to confusion: an internal target between two teams is not an SLA unless someone owes something.

Souq Express is made up. Its checkout might have an SLI (the share of checkouts that work), an SLO (99.9% over 28 days) and, for its restaurant partners, an SLA that gives a credit if the order feed is below 99.5%. All the percentages here are examples chosen for the course.

</GoDeeper>

## An SLO is one sentence

<MermaidDiagram title="The four parts of an SLO" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Target: 99.9%"] --> B["Of what: valid checkout requests"]
  B --> C["Good means: no 5xx and under 800 ms"]
  C --> D["Over: a rolling 28 days"]
`} legend="If any one part is missing, two people will read the SLO two ways." />

Write an SLO as one sentence with four parts: the target, what you count, what counts as good, and the window. "99.9% of valid checkout requests are good, over a rolling 28 days." If a part is missing, two people read it two ways. "The site is fast" is not an SLO.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: writing SLOs that people can use">

Start from what a user does, not from what a server does. Souq Express has journeys: browse the menu, place an order, track the order. Each journey gets one or two SLOs, not twenty. A few well-chosen SLOs are easier to defend and to alert on than a long list nobody reads.

Two kinds of SLI cover most journeys. An availability SLI asks "did it work?": the share of valid requests that did not fail. A latency SLI asks "was it fast enough?": the share of requests that finished under a threshold, such as 800 ms. In chapter 2.4 you saw why this is written with a percentile and a threshold, "99% of requests under 300 ms", and not with an average. The SRE book also advises using percentiles rather than the mean [S95].

**Rolling or calendar.** A rolling window always covers the last 28 days, so the number moves every minute and old trouble drops out smoothly. A calendar month resets on the first day, which gives a fresh budget but lets a bad last day look fine on the second. Many teams choose 28 days because it always holds exactly four weeks, with four weekends. That is a design choice, not a rule.

**Valid events.** A request the caller got wrong, such as a 404 for a page that does not exist or a 401 for a wrong password, is not our failure. Leave it out of both counts. You will write this rule in code in chapter 17.7.

**Weak wording to avoid.** "Response time under 1 second" has no population, no share and no window. "99.9% of checkouts under 1 second over 28 days" has all three.

</GoDeeper>

## Good over valid

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m17-sli-ratio" />

The usual availability SLI is good requests divided by valid requests. A server error is bad. A 404 is the caller's mistake, so it is not valid and is left out. Here two of three valid requests were good, so the SLI is 66.7%. Real services count millions of requests.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: counting good and valid, and the two ways to count">

In code the idea is a few lines. The version you write in 17.7 also handles slow requests and empty windows:

```js
// A request is { ts, status, ms }. 4xx are the caller's mistakes: not valid.
function availabilitySli(requests) {
  const valid = requests.filter((r) => r.status < 400 || r.status >= 500);
  const good = valid.filter((r) => r.status < 500);
  return valid.length === 0 ? null : good.length / valid.length;
}
```

There are two ways to count. A request-based SLI counts requests: good divided by valid. A time-based SLI counts minutes: the share of minutes in which the service worked. In chapter 2.6 you turned an availability into hours of downtime, which is the time-based view. For a service with steady traffic the two are close. For a service whose traffic changes, a 10-minute outage at the evening peak hurts far more users than one at 4 am, and a request-based number shows that while a time-based number does not.

**Latency counts too.** A request that comes back after 12 seconds as a 200 is not good for the user. Many teams count it as bad when it is slower than a threshold: good means no 5xx and under 800 ms. A fast error is still bad. A slow success is bad too.

**Low traffic.** With one request a minute at night, a single failure is a 100% failure rate for that minute. This is why teams add synthetic probes (a script that sends a steady trickle of test requests) and a minimum number of requests before an alert may fire.

</GoDeeper>

## Pick a target and a window

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m17-slo-window" />

Drag the target along the rail and choose a window. The bar shows the allowed bad time as a sliver at its end. Try 99.9% over 30 days, then 99.99%: each extra nine cuts the allowance ten times. Then see whether a 25 minute outage fits inside it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the table behind the picture">

The arithmetic is one multiplication: allowed bad minutes equal one minus the target, times the days in the window, times 1,440 minutes in a day. For the request view, allowed bad requests per million equal one minus the target, times a million.

| Target | 7 days | 28 days | 30 days | 90 days | Bad requests per million |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 99% | 100.8 min | 403.2 min | 432 min | 1,296 min | 10,000 |
| 99.5% | 50.4 min | 201.6 min | 216 min | 648 min | 5,000 |
| 99.9% | 10.1 min | 40.3 min | 43.2 min | 129.6 min | 1,000 |
| 99.95% | 5.0 min | 20.2 min | 21.6 min | 64.8 min | 500 |
| 99.99% | 1.0 min | 4.0 min | 4.3 min | 13.0 min | 100 |

Read the 99.99% row. Over 30 days you may lose 4.3 minutes. That is shorter than most people need to notice an alert, open a laptop and log in, so a team at this target must recover without a human. A shorter window gives less room: 99.9% over 7 days allows only about 10 minutes, so one bad deploy can spend it.

**Why not aim for 100%?** The SRE book calls a target of 100% "unrealistic and undesirable" [S95]. It would also stop the team from ever shipping a risky change. The same book tells a story about the Chubby lock service: the team deliberately took planned outages so that users could not come to depend on a service that was much more reliable than its SLO [S95]. A target is also a promise about what users may rely on.

**Choosing a number.** Look at what users need, and at what your service does now. If it already runs at 99.95%, an SLO of 99.9% leaves a margin for change. If it runs at 99.5%, an SLO of 99.99% is a wish. Start near what you can do, and raise the target when you can show users would notice.

</GoDeeper>

## Count as close to users as possible

<MermaidDiagram title="Where to count" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["Phone or probe: sees what a user sees"] --> L["Load balancer: sees most requests. The usual place"]
  L --> A["App: misses what never arrives"]
  A --> D["Database: cause, not a symptom"]
`} legend="The further right you count, the more failures are invisible. Failures to the left of your counting point do not appear in the SLI." />

Where you count changes the number. Counting in the app misses requests that never arrived, such as a crash loop. Counting at the load balancer sees most of what users meet. A probe from outside sees what a user sees, but it sends little traffic. Pick the place closest to the user that you can trust.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: symptoms, causes and black-box checks">

The SRE book separates a symptom (what a user sees: "I am getting HTTP 500s") from a cause (what is behind it: "the database refuses connections"). An SLI should follow the symptom [S97]. A database that is slow but still answers inside the user's time limit is not a symptom yet.

Black-box monitoring tests what is visible from outside, so it sees the symptom whatever the cause. White-box monitoring uses what the system reveals about itself, such as logs and metrics. For an SLI, the load balancer logs are a good middle: they are white-box data about the very point where users arrive.

A crash loop shows why the place matters. If the app dies on start for 10 minutes, no request reaches it, so the app's own log has no failures and its SLI shows nothing wrong. The load balancer sees every request and every error answer. A probe does too. In chapter 17.7 your function returns `null`, not 100%, when there are no valid requests, so an empty window is never mistaken for a perfect one.

Dependencies you do not control, such as a card provider, belong in the picture too. Your users do not care whose fault the failure is. Count what they see, and use traces (17.2) to find out where it came from.

</GoDeeper>

## An SLA adds money, and a margin

<MermaidDiagram title="SLO and SLA are different lines" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["100%: not realistic"] --> B["SLO 99.9%: the team's own line. Missing it stops risky releases"]
  B --> C["SLA 99.5%: the customer's line. Missing it costs a credit"]
`} legend="The SLA is looser than the SLO on purpose, so the team notices trouble long before money is owed." />

An SLA turns a target into a promise with a cost. It is set looser than the SLO on purpose, so the team feels pain long before a customer is owed a credit. Many internal services have an SLO and no SLA at all.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what an SLA needs">

An SLA names the measure, the window, the exact consequence (a credit of a share of the monthly fee, a refund) and the exclusions (planned maintenance, customer mistakes). It is a legal and a business document as much as a technical one, so engineers and lawyers write it together, and the engineers check that the team can measure it.

Look at the picture's two lines. The team's SLO of 99.9% over 28 days allows about 40 minutes of bad time. The customer's SLA at 99.5% allows about 200. If the team misses its own line, a release freeze starts (chapter 17.4). If it misses the customer's line, money is owed. Nobody wants the second to come first. The numbers are examples for the course, and a real SLA is a negotiation.

**A common mistake.** Copying a vendor's SLA into your own SLO. A cloud database that promises 99.95% gives you at best a ceiling: your service cannot be more available than the parts it needs (chapter 2.6, series). Your SLO must be lower than the chain of parts allows, or you must add copies to make up for it.

**In an incident,** it is the SLO and its budget that drive decisions, not the SLA. The SLA only decides who pays afterward.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

How to say it: "An SLI is the measurement, for example good requests over valid requests. An SLO is the target with a window, such as 99.9% over 28 days. An SLA is the customer contract with a penalty, and it is looser than the SLO so we notice first. I pick SLOs per user journey, count as close to the user as I can, and leave out requests that failed because of the caller."

The follow-up you will get: "How did you choose 99.9% and not 99.99%?" A good answer says what users need, what the system does today, and what the extra nine would cost (a 99.99% target allows 4.3 minutes in 30 days).

</GoDeeper>
