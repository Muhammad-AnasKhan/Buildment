## Learning log

1. **Your trace:** In part 1 of challenge 16, which span held the most time in your slowest order, and how many milliseconds of the whole order was that? What would you have guessed before you opened the trace?
2. **Your split:** When you ran with `PROPAGATE=0`, how many traces did 269 orders make? What did the waterfall of the slowest order lose?
3. **Your noise:** In part 2, how many pages did the naive alert open, and how many of them were during a real incident? What would a person on call do after a week of that?
4. **Your price:** The burn-rate alert paged for the outage some minutes after it began. How many, and what share of the 30-day budget was spent by then? Is that a fair price for having no false pages? Say why.
5. **Your pager:** Name one alert in a system you know, or one you invented, that should be a ticket and not a page. Say how you decided.

## Your gate answer: SLOs for your capstone idea

In module 29 you will design, build and defend a system of your own choice. You do not have to have picked it yet. If you have not, choose an app you know well, with users who would notice if it were slow or down. This question asks for what a team would write on the first day of running it: what "reliable enough" means in numbers, and what would wake a person.

Write two or three SLOs for your idea, in about 120 words or more. For each one, give four parts. First, the user journey it protects, such as "a rider requests a ride" or "a student uploads notes". Second, the SLI: what you count, which requests are valid, and what makes one good. Third, the target and the window, for example "99.9% over a rolling 28 days", with the allowed bad minutes in that window. Fourth, how you would measure it and where: at the load balancer, in the app, or with a probe, and why that place.

Then say what would page a person. Use a burn-rate alert, as in chapter 17.4: name the burn rate, the long window and the short window, and say what that alert catches that a simple error-rate alert would not. Name one thing that should be a ticket and not a page.

A weak answer says "the site should be fast and available" or "99.99% uptime". A strong answer is specific and does the arithmetic. For a ride-hailing app it might say: "Requesting a ride: 99.9% of valid ride requests get a driver offer within 3 seconds, over a rolling 28 days. That allows about 40 bad minutes. Counted at the load balancer; 4xx requests are not valid." A strong answer picks a number that the system could really meet. It also does not pretend that every feature needs the same nines: browsing a menu can be looser than paying for an order.

Be careful with three common mistakes. The first is choosing a target because it sounds good. Five nines allows about five minutes a year, and it costs far more than three nines, so say what your users need. The second is an SLI that no one can measure, such as "user happiness". Pick something you can count in a log. The third is paging on a cause, such as high CPU, instead of on users failing. If you mention CPU, put it on a dashboard or in a ticket.

Use your own numbers from challenge 16 where they help. For example: "In my replay the naive alert opened 30 pages and 27 were noise, so I will page only on burn rate." Do not write a design for the whole system. A mentor reads for method, not for one right answer: two students can choose very different targets and both pass, as long as each one names the SLI, the valid requests, the window, the allowed bad time and the alert. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what you would do when the budget is spent, or how a low-traffic night changes the alert. Answer them by editing and resubmitting.

Keep the answer short and concrete. Around 150 words is enough for two SLOs and one alert, and a table of numbers is welcome next to your sentences. If you are unsure about a number, say how you chose it and what you would measure to check it. That honesty is worth more than a confident guess.

You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, and a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.
