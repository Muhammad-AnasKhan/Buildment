The alert fired at 19:27. Nobody planned this evening, and nobody has time to invent a method now. A short routine, practised before it is needed, is what keeps people calm. This chapter gives you that routine, and the habit of writing down what you learned afterward.

## An incident has a shape

<MermaidDiagram title="The life of an incident" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Detect: an alert or a customer"] --> B["Respond: tell people, name a lead"]
  B --> C["Mitigate: stop the harm"]
  C --> D["Resolve: check it is really over"]
  D --> E["Learn: write the postmortem"]
`} legend="Mitigate comes before the root cause. The cause is found in the learning step, when no customer is waiting." />

An incident is any event that hurts users and needs people to react. Its steps run in a fixed order: detect it, respond, mitigate, resolve and learn. Mitigation means stopping the harm, even if you do not yet know the cause. Finding the cause comes later. Write the times down as you go.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: when to declare an incident">

Declare an incident early. The Google SRE book advises declaring one as soon as a second team is needed, or customers can see the outage, or the problem is still unsolved after about an hour of focused analysis. {/* source needed: Google SRE book, "Managing Incidents": declare early, with the three examples */} Declaring costs a few minutes. Not declaring costs hours of people tripping over each other.

Most teams give incidents a severity, such as 1 for a full outage and 3 for a minor problem. The levels are a team choice, not a standard. What matters is that the level is tied to something that can be measured, and the best thing to tie it to is the burn rate you met in chapter 17.4: a fast burn is a high-severity incident, a slow burn is a ticket.

An incident also has a cost, which you can compute. Take the story in the third beat: 6% of requests failed for 16 minutes. That is 0.06 times 16, about 0.96 bad minutes, which is 2.2% of a 43.2-minute budget. A postmortem should write that number down, so everyone sees the true size of the event.

</GoDeeper>

## One person leads, one person changes

<MermaidDiagram title="Roles in a small incident" chart={`flowchart TD
  L["Incident lead: holds the whole picture, assigns jobs"] --> O["Operations: the only people who change the system"]
  L --> C["Communications: updates the team and customers"]
  L --> P["Planning: notes, bugs, handoffs"]
  D["A live incident document: times, facts, who does what"] --- L
`} legend="In a small team one person may wear two hats, but only one group changes the system at a time." />

An incident needs a few jobs, even for two people. One person leads and holds the whole picture. One person, or group, changes the system, and nobody else does. One person tells everyone else what is going on. And someone keeps notes with times. Without roles, three people make three fixes that collide. Pick the roles before you need them.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the roles and the habits behind them">

The SRE book describes four roles. The incident commander holds the high-level state and assigns jobs. The operations team applies tools to the problem and "should be the only group modifying the system". Communications is the public face and sends regular updates. Planning handles longer tasks such as filing bugs and tracking what has diverged from normal so it can be put back afterward. {/* source needed: Google SRE book, "Managing Incidents": roles and practices */}

Three habits make the roles work. First, hand off clearly. If the lead must leave, they say "you are now the incident lead, okay?" and wait for a yes before they go. Second, keep a living document with the most important facts at the top, even if it is messy. Third, declare early, so the roles exist before the panic.

**Why only one group changes the system.** If two engineers each try a fix at the same time, you cannot tell which fix worked, or whether one broke the other. A single set of hands also makes it easy to note every change with its time, which is what the postmortem needs later.

**For a team of two,** the same rules still hold. One of you is the lead and keeps the notes; the other types the commands. It feels slow, and it saves time.

</GoDeeper>

## Roll back first, find the cause later

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m17-rollback-first" />

The first job is to stop the harm, not to find the bug. Version 42 went live at minute 20 and errors rose to 6%. Dina was paged at 27, rolled back at 33, and the errors were gone at 36. The bug can be hunted tomorrow, with no customer waiting. Ask "what changed?" before you ask "why?".

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: ways to mitigate, and when to fix forward">

A rollback is one way to mitigate, and the cheapest when a recent change caused the trouble. So the first question of every incident is: what changed? Look at the list of deploys, configuration changes and feature flags in the last hours. Many incidents are explained by that list alone.

Other ways to stop harm without a root cause: turn off a feature flag; send traffic away from the failing part, to another zone or region (modules 8 and 19); shed some load or open a circuit breaker so the rest keeps working (module 16); add capacity if the cause is plain load; or restart a stuck process, knowing that this hides the cause and you must still look at it later.

Rollback is not always possible. A database change that dropped a column cannot be undone by deploying the old code. In that case the team fixes forward, with a small, careful change and one hand on the controls. The more you plan for rollback when you design a change, such as adding a column first and removing the old one weeks later, the more often it stays possible.

Two cautions. Do not change two things at once, because then you cannot tell which one helped. And write down every action with its time. The times and numbers in the picture (minutes 20, 27, 33 and 36) are course numbers.

</GoDeeper>

## Step through an incident

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m17-incident-stepper" />

Walk the evening of 19:27 one step at a time. At each step choose between two moves. A good choice moves on. A poor one shows its cost and adds minutes to the clock. Notice how many of the better choices are about order, not about technical skill. Try the poor choice once to see what it costs.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the five steps and why one choice is better">

Step 1, 19:27, the page arrives. Acknowledge it and tell the team channel. Waiting to see whether it clears costs time while errors rise, and a page nobody acknowledges can escalate to a second person for no reason.

Step 2, 19:29, three engineers join and each starts trying fixes. Name one lead and one person who changes the system. Otherwise two changes collide, as in the story of the second beat.

Step 3, 19:32, the deploy list shows version 42 at 19:20, when errors began. Roll back to version 41, which is done at 19:33. Reading code first would keep customers failing for another hour.

Step 4, 19:36, the error rate reads 0%. Watch the SLI for 15 minutes, then post an all-clear at 19:51. Closing at once misses a second spike. The graph is what proves the end, not a feeling.

Step 5, next day. Write a blameless postmortem and ask what let the bug through. Looking for the person who wrote the line teaches everyone to hide mistakes.

If you make no wrong choice, the errors are gone 9 minutes after the page, from 19:27 to 19:36. This story is a course composite and its times are course numbers.

</GoDeeper>

## A blameless postmortem teaches the system

<MermaidDiagram title="What a postmortem contains" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Summary"] --> B["Impact, with numbers"]
  B --> C["Timeline, with times"]
  C --> D["Contributing causes"]
  D --> E["What went well, what went badly"]
  E --> F["Actions: owner and date"]
`} legend="Each part has one job. The actions are the part that makes the next incident smaller." />

After the incident, write down what happened. A postmortem holds the impact, the timeline, the causes and the actions. It is blameless: it asks what let the mistake through, not who made it. You cannot fix people, but you can fix systems and routines. Give every action an owner and a date.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what makes a postmortem useful">

The SRE book defines a postmortem as a written record of an incident: its impact, the steps taken to mitigate or resolve it, the root causes and follow-up actions to stop it happening again. It says a postmortem should find contributing causes "without indicting any individual or team", and that you cannot fix people but you can fix systems and processes. {/* source needed: Google SRE book, "Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure" */}

Write one when the criteria say so, and decide the criteria before the incident. The book lists user-visible downtime above a threshold, data loss of any kind, an on-call engineer having to step in (for example a rollback), a long time to resolve, and a monitoring failure, meaning the incident was found by hand. {/* source needed: same chapter, triggers */}

**A short example for Souq Express (made up).** Impact: 6% of orders failed for 16 minutes, about 2.2% of the 28-day budget. Timeline: 19:20 version 42 live, 19:27 page, 19:32 cause found, 19:33 rollback, 19:36 errors gone, 19:51 all clear. Contributing causes: the new payments client waits 30 seconds for the provider and has no timeout of its own; the test environment has no slow provider; releases go to 100% of traffic at once. Actions: set a 2 second timeout (owner Omar, 3 November); add a slow-provider test (owner Lina, 10 November); send new builds to 5% of users first (owner Dina, 17 November).

Weak actions say "be more careful" or "review more". Good actions are specific, have an owner and a date, and change the system so that the same mistake is harder to make.

**Public examples.** Companies sometimes publish real postmortems. GitHub's analysis of 21 October 2018 describes a 43 second network partition that led to 24 hours and 11 minutes of degraded service, and the follow-up work that came from it [S134]. Read one to see how the sections above look in practice.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

How to say it: "In an incident I tell people early, name one lead and one person who changes the system, and look at what changed. I mitigate first, usually by rolling back, and find the root cause afterward. I confirm the SLI is back before I close it. Then we write a blameless postmortem with numbers, a timeline and actions with owners."

The follow-up you will get: "Rollback is not possible. What now?" A good answer fixes forward with one small change and one person at the controls, or shifts traffic or turns off the feature to stop the harm first.

</GoDeeper>
