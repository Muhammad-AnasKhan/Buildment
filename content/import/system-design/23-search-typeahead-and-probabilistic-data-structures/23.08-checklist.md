## Your gate answer: Wazifa Jobs

This is the case study again on a different product, and the numbers are very different. Wazifa Jobs (a made-up service) is a job board. Employers post jobs, and job seekers search them. Here are the assumptions. There are 3 million active job posts, and each is about 3 KB of text. Job seekers make 40 million searches a month, and the busiest second is 4 times the average. The search box suggests job titles and city names while the seeker types, and the app sends about 5 suggestion calls for each search. There are about 150,000 different titles and cities. A new or edited post must be searchable within 5 minutes. In an interview, this is the "design a search system" question, and it is the same loop you just ran.

A weak answer copies Dalil: eight shards, a Bloom filter, a count-min sketch, a HyperLogLog. A strong answer starts from the numbers and lets them decide which boxes to draw. Compute the searches a second at peak, the suggestion calls a second at peak, and the size of the posts and the index. Chapter 23.1 showed that Dalil's index was 80 GB and its suggestion rate was 35,000 calls a second, and that decided almost everything. Does Wazifa have that problem? If the whole index fits in the memory of one machine and the peak is a few dozen searches a second, then shards are a decision you can defend leaving out, and you should say so. Say also when you would change your mind: what number would have to grow, and by how much?

Then say how a search works. Name the analyzer steps you would apply to a job title, and why queries use the same ones. Say how a new post becomes searchable and how long that takes, and what the employer who just posted sees meanwhile. For suggestions, say what structure you would use for 150,000 titles and cities, where it lives, and how it is refreshed. Say whether any of the probabilistic structures from chapter 23.4 earn a place in this design. For some products the honest answer is that none does, and that is a good answer if you can show the numbers.

Be explicit about one trade-off. Write it in the same shape each time: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would switch. A good cost line has a number or a name. For example: "a growth ceiling of about 20 times today's posts before the index no longer fits in memory," or "suggestions up to 10 minutes old." If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished.

A good order for 250 words is the order of the loop. Numbers come first, in two or three lines. Then the search path and the suggestions, which are the sketch and the deep dive. Then the trade-off, and last the failure. If you run short of words, cut the sketch, not the numbers or the trade-off.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is copying Dalil's design without checking the numbers, so shards and sketches appear that nothing in the estimate asks for. The second is forgetting the source of truth: the job database holds the posts, and the index must be rebuildable from it. The third is forgetting what the employer sees. A post that is saved but not yet searchable looks like a lost post unless the page says it is being published.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right design. Two students can choose differently and both pass, as long as each one starts from the numbers, explains why the design is that big and no bigger, names the failure the choice answers, and says when they would change their mind. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens when the index node restarts, or how you would add Arabic job titles. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, about 250 words. A recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.
