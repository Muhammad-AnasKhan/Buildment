Dalil is the search box of an online marketplace (a fictional product). A shopper types "red running shoes" and Dalil lists the matching products, best first. While she types, it also suggests searches she might mean. This module runs the design loop from chapter 1.4 on Dalil. This first chapter does the first two steps: say what Dalil must do, then turn that into numbers.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: clarify first, the way chapter 1.4 does">

Before any numbers, ask what Dalil must do and what it will not do. This is the scope the product owner agreed. All of it is a course choice, because Dalil is made up.

**Dalil does.** A shopper types words and gets a ranked list of products, ten to a page. The words are matched against the product title and description. While she types, Dalil suggests up to ten popular searches that start with what she has typed so far. When a seller adds or edits a product, the change shows up in search.

**Dalil does not, for now.** It has no personal results, no ads, no search by picture or voice, no filters by price, and no spelling correction. It supports English text only. Another language needs its own way of splitting text into words, and that is a project of its own. Each of these is a fair feature, and each would add work to every later step.

**Fast.** A results page comes back in under 300 ms at the service, as a p99 (chapter 2.4). A suggestion must be on the screen within 100 ms of the key press, also as a p99. The 100 ms includes the trip over the network, which is why it is hard.

**Fresh.** A product that a seller adds or edits is searchable within 30 seconds.

**Reliable.** Search works 99.9% of the time. Suggestions are a convenience: when they fail, the search box must still work. The product database is the source of truth. The search index can always be rebuilt from it, so losing the index is a slow day, not lost data.

**Honest about.** Results may differ for a few seconds between two shoppers, because copies of the index update at slightly different times. Suggestions are built from searches made a few minutes ago.

The line about freshness matters later. A 30 second allowance is what lets Dalil update its index in batches, and chapter 23.2 shows why batches are cheaper than updating on every save.

</GoDeeper>

## Drag the shoppers, find the load

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m23-estimate-play" />

Drag the shoppers a day, then the suggestion calls for each search. Search stays near 7,000 a second at the peak. Suggestions reach about 35,000 a second, and each must be answered in 100 ms. The ringed card is the number that shapes the design. Also watch the index size: it sets the number of shards.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the working, and what is an assumption">

Nobody gave Dalil these numbers, so each one is written down as an assumption: 200 million products, 1 KB of searchable text for each, 100 different words in each product, 4 bytes to store one word-in-one-product entry, 50 million shoppers a day, 4 searches for each shopper, 5 suggestion calls for each search, a busiest second that is 3 times the average, 5 million product changes a day, and 10 GB for one shard. The method is the one from chapter 2.3: start from people, divide by seconds, keep the unit next to every number.

| Quantity | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Raw product text | 200,000,000 x 1 KB | 200 GB |
| Searches a day | 50,000,000 x 4 | 200 million |
| Searches a second, average | 200,000,000 / 86,400 | about 2,315 |
| Searches a second, peak | 2,314.8 x 3 | about 6,944, planned as 7,000 |
| Suggestion calls a day | 200,000,000 x 5 | 1 billion |
| Suggestion calls a second, average | 1,000,000,000 / 86,400 | about 11,574 |
| Suggestion calls a second, peak | 11,574 x 3 | about 34,722, planned as 35,000 |
| Word entries in the index | 200,000,000 x 100 | 20 billion |
| Index size | 20,000,000,000 x 4 B | 80 GB |
| Shards | 80 GB / 10 GB | 8 |
| Shard calls a second, peak | 7,000 x 8 | 56,000 |
| Product changes a second | 5,000,000 / 86,400 | about 58, peak about 174 |

Now ask which numbers drive the design. The 80 GB index is not huge, but a query must read parts of it quickly, and 7,000 searches a second at the peak is too much for one machine. So the index is split into 8 shards, and every shard has several copies. Chapter 23.2 builds that.

The suggestion rate is the other driver. Thirty-five thousand calls a second is five times the search rate. If each call were a normal search, it would cost 8 shard calls, so 280,000 shard calls a second, and it would still need to finish in 100 ms including the network. That does not work. Suggestions need their own small structure, built ahead of time, that answers in memory. Chapter 23.3 builds that.

Product changes are the quiet number. Sixty-odd writes a second is small, so writing is not the problem. The 30 second freshness allowance is.

These are averages and guesses. Say them out loud and invite correction. If the app waits until the shopper has typed 2 letters, it sends 4 calls for each search instead of 5, so the suggestion rate falls by a fifth. The shape stays the same: suggestions are still several times the search rate.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say:** "Before I draw, let me fix scope and numbers. I will support ranked text search over product titles and descriptions, suggestions as you type, and edits visible within 30 seconds. I will leave out personalisation, filters and spelling correction. I will assume 50 million shoppers a day, 4 searches each, and 5 suggestion calls per search. That is about 7,000 searches a second at peak and about 35,000 suggestion calls a second. The index is about 80 GB, so 8 shards. So there are two systems: a sharded index for search, and a small precomputed structure for suggestions."

**Expect:** "Why not answer suggestions with the search index?" **Answer:** "Each suggestion would fan out to all 8 shards, so 280,000 shard calls a second at peak, and it must fit in a 100 ms budget that also pays for the network. A precomputed structure in memory answers in microseconds."

</GoDeeper>
