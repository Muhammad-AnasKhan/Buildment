DalilBot needs work waiting for about 770 sites at once, and it must never call one site too often. The structure that keeps both promises is the URL frontier. This first deep dive builds it in two layers, finds the number that sets the speed, and adds two rules: read `robots.txt`, and revisit pages by how fast they change.

## One shared queue hammers one site

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m26-one-queue-hammer" />

A plain first-in first-out queue knows nothing about hosts. If three shop.example URLs sit in a row, three fetchers take them and call the site at the same moment. A small site slows down, returns errors, and may block DalilBot's addresses. A crawler that does this is rude, and it is soon blocked.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the wait exists, and its link to chapter 6.5">

A crawler is a guest on someone else's server. Many sites are small and run on shared hosting, and they have no way to tell a crawler from a surge of visitors. Politeness is good manners, and it is also self-interest: a blocked site is a site DalilBot can no longer read.

The rule has two parts. First, only one request to a host is in flight at a time. Second, there is a minimum gap between the end of one request and the start of the next, the delay. The course uses 1 second as the default. A site can ask for more, and a site that answers slowly or with `429 Too Many Requests` [S33] should get a longer delay.

This is a rate limiter, seen from the client's side. Chapter 6.5 built a token bucket that lets requests through at a steady rate. A host with a delay of 1 second is a bucket with a rate of 1 token a second and a size of 1: no burst at all. The frontier is a set of millions of these small buckets, one per host, kept in a structure that always knows which bucket is full next.

</GoDeeper>

## Two layers: the host, then the priority

<MermaidDiagram title="The two layers of the frontier" chart={`flowchart LR
  N["New URL with a priority"] --> H{"Which host?"}
  H --> A["shop.example: queues high, normal, low; ready at 10:00:01"]
  H --> B["news.example: queues high, normal, low; ready at 10:00:00"]
  H --> C["wiki.example: queues high, normal, low; ready at 10:00:03"]
  A --> R["Pick from the ready hosts: best priority first"]
  B --> R
  C --> R
  R -->|"URL"| F["Fetcher"]
  F -->|"host ready again at now + delay"| R
`} legend="Each host has its own queues, split by priority, and a time when it may be called again. The frontier picks, from the hosts whose time has passed, the one with the best priority. After a fetch, that host's time moves forward by its delay." />

Each host gets its own set of queues, split by priority, and a clock: the time it may be called again. The frontier gives a fetcher the best URL among hosts whose clock has run out. After the fetch, that host's clock is set to now plus its delay. Hosts that are waiting are simply skipped.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the structure, its rules and its cost">

Four rules describe a correct frontier.

1. At most one URL per host is out at a time.
2. Two fetches of a host are never closer than that host's delay.
3. Among hosts that are ready, the best priority goes first. Equal priorities go to the host that has been ready longest.
4. If any host is ready, the frontier hands out a URL. It never idles on purpose.

Rule 4 is the one that is easy to break. Suppose the best URL in the whole frontier belongs to a host that is waiting. A frontier that insists on that URL leaves every fetcher idle until the host is ready. The right behaviour is to skip it and give the best URL of a ready host. Politeness beats priority. You will test exactly this in chapter 26.5.

The cost is small. Keep the hosts that have work in a heap ordered by their next allowed time. Taking the next ready host is a pop, which costs about log2 of the number of hosts: for 10 million hosts, 23 steps, at 772 fetches a second. The queues themselves can sit on disk with only the front of each in memory, since each host is read in order. Memory goes to the heap and the queue heads, not to the billions of URLs behind them.

Published crawler designs, such as the Mercator crawler, also split the frontier in two layers: queues by priority in front, and one queue per host behind them. {/* source needed: Heydon and Najork, "Mercator: A scalable, extensible web crawler", World Wide Web 2(4), 1999; not in the dossier. */} This chapter's version keeps both in one place, per host, which is the shape you will code.

</GoDeeper>

## Sites in play set the speed

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m26-host-rate-play" />

Drag the sites that have work waiting and the wait between calls to one site. Speed is the smaller of sites divided by the wait, and the download slots. At a 1 second wait, 770 pages a second needs about 770 sites. At 5 seconds it needs about 3,900. A card shows how long one big site takes.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the formula, and what a big site costs">

The crawl speed is limited by whichever is smaller.

- **Politeness limit** = sites with work waiting / delay. With 20 sites and a 1 second delay that is 20 pages a second.
- **Capacity limit** = download slots / time per fetch. The course has 800 slots, planned from chapter 26.2, and a fetch of 0.4 s with a DNS cache, so 2,000 pages a second.

| Sites waiting | Delay | Politeness limit | Capacity limit | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 1 s | 20/s | 2,000/s | 20 pages/s |
| 100 | 1 s | 100/s | 2,000/s | 100 pages/s |
| 772 | 1 s | 772/s | 2,000/s | 772 pages/s, the target |
| 3,000 | 1 s | 3,000/s | 2,000/s | 2,000 pages/s, slots are the limit |
| 1,000 | 5 s | 200/s | 2,000/s | 200 pages/s |
| 1,000 | 0.5 s | 2,000/s | 2,000/s | 2,000 pages/s |

The big-site card is the other side of the same rule. A site with 1 million pages at one request a second takes 11.6 days to finish (1,000,000 / 86,400). At 5 seconds it takes 58 days. A site with 100 million pages takes over 3 years. So DalilBot cannot read everything on a huge site, and it must choose which pages matter. That choice is priority, and it is the next beat.

Two ways to raise the speed without being rude. First, find more sites: breadth is what the limit rewards. Second, adapt the delay to each site: a site that answers in 50 ms can safely get a shorter delay than one that answers in 2 seconds. A simple rule is to set the delay to a multiple of the site's last response time, with a floor and a ceiling. Never go below what the site's `robots.txt` asks for.

Limits of the model: it treats every site as having work ready all the time, and every fetch as equal. A real crawl has sites that run dry, so the number of sites with work waiting changes through the day.

</GoDeeper>

## Important URLs go first

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m26-priority-pick" />

Not every URL is worth the same. A news front page matters more than one page of an old forum. Each URL carries a priority. Among sites that are ready, the fetcher takes the best URL first. A URL on a site that is still waiting is skipped, so the fetcher never sits idle.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where priority comes from, and starvation">

Priority is a score the crawler computes when it adds or reschedules a URL. Useful signals:

- **Importance.** How many other pages link to it, and how many people look for it. A page with many links from good sites is read first.
- **Change rate.** A page that changes every few hours is worth visiting more often than one that never changes (see the last beat).
- **Staleness.** The longer since the last visit, the higher the priority, so no page is forgotten.
- **New or known.** A first visit to an unseen URL on a well-known site may rank above a revisit of a quiet page.

The course uses three levels: high, normal and low. More levels mean more queues per host and a harder choice about which level a URL belongs to, with little gain at this scale.

The danger is starvation. If high-priority work never ends, low-priority URLs wait for ever. The usual fix is aging: raise a URL's priority the longer it waits, for example from low to normal after 24 hours. Aging is a simple rule that makes the guarantee "everything is fetched eventually" true.

Priority also decides what a big site gets. With the 1 million page site from the last beat, DalilBot sets a budget of pages for it, and spends the budget on the highest priority URLs first.

</GoDeeper>

## Ask robots.txt, then obey it

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m26-robots-check" />

Before it fetches from a host, DalilBot reads that host's `robots.txt`: a plain file of rules at the top of the site. It keeps the rules for up to a day. A blocked path is never requested. A missing file means everything is allowed. A server error means wait, and crawl nothing from that site in the meantime.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading the rules, the status codes and the limits">

The Robots Exclusion Protocol is a standard, RFC 9309. {/* source needed: RFC 9309 is not in the dossier; every statement in this fold comes from it and should be checked at G5. */} The file lives at `/robots.txt` of each site, and each host and scheme and port has its own. It is made of groups that start with `User-agent:` lines and hold `Allow:` and `Disallow:` rules. The rule with the longest matching path wins. If an allow and a disallow match equally, allow wins.

```js
// Partial: reads one group of rules (the group that matches our bot) and ignores wildcards.
function parseRules(text) {
  const rules = [];
  for (const line of text.split("\n")) {
    const m = line.split("#")[0].match(/^\s*(allow|disallow)\s*:\s*(\S*)\s*$/i);
    if (m && m[2] !== "") rules.push({ allow: m[1].toLowerCase() === "allow", path: m[2] });
  }
  return rules;
}
// The longest matching rule wins. If an Allow and a Disallow match equally, Allow wins.
function isAllowed(rules, path) {
  let best = null;
  for (const r of rules) {
    if (!path.startsWith(r.path)) continue;
    if (!best || r.path.length > best.path.length || (r.path.length === best.path.length && r.allow)) best = r;
  }
  return best ? best.allow : true;
}
```

Run on the rules `Disallow: /private/`, `Allow: /private/press/` and `Disallow: /search`, it prints:

```text
/shop/shoes                allowed
/private/files/a.pdf       blocked
/private/press/2026.html   allowed
/search?q=tea              blocked
```

What the status code of the `robots.txt` fetch means, per the standard:

| Answer | Meaning | What DalilBot does |
|---|---|---|
| 200 with a file | Rules found | Parse and cache them |
| 404 or another 4xx | No rules (unavailable) | Treat every path as allowed |
| 5xx or no connection | The site is unreachable | Assume everything is disallowed; try again later |

The crawler should not reuse a cached copy for more than 24 hours, unless the file cannot be reached. It must parse at least the first 500 KiB of the file. A line `Crawl-delay:` is common, but it is not part of the standard, and some crawlers ignore it. DalilBot reads it as a request for a longer delay and uses the larger of it and its own.

Two limits to teach. `robots.txt` is a request, not a lock. A page the file blocks is still public to anyone who has its address, so it is not a way to keep a secret, and a site that needs privacy must use a login. And the rules are cached with the host in the HOST record from chapter 26.2, so reading them costs one fetch a day for each site, not one per page.

</GoDeeper>

## When to visit a page again

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m26-recrawl-play" />

Pages change at different speeds. Pick a kind of page, then drag how often DalilBot revisits it. Fresh is the share of time the saved copy matches the live page. Wasted is the share of visits that find nothing new. Aim to match the visit gap to the page's own pace.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the freshness maths, a budget, and an adaptive rule">

Model a page as changing at random, on average `c` times a day. A visit every `T` days finds the saved copy fresh for a share of the time equal to (1 - e^(-cT)) / (cT), and finds nothing new on a share of visits equal to e^(-cT). {/* source needed: this is the usual Poisson change model for web pages (Cho and Garcia-Molina, "Estimating frequency of change", ACM TOIT 2003); the course derived the two formulas from it and checked them by script. */} Both depend only on the product `cT`. When `cT` is small, most visits find nothing. When it is large, changes pile up unseen. When `cT` is near 1, the page is fresh about 63% of the time and about 37% of visits are wasted.

The course's kinds of page: a news front page (changes 6 times a day), a product page (once a week) and an old article (once in 90 days).

| Page kind | Visit every | Fresh | Wasted |
|---|---|---|---|
| News front page | 1 hour | 88% | 78% |
| News front page | 4 hours | 63% | 37% |
| News front page | 1 day | 17% | 0% |
| Product page | 1 day | 93% | 87% |
| Product page | 7 days | 63% | 37% |
| Old article | 7 days | 96% | 93% |
| Old article | 90 days | 63% | 37% |

Now spend a budget. Take 1 billion pages and 2 billion fetches a month (66.7 million a day): 2% are news-like (20 million), 18% product-like (180 million) and 80% old articles (800 million). A single visit gap for all, 15 days, uses the whole budget. A gap by kind uses about the same money.

| Policy | News fresh | Product fresh | Old fresh | Fetches a day |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Every page every 15 days | 1% | 41% | 92% | 66.7 million |
| News daily, products weekly, old every 45 days | 17% | 63% | 79% | 63.5 million |

The second policy gives up 13 points of freshness on old articles, where nobody notices, to gain 16 points on news and 22 on products. These page mixes are course numbers.

The crawler does not know each page's pace in advance. It learns it from fingerprints (chapter 26.4): if the content hash changed since the last visit, the page moved; if not, it did not. A simple rule halves the wait after a change and doubles it after none, within a floor and a ceiling.

```js
// Halve the wait when the page changed, double it when it did not. Keep it inside [min, max] days.
function nextInterval(days, changed, { min = 1 / 24, max = 90 } = {}) {
  return Math.min(max, Math.max(min, changed ? days / 2 : days * 2));
}
```

Run on a fake page that changes every 4 days, starting with a 1 day wait, the first ten visits print:

```text
visit 1 at day 1: same     next wait 2 days
visit 2 at day 3: same     next wait 4 days
visit 3 at day 7: changed  next wait 2 days
visit 4 at day 9: changed  next wait 1 days
visit 5 at day 10: same     next wait 2 days
visit 6 at day 12: changed  next wait 1 days
visit 7 at day 13: same     next wait 2 days
visit 8 at day 15: same     next wait 4 days
visit 9 at day 19: changed  next wait 2 days
visit 10 at day 21: changed  next wait 1 days
```

The wait bounces between 1 and 4 days around the page's own pace of 4 days. That is good enough for a first version, and a real crawler would smooth it with several past visits.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say:** "The frontier is two layers. Each host has its own queues split by priority, plus a time when it may be called again. The frontier hands a fetcher the best URL among hosts whose time has passed, and after the fetch sets that host's time to now plus the delay, one second by default or the site's own request. The speed limit is sites divided by delay, so at 770 pages a second and a one second delay I need work waiting for at least 770 sites. Before I fetch from a host I read its robots.txt and cache it for a day. I set revisit gaps by how often a page changed, halving the gap after a change and doubling it after none."

**Expect:** "What if one site has 100 million pages?" **Answer:** "At one request a second that is over three years, so I cannot read it all. I give the site a page budget, spend it by priority, use its sitemap if it has one, and ask the owner if we may crawl faster."

</GoDeeper>
