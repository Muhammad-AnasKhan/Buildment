## Your gate answer: Souq Watch

This is the case study again on a different product. Souq Watch (a made-up service) compares prices for online shops. Its crawler visits 150,000 shops and keeps about 40 million product pages up to date. Prices change, so every product page should be fetched again once a day. Here are the assumptions. A product page is about 80 KB of HTML. Most shops are small, about 270 product pages each on average. A few hundred are huge, and the largest has 2 million product pages. Souq Watch obeys `robots.txt` and waits 1 second between requests to one shop unless a shop asks for more. In an interview, this is the "design a web crawler" question with a freshness target added, and it is the same loop you just ran.

A weak answer lists boxes: a queue, some fetchers, a database, a cache, and stops. A strong answer starts from the numbers and lets them decide which boxes matter. Compute the pages a second for a daily refresh of 40 million pages, the bandwidth, and the number of shops that must have work waiting at the same moment. Then do the check that chapter 26.3 taught and that most answers miss: for each kind of shop, how long does one full refresh take at one request a second? A small shop with 270 pages takes under 5 minutes. A shop with 86,400 pages takes exactly a day, so it is the largest shop you can refresh daily. The shop with 2 million pages would take 23 days. That single number changes the design, because for those shops a daily refresh of every page is impossible, whatever machines you buy.

Then say what you do about it. You have choices, and each one has a price. You can give big shops a longer cycle. You can refresh the pages people look at most each day, using priority, and the rest weekly. You can read a shop's sitemap or product feed if it has one, and only fetch pages whose date changed. You can ask a big shop in writing for a faster allowed rate. Pick one, and say what it costs in stale prices or in goodwill.

Also cover the rest of the loop briefly. Say how the frontier is built and split, so that one worker owns each shop. Say how you stop the same product from being stored many times, since shops show one product under many sort and filter addresses. Say how you stop a shop with endless filter combinations from swallowing the crawl. Say what happens when one frontier worker fails.

Be explicit about one trade-off. Write it in the same shape each time: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would switch. A good cost line has a number or a name. For example: "prices on the long tail of a big shop up to 7 days old, which a shopper may notice," or "one more key-value lookup for every link, about 0.5 ms." If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished.

A good order for 250 words is the order of the loop. Numbers come first, in two or three lines. Then the frontier, the big-shop plan, duplicates and traps, which are the sketch and the deep dive. Then the trade-off, and last the failure. If you run short of words, cut the sketch, not the numbers or the trade-off.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is to answer "add more fetchers" to the big-shop problem. Machines do not help when the limit is one request a second to one shop. The second is to forget that two addresses can show one page, so the same product is fetched and stored again and again. The third is to use a fixed page budget for every shop, which would cut off a big honest shop at the same number as a trap.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right design. Two students can choose differently and both pass, as long as each one starts from the numbers, finds the big-shop limit, names the failure the choice answers, and says when they would change their mind. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens when a shop starts returning errors, or how you would learn which pages people look at most. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, about 250 words. A recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.

Design: the frontier has a queue per shop, split by priority, and a ready time. A hash of the host picks the worker, so one worker owns each shop and keeps its wait. Fetchers are stateless and take URLs on a lease. I clean product URLs, dropping sort and session parameters, check a Bloom filter for new URLs, and fingerprint the page text so one product under two addresses is stored once. Each shop gets a page budget and a depth limit against endless filters. Trade-off: for big shops I refresh the 86,000 most viewed pages daily and the rest weekly, by priority. It buys fresh prices where shoppers look. It costs prices up to 7 days old on the long tail. I would switch if a big shop gives a product feed or allows a faster rate. Failure: if a frontier worker dies, I rebuild its queues from the URL table and each shop waits one delay before its first fetch, so no shop gets two requests together."
