In 15.1 the signup waited for an email, and the retry sent it twice. The fix for the wait is old and simple: stop doing the slow work inside the request. Put a note about it in a line, and let someone else do it.

## Put a line between request and work

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m15-queue-decouples" />

The API saves Layla's account, drops an email job in a queue and answers in 40 ms. A worker takes the job from the queue and sends the email. The slow call is still slow, but now no customer is waiting for it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the parts of a queue, and what decoupling means">

A <GlossaryTerm term="queue" definition="A place where one program puts messages and another program takes them out later. The messages wait in order until a worker is ready." /> has three parts. The producer puts messages in. The broker is the system that holds them, such as Amazon SQS, RabbitMQ or Valkey. The consumer takes them out and does the work. In the picture the API is the producer and the mailer is the consumer.

The queue separates the two sides in three ways. In time: the producer does not wait for the consumer. In speed: each side works at its own pace. In failure: if the mailer is down for ten minutes, signups still work, and the jobs wait in the queue until the mailer returns. SQS describes this as decoupling the parts of a distributed application, so they do not need to be available at the same time [S83].

What you give up is the answer. Before, the API knew whether the email was sent. Now it only knows the job was accepted. If the email fails, nobody told Layla. Later beats and the next chapters deal with that: retries, dead letter queues and idempotent consumers.

</GoDeeper>

## A spike waits instead of crashing

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m15-spike-absorbed" />

Six signups arrive in one second, but the mailer sends one email per second. The queue holds the other five, and the count falls to zero. Nothing is dropped. A queue turns a short spike into a short wait.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: buffering and smoothing">

Traffic is not even. A flash sale, a school term starting or a push notification can send ten times the usual load for a minute. Without a queue, you must size the whole system for that minute, and pay for it all day. With a queue, you size the workers for the average load plus some room, and the queue absorbs the difference. This is the same idea as the half-idle server with a line in chapter 2.5: bursts make a line even when the average is fine.

The queue only works while the spike is short. It borrows from the future: jobs wait now and are paid for by spare capacity later. If there is no spare capacity later, the loan is never repaid. The next beat shows exactly when that happens.

</GoDeeper>

## Drag the burst, watch the line

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m15-backlog-play" />

Drag the burst rate and pick the number of workers. While the burst lasts, the line grows by arrivals minus capacity. After it, the line shrinks only by the spare capacity. With too few workers, the line never shrinks. With a limit on the queue, jobs beyond the limit are refused.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the model behind the picture">

The picture uses a simple model with no randomness. Each worker finishes 40 jobs per second (25 ms per job), so 5 workers have a capacity of 200 per second. Normal arrivals are 150 per second. During a 60 second burst, arrivals rise to the number you choose.

While arrivals are above capacity, the backlog grows by (arrivals minus capacity) per second. With a burst of 300, that is 100 per second, so after 60 seconds the backlog is 6,000. The last job in line waits backlog divided by capacity: 6,000 / 200 = 30 seconds. After the burst, the backlog shrinks by (capacity minus normal arrivals) per second: 50. So it takes 6,000 / 50 = 120 seconds to empty. If capacity is at or below the normal arrivals, the backlog never empties. With 3 workers (capacity 120) and 150 arriving, it grows by 30 per second for ever.

Two warnings. First, real arrivals are random, so lines form even when average capacity is above average arrivals (chapter 2.5). Second, the delay is the real cost of a queue: with a backlog of 6,000 and a capacity of 200, an email sent now leaves in 30 seconds. For a welcome email that is fine. For a "your code is 4821" message it is not. Watch the age of the oldest message, not only the count.

</GoDeeper>

## Size the workers with Little's law

<MermaidDiagram title="How many workers does the mailer need?" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["150 jobs per second arrive"] -->|"x 0.04 s per email"| B["L = 6 workers busy on average"]
  B --> C{"How many workers do we run?"}
  C -->|"5 workers"| D["Fewer than 6: the line grows without end"]
  C -->|"6 workers"| E["100% busy: every burst leaves a long line"]
  C -->|"9 workers"| F["About 67% busy: room for bursts"]
`} legend="Little's law gives the workers busy on average. Run clearly more than that, or the line never stays short." />

Little's law, from chapter 2.5, says the number busy equals the arrival rate times the time each job takes. At 150 jobs per second and 40 ms each, 6 workers are busy on average. Five can never keep up. Six sit at the knee. Nine leave room.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: choosing a target utilisation">

The calculation is 150 per second times 0.04 seconds, which is 6. Utilisation is that number divided by the workers you run. With 9 workers it is 6 / 9, about 67%, which sits below the knee of the curve in chapter 2.5. With 6 it is 100%, and in the M/M/1 model the wait grows without limit as utilisation approaches 100% [S6, S7].

There is no single right target. A queue exists to absorb bursts, so it can tolerate a higher utilisation than a user-facing server, where every extra millisecond is felt. But the higher you go, the longer a burst takes to clear. Pick a target from the delay you can accept: if the oldest message may wait 30 seconds, measure how long a typical burst of yours takes to drain at each worker count, then choose the smallest count that stays inside 30 seconds.

Autoscaling can add workers when the backlog grows. It needs time to start them, so keep a floor that covers the normal load. The signal to scale on is the age of the oldest message, or the backlog divided by the capacity, not the CPU of the workers.

</GoDeeper>

## A full queue tells the sender to wait

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m15-full-queue" />

A queue with no limit can hide a problem until memory runs out. A queue with a limit says "full" when it is full. The sender then has a choice: wait, slow down, drop the job or tell its own caller to try later. Nothing is lost silently.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: backpressure in Node.js and in brokers">

<GlossaryTerm term="Backpressure" definition="A signal from a slow receiver to a fast sender that says: slow down. It stops a buffer from growing without limit." /> exists inside a single Node.js program too. A writable stream has a buffer. Its `write()` method returns `false` when the buffer is past the `highWaterMark` (16 KB by default, or 16 objects in object mode). A good producer stops writing, waits for the `drain` event and then goes on. Piping handles this for you: `pipe()` pauses the source when the destination is full [S88].

```js
// Partial: the same idea as a queue limit, inside one Node.js program.
import { createWriteStream } from "node:fs";
import { once } from "node:events";

const out = createWriteStream("emails.log");
for (let i = 0; i < 1_000_000; i++) {
  const ok = out.write(`job ${i}\n`);
  if (!ok) await once(out, "drain"); // the buffer is full: wait for it to empty
}
out.end();
```

Brokers do the same on a larger scale. A bounded queue refuses new messages, a producer slows when the broker is slow to acknowledge, and a service in front of a queue can answer 503 or 429 to its own callers. The choice depends on the job. An email can wait. A payment should probably fail fast so that the customer sees it. A metrics event can be dropped. State your choice for each kind of job, because "we never drop anything" and "we never make anyone wait" cannot both be true when the workers fall behind.

</GoDeeper>

## Not everything belongs in a queue

<MermaidDiagram title="Should this work go through a queue?" chart={`flowchart TD
  Q1{"Does the user need the result to continue?"} -->|"Yes: a price, a login"| S["Do it in the request"]
  Q1 -->|"No: an email, a thumbnail"| Q2{"Is it fine if it finishes seconds or minutes later?"}
  Q2 -->|"No"| S
  Q2 -->|"Yes"| Q3{"Can the job safely run twice?"}
  Q3 -->|"Yes"| Q["Put it in a queue"]
  Q3 -->|"No"| R["Make it safe to repeat first (14.5), then queue it"]
`} legend="Three questions decide. The answer to the third is the one teams forget." />

A queue adds a hop, a delay and a new thing to run. Use it when the user does not need the result now, a short delay is fine, and a repeated job does no harm. The last question is the one that bites: a queue that delivers at least once will sometimes deliver twice.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what a queue costs">

Every queue adds four costs. There is another system to run, monitor and pay for. The answer is no longer final: the API says "accepted", not "done", so the user interface must say "we will email you". Failures move: a job can fail long after the request that created it, so you need retries and a place for jobs that never succeed (chapter 15.5). And the order and number of deliveries are no longer obvious, which chapters 15.3 and 15.4 take apart.

A useful test is to ask what the user sees if the job is delayed by ten minutes. If the answer is "nothing", queue it. If the answer is "an angry phone call", keep it in the request, or make the delay visible and honest.

</GoDeeper>
