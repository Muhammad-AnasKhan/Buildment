## Your gate answer: order emails and receipts

This is the question that started the module. Zaytoun Market (a made-up shop) sends a confirmation email and saves a receipt for every order. Today the order request does both, which makes it slow and sometimes sends the email twice. Design the async flow. In an interview this is the "send emails and notifications" question that sits inside many bigger designs. In a real team it is the first thing someone asks for after the first outage of the email provider.

A weak answer says "use a queue" and stops. A strong answer is short and walks the path of one order. It starts with what must be true when the request returns: the order is saved, and the intent to send an email and a receipt is saved with it, for example in an outbox row in the same transaction (chapter 14.4). Then it names how the event gets to the workers, and why you chose a queue per subscriber or a log with a consumer group for each (chapter 15.3). It says what happens when a worker fails: how many tries, which backoff, what jitter, and where the message goes after the last try (chapter 15.5). It says how a repeat is made harmless: the event id and where it is remembered (chapter 14.5), and how long. And it says one number or one signal you would watch: the age of the oldest message, the size of the dead letter queue, or the lag of each subscriber.

Be explicit about two choices. First, when does the worker ack: before or after sending, and what does that mean for a crash? Second, does order matter here? An email about an order and a receipt for it are independent, so a retry queue is fine. If a later event for the same order could depend on an earlier one, say which key keeps them together.

A good cost line is short and has a unit or a name. For example: "an outbox row per order, a relay that may publish twice, and a dedupe table per subscriber that I keep for 7 days." Or: "a minute of delay on a receipt when the receipts worker is behind, and a page that says 'your receipt is coming'." If your line says "some complexity", it is not finished.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is saying a queue gives exactly-once: it gives at least once, and the exactly-once effect comes from an idempotent consumer. The second is retrying without a limit or without a dead letter queue, which lets one bad address block the line. The third is forgetting the user: say what the customer sees while the email is not yet sent.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right design.
