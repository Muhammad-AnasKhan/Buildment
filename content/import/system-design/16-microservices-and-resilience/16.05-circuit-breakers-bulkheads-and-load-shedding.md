A time limit and careful retries (16.4) keep each call short. They do not stop Checkout from making thousands of doomed calls to a Picks that has been failing for a minute, and they do not stop a slow Picks from using up the workers that payments need. Three more tools handle that.

## Closed, open, half-open

<MermaidDiagram title="The three states of a circuit breaker" chart={`stateDiagram-v2
  state "Half-open" as HalfOpen
  [*] --> Closed
  Closed --> Open: 5 failures in a row
  Open --> HalfOpen: after 10 seconds
  HalfOpen --> Closed: the probe call works
  HalfOpen --> Open: the probe call fails
`} legend="Closed: calls go through. Open: calls are refused at once. Half-open: one probe call decides." />

A circuit breaker watches the calls to one dependency. While it is closed, calls go through. After 5 failures in a row it opens, and calls are refused at once, with no wait and no load on the dependency. After a pause it half-opens and lets one probe call through. If the probe works, the breaker closes. If it fails, the breaker opens again.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: counting failures, and what the breaker returns">

Martin Fowler describes the breaker as a wrapper that trips when failures reach a threshold, and that stops cascading failures from resource exhaustion [S92]. The name comes from the electrical fuse box: when the current is too high, the switch opens to protect the house.

Design choices matter. **How to count:** the picture uses failures in a row, which is simple and exact (the code in 16.7 does this). Many libraries instead use the share of failures in a window of recent calls, say 50% of the last 20, which copes better with a mix of good and bad calls. **What counts as a failure:** timeouts, connection errors and 5xx answers, but not a 404 or a 400, which are the caller's mistakes. **How long to stay open:** long enough for the dependency to recover, short enough to notice recovery; 10 seconds is a common start. **What to return while open:** an error, or better, a fallback (16.6).

Keep one breaker for each dependency, and often for each host, so one bad machine does not block calls to its healthy neighbours. Each process keeps its own breaker, so a fleet of 20 Checkout servers has 20 breakers. That is fine: each one learns within a few calls. A breaker with too low a threshold opens on bad luck; one that never gets traffic never learns. {/* source needed: library names (Resilience4j, Polly) and Envoy outlier detection as ready-made breakers; not in the dossier. */}

</GoDeeper>

## Tap Picks, watch the breaker

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m16-breaker-play" />

Tap Picks to break it, and tap again to fix it. At 10 calls a second, a 30-second outage sends only 9 calls to Picks instead of 300. The breaker opens after the fifth failure, and every 10 seconds it sends one probe. When Picks is fixed at 35 seconds, the breaker still waits until its next probe at 36.3 seconds.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers in the picture">

The model is exact, not random. Checkout makes 10 calls a second to Picks, and a call to a broken Picks fails after a 300 ms time limit. The breaker needs 5 failures in a row and stays open for 10 seconds.

Picks breaks at 5.0 seconds. The calls at 5.0, 5.1 up to 5.6 are already out when the fifth failure arrives at 5.7, so 7 calls reach Picks and the breaker opens. At 15.7 it half-opens and the next call is the probe. It fails at 16.0, and the breaker opens again until 26.0. The second probe fails too, until 36.3. In the outage, from 5.0 to 35.0, 9 calls reached Picks: 7 plus 2 probes. Without a breaker, all 300 would have gone, and each would have held a worker for 300 ms.

Picks is fixed at 35.0. The next probe is at 36.3, and it works, so the breaker closes at 36.34. The 1.3 seconds between the fix and the close is the cost: the page shows no picks for that long although Picks is ready. A shorter open time shortens that gap and sends more probes into a dependency that is still hurt. This is the same kind of trade as a short or long timeout in chapter 12.2.

</GoDeeper>

## One shared pool, one slow friend

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m16-shared-pool" />

Home pages and payments share one pool of workers. Home pages wait on a slow Picks and hold every worker, so a payment request that needs nothing from Picks still waits for a free worker. We saw this in 16.1. The cure is to give each kind of work its own pool, so one slow friend can only fill its own.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: bulkheads">

The bulkhead pattern takes its name from the walls inside a ship's hull. A hole floods one compartment, not the whole ship. In software it means separate pools of a resource, such as workers, connections or queue slots, for each dependency or each kind of caller, so that a failure cannot use up what everything shares [S93].

You can put the wall in different places. Inside one process, it is a separate semaphore or thread pool for each dependency (the challenge uses a small `Pool` class). Between processes, it is a separate service instance for a critical caller. Between customers, it is a pool for each large customer, so one noisy tenant cannot starve the rest.

A bulkhead does not make Picks faster. It limits the damage to the home page, which depends on Picks anyway.

</GoDeeper>

## Drag the wall between the pools

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m16-bulkhead-pools" />

Checkout has 50 workers. Payments needs 20 requests a second at 0.1 s each. The home page needs 80 a second and Picks holds each for 3 s. With one pool, only about 21% of payments get a worker. Drag 10 workers into a payment pool and 100% of payments are served. The home page serves only 17%, but it was the slow one.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: sizing a bulkhead, and its price">

The pool size for a kind of work comes from Little's law (chapter 2.5): workers busy equals arrival rate times time held. Payments needs 20 x 0.1 = 2 workers, so 10 is five times that, with room for bursts. With a healthy Picks, the home page needs 80 x 0.1 = 8 workers. With a slow Picks, it would need 80 x 3 = 240, far more than 50, and it can only ever serve what its pool allows: 40 workers / 3 s = 13 a second, 17% of 80.

With one shared pool and a slow Picks, the total work held per request in the mix is 20 x 0.1 + 80 x 3 = 242 worker-seconds each second, and the pool gives 50. So only 50 / 242 = 0.21 of every kind of request gets served, payments included. The wall changes that to 100% for payments.

The price of a wall is idle capacity. Workers kept for payments cannot help the home page when payments is quiet. So size the protected pool for the critical work plus headroom, and give the rest to the shared pool. In the challenge you measure this: payments served 41% with one pool and 100% with a wall.

**In AWS.** In Lambda, reserved concurrency for a function works as a bulkhead. {/* source needed: AWS Lambda reserved concurrency; not in the dossier. */}

</GoDeeper>

## Refuse early, serve well

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m16-shed-load" />

150 requests a second arrive at a server that can do 100. If every request joins one line, the line grows by 50 a second and passes 100 after 2 seconds. Then every answer is late and clients have given up. Load shedding refuses the extra 50 at once with a quick "busy" answer, so the other 100 are served on time.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how real systems shed load">

When demand is above capacity, something has to give. Queueing everyone only moves the failure from "no" to "too late". The Google SRE book lists load shedding as a core defence against cascading failures: drop some traffic so the rest is served well [S91]. Stripe describes four limiters in front of its API: a request rate limiter, a concurrent request limiter, a fleet usage load shedder and a worker utilisation load shedder [S32]. The shedders act only when the system is busy, and drop lower-priority traffic first.

Practical choices: **Where.** Refuse as early as possible, before the expensive work, ideally at the gateway (chapter 6.6). **What.** Drop the least important traffic first: background jobs before checkouts, anonymous browsing before paying customers. **How long the line may be.** Set the longest queue from the wait you accept: at 100 a second and 1 second of patience, a queue longer than about 100 is useless. **What to say.** A 503 for "the server is busy" and a 429 for "you are over your own limit" [S33]. Send a `Retry-After` header so well-behaved clients wait.

Shedding goes together with the retry budget of 16.4. A client that gets a 503 and retries at once makes the overload worse, so clients must back off.

**In AWS.** Amazon API Gateway throttles with a token bucket and answers 429 when a client goes over its rate or burst [S30].

</GoDeeper>

## Which tool for which failure

<MermaidDiagram title="Choosing a protection" chart={`flowchart TD
  P["A dependency is slow or failing"] --> A{"Does a single call wait too long?"}
  A -->|yes| T["Time limit (16.4)"]
  P --> B{"Does it keep failing, call after call?"}
  B -->|yes| CB["Circuit breaker"]
  P --> C{"Does one dependency use up workers that others need?"}
  C -->|yes| BH["Bulkhead: a separate pool"]
  P --> D{"Is total demand above capacity?"}
  D -->|yes| LS["Load shedding: refuse the extra"]
`} legend="Each tool answers one failure. They are used together, from the call outward." />

Each tool answers one problem, and they work together. The time limit shortens each call. The breaker stops calls to a dependency that keeps failing. The bulkhead stops a slow dependency from taking all the workers. Load shedding protects the whole service when demand is above what it can do. None of them replaces the others.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the layers, and their order">

Think of the protections as layers around one call, from the inside: the time limit on the call itself, a retry with a budget around that, a breaker around the retries, a bulkhead that holds the worker while all of it happens, and at the very edge, load shedding that decides whether the request starts at all. Challenge 15 builds the first four and measures each.

None of these tools fixes the dependency. They make your service survive while someone else repairs theirs. What the customer sees while the breaker is open or the pool is full is chapter 16.6: fallbacks and graceful degradation.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

Say: "A timeout bounds each call. A circuit breaker stops calls to a dependency that keeps failing: it opens after a threshold, half-opens after a wait and lets one probe through. A bulkhead gives each dependency its own pool so a slow one cannot starve the rest. Load shedding refuses the excess when demand is above capacity, so the rest is served on time."

An interviewer may ask: "What does the user see when the breaker is open?" Answer with a fallback: a cached copy, a default, or the feature hidden, chosen ahead of time for each dependency.

</GoDeeper>
