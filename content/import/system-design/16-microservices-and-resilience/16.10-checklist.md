## Your gate answer: split or not?

This is the question that started the module. A product grows. The team has more people, releases start to collide, and someone says "let's split it into microservices". In an interview, this comes up as soon as a design has more than one team or more than one scale need. In a real team, it comes up the first time a deploy is blocked for a week.

Here is the case. Rihla is a made-up travel booking app: one codebase and one database, built by 12 engineers, with 40 expected next year. It has four parts: search, bookings, payments and notifications. Search needs ten times more machines than the others at peak and changes every week. Bookings and payments change together, and a bug in either blocks everyone's deploy about three times a month. Notifications send emails after a booking. The boundary between bookings and payments is still being argued.

Write your decision in about 150 words or more. A weak answer says "split everything into services because it scales" or "keep it as it is because it works". You know from this module why both skip the evidence. A strong answer is built from the tools of the module, in order.

First, say which shape you choose for each part and why. Use the factors: team size, release collisions and how stable the boundaries are. You may decide that search leaves first because it has its own scaling and release pace, that bookings and payments stay together in one modular monolith until the boundary is clear, and that notifications become a message consumer. Say what evidence would change your mind. A decision with no evidence that could overturn it is a belief.

Second, say how the parts talk, and which calls wait and which send messages. The customer must know whether they paid, so that call waits. A confirmation email does not need to finish before the booking, so it is a message saved in the same transaction as the booking, with an idempotent consumer. Name the data each service owns, and how search gets booking data without reading its tables.

Third, name the protections for the calls that remain. Which call gets a time limit, and how did you choose the number? Which call may retry, at which layer, with what budget? Where does a breaker sit, and what does it return while open? Which pool gets a wall so that a slow search cannot starve payments? A design that says "retries and circuit breakers" without saying where is not finished.

Fourth, name what the customer sees when search is down: the fallback. Use a number from your own challenge 15 results to back one choice. For example, you can say that a hanging Picks received 328 calls with only a time limit and 18 with a breaker, or that a shared pool served 41% of payments and a wall served 100%. A number from your own run is the best evidence you have.

Fifth, name one cost or gap that your decision does not remove. For example, splitting search adds a network call that can fail and a second thing to deploy and watch. Or the wall between pools wastes some idle workers. A design that claims to have no cost is not finished.

A good cost line is short and has a unit. For a breaker it can read: "while Picks was healthy again, 13% of pages still went out without picks for up to 2 seconds." For a wall it can read: "payments always have 8 workers, and the home page loses them when payments is quiet." Compare your own lines with these. If a line says "some overhead" or "a bit more complexity", it is not finished.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right choice. Two students can choose different splits and both pass, as long as each one uses the evidence, names how the parts talk, names where each protection sits, and says what the design still costs. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what you would do if the boundary between bookings and payments turned out wrong, or how you would test the fallback for search. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required; a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.
