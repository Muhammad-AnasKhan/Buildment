Tawseel's team decides to split out some parts. The hard question is not how to run a service. It is where to draw the lines, and how the parts then talk to each other. A bad line makes every change touch three services. A bad call style makes one slow service stop the others.

## Draw lines around what changes together

<MermaidDiagram title="Tawseel's services and the data each one owns" chart={`flowchart LR
  O["Orders service"] --- OD[("Orders data")]
  P["Payments service"] --- PD[("Payments data")]
  C["Catalog service"] --- CD[("Catalog data")]
  D["Delivery service"] --- DD[("Delivery data")]
  N["Notifications service"] --- ND[("Message log")]
`} legend="Each service owns one set of data. No arrow joins a service to another service's data." />

A good line puts things that change together on one side. Test a line with three questions. Can one team change this service without asking another team? Does it own its own data? Is its public interface small? If the answer to any is no, the line is probably in the wrong place.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to find the lines">

Start from the business, not the code. Ask which words mean different things to different teams. To Orders, a "customer" is a delivery address and a basket. To Payments, a "customer" is a card and a billing name. Two meanings of one word is a hint that there are two parts, each with its own data and rules. This idea is called a bounded context. {/* source needed: bounded context from Eric Evans, Domain-Driven Design (2003); not in the dossier. */}

Then check each candidate with the three questions. A **change test** helps: look at the last 20 changes your team made. If most of them touch two candidate services at once, those two belong together. A **data test** also helps: if two services need the same table to do their job, either they are one service or one of them should ask the other.

Lines are also a team matter. A service that no team owns will decay. A common rule is one team per service, and no team owning so many services that it cannot run them. Three or four larger services owned by three or four teams is often better than twenty tiny ones.

Two more things show up once there are services. Callers must find each other: inside the network by name through service discovery (chapter 12.9), and from outside through a gateway (chapter 6.6). Both are covered earlier, and both come back in this chapter's call chains.

</GoDeeper>

## Two services, one table: a hidden link

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m16-shared-table" />

Reports reads the Orders table directly, because it was easy. The Orders team renames a column in its own service, and Reports breaks. Nothing in the code showed the link. A shared table is a contract that nobody wrote down, so the rule is simple: one service owns the data, and all others ask it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: one owner, and how others get the data">

When every service owns its data, the owner can change how it stores things without asking anyone. The price is that other services cannot join across tables any more. {/* source needed: database per service (microservices.io pattern, Chris Richardson); not in the dossier. */}

There are three honest ways for Reports to get order data.

- **Ask at request time.** Reports calls the Orders interface. The data is fresh, but Reports now depends on Orders being up and fast, and it pays a network call for each lookup.
- **Keep a copy.** Orders publishes an event each time an order changes, and Reports keeps its own table built from those events. Reports works when Orders is down, and the data is a little late. The copy belongs to Reports, and the owner of the truth is still Orders.
- **Join at the edge.** A gateway or a backend for each client (chapter 6.6) calls the services it needs and puts the answers together for one screen.

Each way trades freshness, independence and cost. A report that can be five minutes late should keep a copy. A balance that must be exact should ask the owner.

</GoDeeper>

## Wait for the answer, or send a message

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m16-order-path" />

Tap each call to switch it between "wait for the answer" and "send a message". Then tap Notifications to make it fail. Waiting for all three takes 150 ms, and a failed Notifications fails the order. Sending the email as a message cuts the wait to 110 ms and the order goes through. But do not send Payments as a message: the customer would not know if they paid.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: sync and async, and what each costs">

A **synchronous** call waits: Checkout sends the request and does nothing else until the answer comes. It is simple to reason about, and the answer tells the caller whether it worked. Its cost is coupling in time: both services must be up at once, and the caller's time and availability now include the callee's. The numbers in the picture are course numbers: Stock takes 30 ms, Payments 80 ms and Notifications 40 ms, so three waits in a row cost 150 ms. If each service is available 99.9% of the time, three in a row give 0.999 to the power 3, about 99.7%. Two give about 99.8%.

An **asynchronous** call sends a message to a queue and carries on. A worker in the other service takes it later. The caller does not need the callee to be up, and the queue absorbs a burst. The costs are that the caller does not learn the result, messages can arrive twice (queues such as SQS deliver at least once, so consumers must be idempotent [S83]), and an order of events is harder to follow. A message that keeps failing should move to a dead-letter queue after a set number of tries [S85]. Module 15 covers queues in depth.

Choose by what the customer needs to know right now. Payments: the customer must know whether they paid, so wait. Stock: you must not oversell, so wait, or reserve with a short hold. An email, a receipt PDF, an analytics event: the customer does not need them to finish, so send a message.

</GoDeeper>

## Save the message with the order

<MermaidDiagram title="Order placed, email sent later" chart={`sequenceDiagram
  participant C as Customer
  participant O as Orders
  participant Q as Message queue
  participant N as Notifications
  C->>O: place order
  O->>O: save order and message in one transaction
  O-->>C: order placed
  O->>Q: relay the message
  Q->>N: deliver (maybe twice)
  N->>N: send email once
`} legend="The customer is told 'placed' before the email exists. The message is saved with the order, so a crash cannot lose it. The email service must cope with a repeat." />

The customer hears "order placed" first. The email follows. For this to be safe, the message must be saved in the same transaction as the order, or a crash between the two loses it. The email service must also cope with a message that arrives twice. Those two rules come from module 14.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the outbox and the repeat">

If Orders saved the order and then published the message in a second step, a crash between the two would leave an order with no email, the dual write from chapter 14.4. The fix is the outbox: Orders writes the order and the message to its own database in one transaction, and a relay later reads the message table and publishes it. The relay may publish a message twice, so the consumer must be idempotent [S79]. The consumer keeps a list of message ids it has handled and skips a repeat, as chapter 14.5 showed.

A message that arrives after the customer has cancelled needs care too. Include the state it expects, or check the current state before acting. Treat every message as possibly late, repeated and out of order, and write the receiver for that.

</GoDeeper>

## Joins do not cross services

<MermaidDiagram title="Need data you do not own?" chart={`flowchart TD
  Q{"Do I need another service's data?"} -->|"it must be exact now"| A["Ask its interface at request time"]
  Q -->|"a few minutes late is fine"| B["Keep my own copy, built from its events"]
  Q -->|"only to show one screen"| C["Combine the answers at the gateway or a backend for the client"]
`} legend="Three ways to use data you do not own. None of them reads the other service's tables." />

You cannot write a SQL join across two services' databases. Choose a way that fits how fresh the data must be. Ask the owner when the answer must be exact now. Keep a copy when a short delay is fine. Combine answers at the edge when you only need them for one screen.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: keep call chains short">

Every extra service on a request path adds latency, a failure chance and a place to wait. A request that goes Checkout, then Orders, then Stock, then Pricing, then Payments is five hops deep. From chapter 2.6, five services each at 99.9% give about 99.5%, and from 16.4, a retry at every layer can multiply the load by 3 to the power 5, which is 243 [S89].

Rules of thumb to keep chains short: let one service orchestrate the others instead of calling them in a long relay; keep slow or optional calls off the main path (16.6); and pass the time limit down so deeper calls know how long they have left. Prefer one call that returns what the caller needs over three calls that each return a little. If two services always call each other, they are probably one service split in the wrong place.

**In AWS.** A queue between two services is Amazon SQS, with a dead-letter queue for messages that keep failing [S83, S85]. Callers in a private network find services by name; AWS Cloud Map keeps a list of names and returns healthy instances by default [S130].

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

Say: "I draw service lines around things that change together, and each service owns its data. Others get the data through its interface or by keeping their own copy from events, never by reading its tables. I make a call synchronous when the user needs the answer now, such as a payment, and asynchronous through a queue when they do not, such as an email. I save the message in the same transaction as the order, with an outbox, and make the consumer idempotent."

An interviewer may ask: "What if the order service and the payment service need a transaction together?" Say that a transaction across services is not available, and point to a saga (chapter 14.3): a series of local steps with compensating actions.

</GoDeeper>
