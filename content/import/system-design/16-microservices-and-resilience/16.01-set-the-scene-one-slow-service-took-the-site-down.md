Tawseel is a delivery app for neighbourhood shops (a made-up app). Its Checkout server has 50 workers. A <GlossaryTerm term="worker" definition="One slot in a server that handles one request at a time. While it waits for another service, it cannot take a new request." /> handles one page at a time, and waits if the page calls another service. One of those services is Picks, which adds a "you may also like" row. It is only an extra.

## One slow extra, and no page loads

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m16-slow-picks" />

Phones send 100 pages a second. Each page uses a worker for about 100 ms, so 10 of the 50 workers are busy. Then Picks slows down to 10 seconds. Each page now holds its worker for 10 seconds, and all 50 are gone in half a second. Picks did not fail. It was only slow.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why slow is worse than dead">

The arithmetic is Little's law from chapter 2.5: the number of busy workers equals the arrival rate times the time each request takes. At 100 pages a second and 0.1 seconds each, that is 10 busy workers. At 10 seconds each, it is 100 times 10 = 1,000 workers. Tawseel has 50, so the pool is full after 50 divided by 100 = 0.5 seconds. The numbers are made up for easy sums.

A dead service is kinder than a slow one. When a service is down, the connection is refused at once and the caller gets an error in milliseconds. It can show the page without the row and free the worker. A slow service gives no answer and no error. The caller keeps waiting, and every wait holds a worker, a connection and some memory. The same shape appears in Google's SRE book as a cascading failure: a resource runs out in one place and the trouble spreads to places that did not cause it [S91].

</GoDeeper>

## The pay page died too

<MermaidDiagram title="Why the whole site stopped" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["Phones"] --> W["Checkout: 50 workers shared by every page"]
  W -->|"home page calls Picks"| K["Picks: slow, 10 s"]
  W -->|"pay page needs nothing from Picks"| Y["Payments: healthy"]
`} legend="Every page shares the same 50 workers. The pay page never calls Picks, but it needs a free worker, and the stuck home pages hold them all." />

The pay page does not use Picks at all. It still needs a free worker, and the home pages are holding all of them. So an optional feature took down the one that earns money. This is called a cascading failure: trouble in one part spreads to parts that did not cause it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what this module teaches">

The rest of module 16 is about two questions. The first is when to split a system into services, and where to draw the lines (16.2 and 16.3). Splitting makes this story possible: in a single program, Picks would be a function call, and a function call cannot hang on a network. The second question is how to keep a system standing when one part is slow or broken. You will learn time limits and careful retries (16.4), circuit breakers, separate worker pools and load shedding (16.5), and fallbacks that give the customer something useful (16.6). In 16.7 you write a breaker and a retry budget, and in challenge 15 you run Tawseel's Checkout against a slow Picks and measure what each protection buys.

Chapter 1.1 mentioned that retries can keep a system down after the first problem is over. Chapter 16.4 shows exactly how.

</GoDeeper>
