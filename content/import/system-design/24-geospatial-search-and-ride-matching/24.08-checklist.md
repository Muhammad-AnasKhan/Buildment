## Your gate answer: Hamel Couriers

This is the case study again on a different product. Hamel (a made-up service) delivers parcels on the same day by bicycle and motorbike in one city. Here are the assumptions. There are 80,000 couriers online at the busiest time, and each courier's phone reports its place every 10 seconds. Customers send 300,000 parcels a day, and the busiest second is 5 times the average. When a parcel is booked, Hamel finds the nearest free couriers and offers the job to one at a time. Once a parcel is on its way, 400,000 customers at the busiest time watch their courier on a map, and each map refreshes every 10 seconds. A customer sees only their own courier. Couriers move at about 15 km/h. In an interview, this is the "design a delivery tracking service" question, and it is the same loop you just ran.

A weak answer copies Safar: shards, a stadium plan, a batch window. A strong answer starts from the numbers and lets them decide which boxes to draw. Compute the location updates a second, the nearby searches a second, and the memory for the latest places. Then ask what those numbers need. Chapter 24.1 showed Safar's updates at 250,000 a second, and that decided almost everything. Does Hamel have that problem? If the updates are a few thousand a second, one memory node with a standby may be a design you can defend, and sharding by box may be something you leave out. Say so, and say when you would change your mind.

Then separate the two jobs. Finding couriers near a pickup point is a nearby search, and it happens only when a parcel is booked. Showing a customer their own courier is not a search at all. It is a lookup of one courier by id. Say how you serve those 400,000 customers, and whether a short copy, a push connection or something else suits it. Say how a courier is claimed so that two parcels do not get the same courier. Say what happens when the memory node restarts.

Be explicit about one trade-off. Write it in the same shape each time: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would switch. A good cost line has a number or a name. For example: "a courier's place can be up to 10 seconds old, about 42 m at bicycle speed," or "a restart leaves the map empty for 10 seconds." If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished.

A good order for 250 words is the order of the loop. Numbers come first, in two or three lines. Then the sketch and the deep dive: how a place is stored, how a search runs, how a courier is claimed. Then the trade-off, and last the failure. If you run short of words, cut the sketch, not the numbers or the trade-off.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is copying Safar's design without checking the numbers, so shards and a quadtree appear that nothing in the estimate asks for. The second is treating "show my courier" as a nearby search, which turns 40,000 simple lookups into 40,000 searches. The third is forgetting the claim: whichever design you pick, a courier must not be offered two parcels at once.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right design. Two students can choose differently and both pass, as long as each one starts from the numbers, ties the design to them, names the failure the choice answers, and says when they would change their mind. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens in the market square at closing time, or how you would find couriers that go silent. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, about 250 words. A recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.

Store: one memory node holds a map from driver id to place and from geohash box (6 letters) to couriers, with a standby that receives the same pings. History goes to a log. A search reads the box and its rings, then measures the true distance. Claim: a compare-and-set on the courier's record, free to held with a 10 second expiry, so two parcels never get one courier. Tracking: 400,000 customers every 10 seconds is 40,000 lookups of one courier by id. I serve them from a copy of each courier's place refreshed every 5 seconds, or push it over server-sent events. Trade-off: I do not shard. It buys a much simpler system with one node and no scatter-gather. It costs a single hot node: 8,000 updates is under a fifth of the 50,000 planned for one node. I would shard by box prefix if updates passed 25,000 a second. Failure: if the node restarts, the map is empty for up to 10 seconds, then refills from the next reports, and the standby covers most restarts."
