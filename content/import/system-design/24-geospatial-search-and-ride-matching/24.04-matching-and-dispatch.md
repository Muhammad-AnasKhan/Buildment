The search has returned a short list of drivers near the rider. Now Safar must choose one, ask them, and be sure that no other rider gets the same driver. This is dispatch.

## Offer to one driver at a time

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m24-offer-one-driver" />

Safar picks the best driver and offers the ride. The driver has 10 seconds to accept. If the driver says no, or does not answer, the offer goes to the next driver. The rider waits for the first yes. If a driver accepts 7 offers in 10, three offers in a row all fail only 2.7 times in 100.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: one at a time, or many at once">

Offering to one driver at a time is simple and fair. Nobody is asked and then told "never mind". The cost is time: with a 10 second limit, three misses cost up to 30 seconds. At 7 acceptances in 10, the chance of three misses in a row is 0.3 x 0.3 x 0.3 = 0.027, or 2.7%. The chance of at least one miss is 30%, and each miss can cost the full 10 seconds if the driver does nothing.

The other choice is to offer to several drivers at once and take the first yes. That finds a driver faster. But two drivers may both say yes, and the second one is told the ride is gone, after they started to drive. Drivers dislike this, and some stop answering. A middle path: offer to one driver, with a short limit (6 to 8 seconds), and send the next offer early when the driver is slow to open it.

Safar uses one driver at a time and a 10 second limit. These are course choices. Measure the acceptance rate in production and tune the limit with it.

</GoDeeper>

## The straight line is not the road

<MermaidDiagram title="Straight line against the road" chart={`flowchart LR
  R["Rider"] -->|"300 m straight"| D1["Driver 1, across the river"]
  R -->|"900 m straight"| D2["Driver 2, same bank"]
  D1 -.->|"4 km by the bridge, about 8 min"| R
  D2 -.->|"1.1 km by road, about 2 min"| R
`} legend="A made-up example. Solid lines are straight-line distance. Dotted lines are the road. At 30 km/h, 4 km takes about 8 minutes and 1.1 km about 2 minutes." />

Straight-line distance is only a first guess. A driver 300 m away across a river may be 4 km away by road. So Safar takes the 10 nearest drivers by straight line, asks a routing service for the travel time of each, and ranks them by time. That is 10 routing calls for a request, not thousands.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why shortlist, and what else to rank by">

Routing is slow compared to a distance check. A search returns perhaps 1,000 candidates in a busy centre. Asking for the travel time of all of them is 580 x 1,000 = 580,000 routing calls a second at the busiest time. Asking about the 10 nearest by straight line is 580 x 10 = 5,800 calls a second. Cheap first, expensive second: the straight line is the filter, and the road is the judge.

The shortlist can miss the best driver, as in the picture, when the nearest 10 by line are all across the river. A wider shortlist (20) costs more calls. This is a trade-off to tune.

Travel time is the first rule, not the only rule. A fair system adds tie-breakers: a driver who has waited longest goes first when two are equal, and a driver who often declines goes later. Fairness has two sides. For the rider it means a quick pickup. For the driver it means the nearest trips do not always go to the same people in the middle of town.

</GoDeeper>

## Two riders, one driver

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m24-two-riders-one-driver" />

At 600 requests a second, two searches often look at the same area in the same moment. Both see Driver D as free, and both send D an offer. D has two rides to choose from, and one rider waits for a driver who never comes. This is the same race as two buyers of the last ticket in chapter 7.1.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: check, then act">

The bug is a read followed by a write with a gap between them: "is D free?" then "offer to D". Another search can run in the gap. It is the lost update from chapter 7.1 in a new place, and it does not need unlucky timing. With hundreds of requests a second in a few busy areas, it happens all the time.

Doing the check twice does not help. The second check also has a gap after it. Telling the driver to refuse the second offer does not help either, because the second rider is already waiting. The check and the change must be one step that nothing can come between.

</GoDeeper>

## Claim the driver first

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m24-claim-first" />

Before sending an offer, a search must claim the driver: change D from free to held, but only if D is still free. This one step is atomic, so only one search wins. The loser gets an answer at once and goes on to the next driver. A claim expires after 10 seconds, so a crash never locks a driver for ever.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the claim in code, and where it lives">

This is a compare-and-set (chapter 7.5): change the value only if it is still what you expect. Here it is in a single process, where a JavaScript function cannot be interrupted. In a real store it is one conditional update, such as an update with "only where the status is free".

```js
function claimDriver(id, requestId, now, holdMs = 10_000) {
  const d = drivers.get(id);
  if (!d) return false;
  if (d.status === "free" || (d.status === "held" && d.until <= now)) {
    drivers.set(id, { status: "held", heldBy: requestId, until: now + holdMs });
    return true;
  }
  return false;
}
```

Running it with two requests gives:

```text
A claims D at t=0: true
B claims D at t=1: false
B claims D at t=11 (A's hold ran out): true
```

The hold expires on its own, like a lease (chapter 12.4). If the dispatcher crashes after the claim, D is free again after 10 seconds. If the driver declines, the dispatcher releases the claim at once.

**Where does the claim live?** Not in the location map. The map is keyed by box, and a driver moves from box to box. The claim needs one owner for each driver. So Safar keeps a small record per driver, keyed by the driver id: free, held, or on a trip. There are two keys for two jobs: the box finds drivers near a point, and the driver id guards one driver's status.

</GoDeeper>

## Match together, not one by one

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m24-batch-match" />

Drag Driver 1 along the road. Matching rider A first gives A the nearest driver, and B gets what is left. Matching both together looks at every pairing and keeps the smallest total. With Driver 1 at 0.4 km, one by one costs 1.9 km of pickups and together costs 1.1 km.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the assignment problem">

Choosing the pairing with the smallest total is a known problem, the assignment problem. For two riders and two drivers there are only two pairings, so checking both is easy. For hundreds of riders and drivers, a method such as the Hungarian algorithm finds the best pairing, in time that grows with the cube of the number of riders. That is why Safar matches inside a region (a box and its neighbours) and not across the whole city.

At 30 km/h, 0.8 km of saved pickup is 1.6 minutes of driving, split between the two riders. It costs rider A up to 2 more seconds of waiting. The saving is also for drivers, who drive fewer empty kilometres.

When the drivers are on the same side, as in the picture when Driver 1 is past the middle at 0.6 km or more, nearest-first is already best. The batch only helps when a nearest-first choice for one rider steals the only good driver of the next. This is common in busy places and rare in quiet ones.

</GoDeeper>

## Wait 2 seconds to match better

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m24-batch-window" />

Dispatch collects requests for a short window, 2 seconds here, then matches everyone together. Rider A waits up to 2 seconds longer. In return the pickups are shorter overall. A quiet city can use a shorter window, and a busy city a longer one. These are course choices to tune.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: who matches, and what happens when it fails">

Safar needs a place where requests wait for their window, and one owner per region. A stream (chapter 15.3) partitioned by box (the partition key from chapter 15.4) does both: all requests for one box and its neighbours reach the same matcher, in order. If a matcher crashes, another reads the same partition from the last committed position, and the requests that were waiting are not lost. The driver claims expire by themselves.

For the stadium, one box has 3,000 requests in a window. The matcher first matches within the small boxes of the quadtree (chapter 24.2), and widens only for riders who found no driver. A window that is too long hurts: riders watch a spinner. A window that is too short loses the gain. Many services measure both, wait time and pickup distance, and pick the point where the extra wait stops paying.

Price, surge and fraud checks are out of scope for this module.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say:** "A request becomes a nearby search, a shortlist by distance, and a ranking by travel time. Before I send an offer I claim the driver with a compare-and-set on the driver's record: free to held, with a 10 second expiry. That stops two riders getting the same driver. I offer to one driver at a time with a time limit. For busy areas I would collect requests for about 2 seconds and match them together, because nearest-first can use up the only good driver for the next rider."

**Expect:** "What if the dispatcher crashes after the claim?" **Answer:** "The claim expires after 10 seconds, so the driver is free again. The request is still in the stream, so another matcher picks it up."

</GoDeeper>
