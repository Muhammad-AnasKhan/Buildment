Safar must find the drivers near a rider about 40,000 times a second. This chapter is about the shape of that search: how to cut a map into boxes, so that "near me" becomes a lookup of a few boxes.

## A table cannot cut a map

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m24-scan-all-rows" />

Safar's first idea is a table: one row per driver, with a latitude and a longitude. A normal index sorts by one number. Sorting by latitude finds a long strip across the whole city, and most of that strip is not near the rider. A map has two directions, so we need a way to cut both at once.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how much a strip costs">

Take a city 50 km wide and 50 km tall with the 1 million drivers spread evenly. That is 400 drivers in each square kilometre. A rider asks for drivers within 1 km. The true answer is the drivers inside a circle with an area of about 3.14 km², so about 1,260 drivers.

An index on latitude alone can say "everyone between 1 km south and 1 km north of the rider". That is a strip 2 km tall and 50 km wide, 100 km², holding about 40,000 drivers. The database reads 40,000 rows to find 1,260, about 32 times too many. This is a normal B-tree index (chapter 7.3) doing its job. The index is fine. The question has two directions, and the index has one.

Some databases have a built-in spatial index that cuts both directions. A design can use one for a first version. Safar's problem is not only the search. It is also the 250,000 location writes a second, which chapter 24.3 solves with a different home for the data. So this chapter builds the search from a simpler idea that works in memory: boxes.

</GoDeeper>

## A short code for every box

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m24-geohash-zoom" />

Cut the world into 32 boxes, and give each box one letter. Tap a box to cut it into 32 smaller boxes and add a second letter. Keep going. The result is a geohash: a short word that names a box. A longer word means a smaller box, inside the bigger one. At 6 letters a box is about 1.2 km wide and 0.6 km tall.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how the letters are made">

A geohash is made by halving ranges again and again. Longitude runs from -180 to 180. Take the middle, 0. A point at or above the middle gets a bit 1, a point below gets a bit 0, and the range shrinks to that half. Then do the same for latitude, from -90 to 90. Alternate: longitude, latitude, longitude, and so on. Every 5 bits become one letter from a 32-letter alphabet: `0123456789bcdefghjkmnpqrstuvwxyz`, which skips a, i, l and o so that letters are not confused with digits.

A worked example: latitude 42.6 and longitude -5.6. The first bit: -5.6 is below 0, so 0. The second: 42.6 is above 0, so 1. The third: -5.6 is above -90, so 1. Then 0 and 1. The bits 01101 are the number 13, and the 14th letter is `e`. Five more bits give `z`. After 5 letters the code is `ezs42`. Chapter 24.5 has you write this. The same method gives `u4pruydqqvj` for latitude 57.64911 and longitude 10.40744.

Because each letter refines the box before it, a longer code always starts with the shorter one. A box named `stq4` contains every box named `stq4` followed by more letters. So a database with an ordinary text index can find all drivers in a box with a prefix search. Geohash was invented by Gustavo Niemeyer in 2008.

| Letters | Box width at the equator | Box height |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 39 km | 19.5 km |
| 5 | 4.9 km | 4.9 km |
| 6 | 1.2 km | 0.6 km |
| 7 | 153 m | 153 m |
| 8 | 38 m | 19 m |

Boxes get narrower as you move away from the equator. At 30 degrees north a 6-letter box is about 1.06 km wide, not 1.22 km. The sizes come from the bit counts, and we checked them with code.

</GoDeeper>

## Close drivers can have different codes

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m24-cell-edge" />

Drag the rider and change the box size. Two points 30 m apart can sit on opposite sides of an edge, so their codes differ. On the 0 degree line they differ from the very first letter. A search of one box misses the driver just across. The fix: search the rider's box and its neighbours, then check the real distance.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how many boxes to search">

A ring of k boxes around the rider's box is guaranteed to cover a radius of k times the smaller box side. The rider might stand at the very edge of the middle box, and the ring must still reach the radius in every direction. So with 6 letters, a box is 0.61 km tall, one ring (9 boxes) covers only 0.61 km north and south. A 1 km search needs two rings (25 boxes), which reach 1.22 km.

| Letters | Search radius | Rings | Boxes read | Area read | Drivers read at 100 per km² |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 1 km | 1 | 9 | 215 km² | about 21,500 |
| 6 | 1 km | 2 | 25 | 18.7 km² | about 1,870 |
| 7 | 1 km | 7 | 225 | 5.3 km² | about 530 |

The circle itself is 3.14 km². Big boxes mean few lookups but many drivers to measure. Small boxes mean many lookups and few drivers. A good choice reads between 9 and 25 boxes, which is why Safar uses 6 letters for a 1 km search. The 100 drivers per km² is a course number for a busy centre.

The nine-box rule only holds when each box is at least as wide and as tall as the radius. Near the poles and the 180 degree line the neighbour rules change: east of the last box wraps to the first, and there is no box north of the north pole.

</GoDeeper>

## Search the box and its eight neighbours

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m24-nine-cells" />

Safar finds the rider's box, adds the boxes around it, and reads the drivers in all of them. Then it measures the true distance to each driver and keeps the nearest. The boxes only shorten the list. The measuring gives the right answer, so the box size changes the speed, never the result.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the search in code (run, with its output)">

The driver map is a map from box code to the drivers in that box. This sketch uses the geohash functions you write in chapter 24.5. It adds four drivers: one 30 m east across an edge, one about 400 m north, one about 980 m north, and one about 2.2 km north.

```js
function nearby(lat, lon, radiusMetres, { precision = 6, rings = 2 } = {}) {
  const me = { lat, lon };
  const hits = [];
  for (const box of boxesAround(lat, lon, precision, rings)) {
    for (const [id, p] of boxes.get(box) ?? []) {
      const d = metres(me, p);          // true distance, the haversine formula
      if (d <= radiusMetres) hits.push({ id, metres: Math.round(d) });
    }
  }
  return hits.sort((a, b) => a.metres - b.metres);
}
```

Searching for drivers within 1,000 m of the rider (box `stq4vy`) gives this output, using 0, 1 and 2 rings:

```text
own box only (rings 0): []
nine boxes (rings 1):    [ { id: 'd1', metres: 30 }, { id: 'd2', metres: 401 } ]
25 boxes (rings 2):      [ { id: 'd1', metres: 30 }, { id: 'd2', metres: 401 }, { id: 'd3', metres: 979 } ]
```

The own box finds nobody, because the closest driver is across an edge. Nine boxes find two drivers but miss `d3` at 979 m, who is inside the radius. Twenty-five boxes find all three. The 2.2 km driver is correctly left out by the distance check. This is the whole lesson in one run: the boxes must reach the radius, and the true distance decides the answer.

</GoDeeper>

## Crowded places need smaller boxes

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m24-quadtree-split" />

A quiet street needs one big box. A stadium after a match needs many small ones. A quadtree starts with one box and cuts it into four whenever it holds too many drivers. Drag the crowd: dense places get small boxes, and quiet places keep big ones. No box holds thousands of drivers.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the quadtree, and what a hot box really costs">

The rule is simple: when a box holds more than a set number of drivers, split it into four equal boxes and move the drivers down. With a limit of 500, a stadium with 3,000 drivers in one small area splits into 22 boxes, none holding more than about 190. A limit of 100 gives 70 boxes of 47 or fewer. The rest of the city stays in a few big boxes. Those counts come from the model in the picture, where the crowd is spread evenly inside one small square.

A fixed grid, like 6-letter geohashes, cannot do this: every box is the same size, so the box over the stadium holds all 3,000. A geohash can still be made adaptive. Use 6 letters everywhere, and 7 or 8 letters inside a box that is too full.

What does a full box really cost? We measured it. In plain JavaScript on one laptop, scanning 3,000 drivers to find the 20 nearest took about 0.1 ms. So one core could answer about 10,000 such searches a second. A stadium crowd of 4,000 searches a second is busy, not broken. The real trouble is that one box has one owner. If a box lives on one machine, adding machines does not help it. This is the hot partition of chapter 9.4: a celebrity key that more shards cannot share. Splitting the box gives its parts to different machines.

The cost of a quadtree is that it changes while drivers move. Drivers cross edges more often as the boxes shrink, and a box that splits must be rebuilt. Use it where density varies a lot, and keep fixed boxes where it does not.

</GoDeeper>

## Hexagons and S2 are other grids

<MermaidDiagram title="Neighbours on two kinds of grid" chart={`flowchart LR

    S0["a box"] ---|"1.0 x4 edge neighbours"| S1["touch an edge"]
    S0 ---|"1.4 x4 corner neighbours"| S2["touch a corner"]
  end
  subgraph HX["Hexagon grid: 1 distance"]
    H0["a cell"] ---|"1.0 x6 neighbours"| H1["touch an edge"]
  end
`} legend="Distances are centre to centre. A square box has eight neighbours at two different distances. A hexagon has six neighbours, all at the same distance." />

Square boxes have two kinds of neighbour: four that share an edge, and four that only touch at a corner, 1.4 times farther. A hexagon has six neighbours, all the same distance away. Uber's H3 uses hexagons. S2 uses square-like cells that are numbered along a curve, so nearby cells get nearby numbers.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: H3, S2 and which to pick">

**H3** cuts the world into hexagon cells at 16 sizes, called resolutions 0 to 15. At resolution 8 a cell has an edge of about 0.53 km and an area of about 0.74 km². At resolution 9 the edge is about 0.20 km and the area about 0.11 km². A sphere cannot be covered with hexagons alone, so there are always 12 pentagon cells. A cell and its six neighbours (a ring of 1) reach about one edge length from any point in the middle cell, which we worked out by drawing it. H3 was built for Uber's needs and is open source under Apache 2.

**S2** projects the sphere onto the six faces of a cube, and cuts each face into four, again and again, like a quadtree. It has 31 levels, 0 to 30, and the smallest cells are about 1 cm across. Every cell has a 64-bit number, and the cells are numbered along a space-filling curve (a Hilbert curve), so nearby cells usually have nearby numbers. All cells inside one cell have numbers in one continuous range. A database with a sorted index on the number can cover a region with a few range scans.

Geohash gets the same kind of range for free from its text prefix. The choice is mostly practical. Geohash is the simplest: no library, and it works with any text index. H3 gives neighbours that are equal and easy to reason about, and is popular for grouping and pricing by area. S2 gives exact, nested cells and good range scans. All three work for Safar. Pick the one your team and your tools already know, and keep the cell size choice from this chapter.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say:** "I would cut the map into cells, and keep the latest place of each driver in memory, in a map from cell code to drivers. A search reads the rider's cell and the cells around it, because a close driver can sit across an edge. Then I measure the true distance and keep the nearest. I would pick a cell size so a search reads 9 to 25 cells. For crowded places I would split cells with a quadtree, so no cell holds thousands of drivers."

**Expect:** "Why not just use a database with a spatial index?" **Answer:** "It is a fine first version for searches. My worry is the write rate: 250,000 location updates a second. That is why I keep the live places in memory, and it is the next chapter."

</GoDeeper>
