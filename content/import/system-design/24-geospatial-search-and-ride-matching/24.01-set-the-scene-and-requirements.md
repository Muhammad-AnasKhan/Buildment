Safar is a ride service (a fictional product in a fictional city). A rider taps Request, and a driver nearby comes to pick them up. This module runs the design loop from chapter 1.4 on Safar. This first chapter does the first two steps: say what Safar must do, then turn that into numbers.

## One tap, one search, one match

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m24-request-a-ride" />

The rider taps Request. Safar asks a map of drivers who is near, picks one driver and sends an offer. The driver accepts, and the rider sees a car is coming. All of this should take a few seconds. Two parts do the work: finding the drivers near a point, and choosing one of them.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: clarify first, the way chapter 1.4 does">

Before any numbers, ask what Safar must do and what it will not do. Here is the scope the product owner agreed. All of it is a course choice, because Safar is made up.

**Safar does.** A rider sees nearby cars on a map. A rider requests a ride from where they stand. Safar offers the ride to a driver, and the driver accepts or declines. The rider sees the driver coming.

**Safar does not, for now.** It has no prices, payments or ratings, no pooled rides, no booking for later, and no route guidance. Each is a fair feature, and each would add work to every later step.

**Fast.** The first offer should reach a driver within 3 seconds of the request, measured as p99 (chapter 2.4). A nearby search should answer in under 100 ms at the service.

**Fresh.** A car on the rider's map may be up to 5 seconds old. A driver's place used for matching should be no older than 5 seconds.

**Reliable.** Requesting a ride works 99.95% of the time. Losing an old location is fine, because the next one comes in 4 seconds. Losing a trip that has started is never fine.

**Limits.** A team of six and a cloud account. Safar starts in one metro area and later copies the design to other cities.

The line about freshness matters later. Because a place is only useful for a few seconds, Safar does not have to keep every location safe on disk. That one answer will decide a trade-off in chapter 24.3.

## Drag the crowd, find the load

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m24-estimate-play" />

Drag the number of drivers, then the seconds between reports. Reports stay far above searches: about 250,000 a second against about 40,000. Ride requests are tiny, about 580 a second at peak. The state is tiny too: one place per driver is about 100 MB. The ringed card is the number that drives the design: the stream of location reports.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the working, and what is an assumption">

Nobody gave Safar these numbers, so each one is written down as an assumption: 1 million drivers online at the busiest time, one report every 4 seconds, 50 bytes per report, 100 bytes to hold one driver's latest place, 10 million rides a day, a busiest second that is 5 times the average, 200,000 riders with the app open and a map refresh every 5 seconds. The method is the one from chapter 2.3: start from people, divide by seconds, keep the unit next to every number.

| Quantity | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Location reports a second | 1,000,000 / 4 | 250,000 |
| Bandwidth in | 250,000 x 50 B | 12.5 MB/s, or 100 Mbit/s |
| Map searches a second | 200,000 / 5 | 40,000 |
| Rides a second, average | 10,000,000 / 86,400 | about 116 |
| Rides a second, peak | 116 x 5 | about 580 |
| Latest places in memory | 1,000,000 x 100 B | 100 MB |
| History if every report is kept | 250,000 x 50 B x 86,400 | about 1.1 TB a day |
| Database nodes for the reports | 250,000 / 2,500 | 100 |

The 2,500 writes a second is the planned load of one database node, the same course number as chapter 20.2.

Now ask which number drives the design. Rides are 580 a second: one ordinary server could take them. Searches are 40,000 a second: a cache and a few servers can take them. Storage is small: the latest places fit in the memory of one machine. The location reports are the problem. 250,000 small writes a second, for data that is out of date in 4 seconds, would need about 100 database nodes. Safar is a write-rate problem first and a "find what is near me" problem second.

These are guesses. Say them out loud and invite correction. If drivers reported every second, the rate would be four times higher. If riders refreshed every 15 seconds, searches would be a third as many.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say:** "Before I draw, let me fix scope and numbers. I will support nearby cars on a map, a ride request, an offer to a driver, and accept or decline. I will leave out pricing, payments and pooling. I assume 1 million drivers online, a report every 4 seconds, and 200,000 riders refreshing every 5 seconds. That is about 250,000 location updates, 40,000 searches and 600 ride requests a second. So the writes are the load, and the data is tiny, 100 MB for the latest places."

**Expect:** "Why report every 4 seconds?" **Answer:** "It is a trade-off between freshness and load. At 30 km/h a car moves about 33 m in 4 seconds, which is fine for a map and for choosing a driver. A shorter interval multiplies the load."

</GoDeeper>
