After chapter 21.3, the feed service can collect a few hundred recent post ids for a reader. This chapter turns them into a page: choose which come first, make it fast, keep scrolling honest, and survive failure. It covers the second deep dive and the failure step of the design loop, and it uses caching from chapter 5.3, timeouts from chapter 16.4 and cursors from chapter 6.3.

## Three steps: candidates, rank, serve

<MermaidDiagram title="Opening the feed, step by step" chart={`sequenceDiagram
  participant P as Phone
  participant F as Feed service
  participant C as Page cache
  participant T as Timelines and star lists
  participant R as Ranker
  participant S as Post store
  P->>F: GET /feed
  F->>C: page?
  C-->>F: miss
  F->>T: post ids
  T-->>F: own timeline and star lists
  Note over F: Candidates: merge, keep the newest 500
  F->>R: score 500 candidates
  R-->>F: top 100 ids
  F->>S: fetch the first 20 posts
  S-->>F: 20 posts
  F->>C: save the page and the list
  F-->>P: page of 20 and a cursor
`} legend="Candidates are cheap and wide. Ranking is expensive and narrow. Serving fetches only what is shown." />

Every feed open runs three steps. Candidates: collect a few hundred recent post ids from the reader's timeline and the star lists. Rank: give each candidate a score and sort. Serve: fetch the top 20 posts and return them with a cursor. The whole path has a budget of 200 ms, and ranking is its slowest step.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the three steps and a latency budget">

**Candidates** are the posts that could be shown. Hikaya takes the reader's timeline (up to 800 ids, already roughly newest first), adds the latest 20 posts of each followed star (a reader follows about 20 stars), merges them by time (the code in chapter 21.5) and keeps the newest 500. This step is cheap and does not need to be smart.

**Ranking** decides the order. It is the expensive step, and the only one that needs the posts' details: who wrote them, how many likes they have, how old they are.

**Serving** fetches the 20 posts to show, drops posts the reader has hidden or no longer follows, and builds the cursor.

The 200 ms is the p99 limit from chapter 21.1. These are course numbers for a typical open, which leave room for the slow tail (chapter 2.4):

| Step | Time |
|---|---|
| Gateway and routing | 5 ms |
| Page cache lookup | 1 ms |
| Candidates (timeline and star lists, in parallel) | 15 ms |
| Merge and keep the newest 500 | 5 ms |
| Rank 500 candidates | 30 ms |
| Fetch 20 posts | 10 ms |
| Save the page and reply | 4 ms |
| Total, typical | about 70 ms |

</GoDeeper>

## Drag how much age counts

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m21-rank-play" />

Each post gets a score from three things: how close the reader is to the author, how many likes it has, and how old it is. Drag the half-life, the time after which a post's value halves. With a short half-life, fresh posts from close friends lead. With a long one, popular posts from far away climb. Watch the order change.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what a score is, and what real rankers do">

The picture uses a small formula so that you can see the idea: score = closeness x (1 + ln(1 + likes)) x 0.5 to the power (age in hours / half-life in hours). Closeness runs from 0 to 1. The five posts are Dina (a close friend, closeness 1.0, 6 likes, 1 hour old), Karim (a star, 0.5, 400,000 likes, 20 hours), the bakery (0.3, 40 likes, 5 hours), Omar (a cousin, 0.8, 3 likes, 9 hours) and Daily News (0.4, 2,000 likes, 3 hours). All of this is made up.

| Half-life | Order, best first |
|---|---|
| 3 hours | Dina, Daily News, the bakery, Omar, Karim |
| 12 hours | Daily News, Dina, Karim, Omar, the bakery |
| 48 hours | Karim, Daily News, Dina, Omar, the bakery |

A real ranker replaces the formula with a model trained on what readers did in the past. It predicts, for this reader and this post, the chance of a like, a reply or a long look, and combines those chances into one score. Its inputs are the same kinds of signals: closeness to the author, recency, engagement so far, and the type of post. The model is trained offline on logs and tested on a share of real readers before it is used for everyone. {/* source needed: a public description of how a large feed ranker is trained and evaluated; the course does not describe any company's ranker. */}

Two honest warnings. A new post has no likes yet, so the formula must not bury it, which is why the likes term starts at 1 and not at 0. And ranking is a product decision as much as an engineering one: a score that chases clicks can show readers more of what upsets them. Teams add rules on top, such as showing no more than two posts in a row from one author.

</GoDeeper>

## Rank a few hundred, not thousands

<MermaidDiagram title="The funnel" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Ids from timeline and star lists: about 1,200"] --> B["Keep the newest 500: candidates"]
  B --> C["Score each: about 30 ms"]
  C --> D["Keep the top 100: the saved list"]
  D --> E["Return 20 a page: fetch only these posts"]
`} legend="Each step throws most of the work away before the next, costlier step." />

Ranking costs time for every candidate, so Hikaya ranks only the newest 500. The top 100 are saved as a list, and only the 20 on the page are fetched as full posts. Scoring 5,000 candidates would take ten times as long, and the extra posts are old ones that would rarely win.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the cost of ranking">

Suppose scoring one candidate takes about 0.06 ms of one core (a course number). Then 500 candidates take 30 ms of one core. At 17,400 feed opens a second, Little's law (chapter 2.5) says about 17,400 x 0.030 = 522 cores are busy ranking at the peak. With 5,000 candidates it would be about 5,200 cores. Ranking is where the compute bill is, so the number of candidates is a budget, not a detail.

Fetching only the 20 shown posts, not all 500 candidates, saves 25 times the post reads. Saving the top 100 means the next four pages need no new ranking at all, which the paging beat uses.

The funnel also explains why the first step is allowed to be dumb. If a good post is left out of the newest 500, no later step can bring it back. So the candidate step is where a bug hurts most quietly.

</GoDeeper>

## Cache the finished page for seconds

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m21-page-cache" />

Building a page takes about 60 ms, and reading a saved page takes about 5 ms. So Hikaya saves each finished page for 10 seconds. A second open in that time is fast, but a new post can take up to 10 seconds to show. A reader's own new post must show at once, so posting removes her saved page.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the page cache and freshness">

The cache key is the reader and the page. The time to live is 10 seconds, the same as the freshness promise in chapter 21.1: a longer time would save more work but break the promise. Suppose 20% of feed opens come within 10 seconds of the same reader's last open (pull to refresh, switching between apps). That is an assumption. Then the page cache removes 20% of the ranking work, from 522 to about 418 cores.

When Maya posts, the post service deletes her cached page. This is delete-on-write from chapter 5.5. Facebook's memcache paper also deletes on write instead of updating, because a delete is safe to repeat [S24]. Without it, Maya would post and not see her own post for 10 seconds, which is worse than any follower seeing it late. It is the read-your-writes rule from chapter 8.4.

Followers' pages are not touched when someone posts. They age out on their own. That is the trade: a bounded delay for a large saving.

</GoDeeper>

## Page two must not reshuffle page one

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m21-reshuffle" />

Ranking again can reorder the list, so "items 21 to 40" no longer means "the ones after page 1". Here three posts come back. The fix: save the ranked ids when page 1 is built, and give the phone a cursor that points into that saved list. Page 2 is cut from the same list, so nothing repeats.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: cursors for a ranked feed">

Chapter 6.3 showed that an offset repeats and skips rows when the list changes, and that a cursor holding the last row's sort key fixes it. A feed sorted by time can use exactly that: the cursor is the time and the id of the last post shown (the time alone could skip a post that shares a millisecond, so the id breaks ties). The code in chapter 21.5 pages that way.

A ranked feed has no stable sort key, because scores move. So the cursor points into a saved list instead. It holds a list id and a position, for example `{ list: "a91f", at: 20 }`, written as an opaque string the client passes back untouched. The saved list is the top 100 ids, kept for 10 minutes under the key (reader, list id). Page 2 is ids 21 to 40 of that list, fetched as posts. The next three pages come the same way.

When the list runs out or expires, the server has two choices: rank again over posts older than the last one shown, or fall back to time order for the tail. New posts do not jump in at the top while the reader scrolls. A small "3 new posts" button builds a fresh page 1 when she asks.

</GoDeeper>

## A slow ranker must not break the feed

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m21-ranker-slow" />

The ranker is another service, and it can be slow or down. The feed service waits only 50 ms for it. If the ranker is late, the feed service returns the candidates sorted by time, which it already has. The reader gets a feed, a little less relevant, on time. Ranking is an extra, not the product.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the failure step">

Ask what each block does when it fails, and what the reader sees.

| What fails | What the reader sees | What saves it |
|---|---|---|
| Ranker slow or down | The feed in time order | A 50 ms timeout (chapter 16.4), a circuit breaker (16.5) and the time-order fallback (16.6) |
| Timeline cache restarts empty | First opens a little slower | Reload each list from the timeline store on a miss, spread over many partitions |
| Timeline store loses data | A thin feed, filling up over time | Rebuild by pull, one reader at a time, active readers first, and merge the latest posts of each followed account |
| A star's list node dies | The feed without that star for a moment | Several copies of each star list, and a reload from the author index |
| Fan-out queue backs up | New posts take minutes to arrive | A backlog alarm, more workers, and the author's own timeline written at posting time |

Reloading 17,400 different lists a second is fine, because they sit on many partitions. Rebuilding every timeline at once is not an option. 50 million readers x 150 lookups is 7.5 billion lookups, and at 100,000 a second that is about 21 hours. So rebuild lazily and coalesce: when a reader asks, build once, and let other requests for the same reader wait for that build (chapter 5.6). One rule runs through the table: show something older or plainer rather than an error.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say:** "A feed open has three steps: candidates, rank, serve. Candidates are the newest 500 ids from the reader's timeline merged with the star lists. A ranker scores them with signals like closeness to the author, engagement and age, and I keep the top 100. I fetch only the 20 posts shown. Ranking about 500 candidates takes about 30 ms, so I cache the finished page for 10 seconds, and I delete a reader's page when she posts. For paging I save the ranked list and put a pointer into it in the cursor, so page two never repeats page one. If the ranker is slow I time out after 50 ms and return the candidates in time order."

**Expect:** "What if the timeline store loses data?" **Answer:** "I rebuild lazily by pull: merge the latest posts of the accounts a reader follows, active readers first, and save the result. Rebuilding all 50 million at once would be about 7.5 billion lookups."

</GoDeeper>

  - "Patching the cached page is the update-in-place that chapter 5.5 warns about. A delete is simpler and safe to repeat."
