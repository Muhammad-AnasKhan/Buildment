Hikaya needs to turn a post into entries in other people's feeds. There are two ways, both old and well known, and Hikaya's numbers choose between them account by account. This chapter is the deep dive and the trade-off step of the design loop. It builds on fan-out from chapter 15.7 and hot keys from chapters 9.4 and 5.6.

## Push: copy the post into every timeline

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m21-push-post" />

Dina posts. A worker looks up her followers and adds the post id to each follower's timeline. This is fan-out on write, called push: the work happens when a post is published. One post costs one write for each follower. Opening the feed later is easy, because the list is ready.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how a push really runs">

The fan-out worker reads the followers from the follow copy keyed by followee, in pages of about 1,000. It writes the new id into the timelines in batches, spread across the timeline partitions by owner id. Each write adds the id to the front of the list and trims the list to 800 ids. Accounts with no timeline, the ones inactive for 30 days, are skipped. An everyday account with 100 followers needs about 100 writes, which finish in a fraction of a second after the queue hands over the event.

Unfollowing and deleting need care, because the timeline only holds ids. When Salma unfollows Dina, a background job removes Dina's ids from Salma's timeline. Until it runs, the feed service drops them anyway: the batch read returns each post with its author, and the service skips any author Salma no longer follows. Deleted posts are skipped the same way, as chapter 21.2 explained.

Following works the other way. A new follow gives nothing until the account posts again, so Hikaya copies the followee's latest 5 posts into the follower's timeline at follow time. Without that step, a new user would open an empty feed.

</GoDeeper>

## Pull: gather the posts when someone looks

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m21-pull-read" />

Omar opens the feed. Nothing was prepared. The feed service asks every account he follows for its latest posts, then merges the answers by time. This is fan-out on read, called pull. A post costs one write. A feed open costs one lookup for each followed account, 150 for Omar.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how a pull really runs, and its tail">

The feed service reads the reader's follow list from the copy keyed by follower. For each followed account it reads that account's latest posts from the author index (chapter 21.2). It merges the answers newest first and keeps a page. Nothing is stored per reader, so there is no timeline store, no fan-out fleet and no repair job. Follows, unfollows and deletes take effect at once, because every read starts from the current truth.

The price is in the reads. Each lookup is a separate call that can land on a slow server. Chapter 2.4 explained the arithmetic from "The Tail at Scale": if each call is slow 1% of the time, a request that makes n calls meets at least one slow call with probability 1 minus 0.99 to the power n [S8]. For 1 call that is 1%. For 150 calls it is 78%. Pull turns a rare slowness into the usual case.

That is why small apps start with pull and often should stay there. With 20 follows per account, the chance is 18%, and the whole design is smaller.

</GoDeeper>

## Most accounts are cheaper to push

<MermaidDiagram title="One everyday account, one day" chart={`flowchart LR
  subgraph PUSH["Push: work at posting time"]
    A["0.11 posts a day"] -->|"x 100 followers"| B["11 timeline writes"]
  end
  subgraph PULL["Pull: work at reading time"]
    C["100 followers, half active, 10 opens each"] -->|"1 lookup per open"| D["500 lookups of her list"]
  end
`} legend="The same account costs about 11 writes with push and about 500 lookups with pull." />

For an everyday account, push does about 11 writes a day and pull does about 500 lookups. People look at the feed far more often than they post. When reads outnumber posts, doing the work once at publish time is cheaper than doing it at every read. That is why push is the default for most accounts.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: when does the answer flip?">

The general rule has two parts. Push work in a day is the account's posts times its followers. Pull work in a day is its active followers times their opens. Both grow with the follower count, so the follower count alone does not flip the answer. What matters is posts per day compared with opens per follower per day. Here an active follower opens the app 10 times a day, and half of the followers are active, so a follower opens the feed 5 times a day on average. An account would have to post more than 5 times a day for pull to be cheaper in raw work.

So why do stars still break push? Not because of the daily total, but because of its shape. Push turns one post into millions of durable writes at the same moment, in a queue that other posts share. Pull turns the same work into reads of one list that is cached in memory. The next beat shows the first problem.

If your app has people who open the feed rarely, or accounts that post all day, redo the sum. The result moves. The sum is the skill.

</GoDeeper>

## One star post is 20 million writes

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m21-star-post" />

Karim has 20 million followers. One post is 20 million timeline writes. At the planned 150,000 writes a second, that is about 133 seconds of work for the whole fan-out fleet. Dina's post, sent a minute later, waits behind it in the line. Push breaks for the few accounts that everyone follows.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the skew in numbers">

Follower counts are not equal. This is a made-up composite of Hikaya's accounts, shaped like real social graphs (many small accounts, very few huge ones). It adds up to the same 15 billion follows, 150 on average, and about 10 million posts a day as chapter 21.1.

| Group | Accounts | Followers each | Posts a day each | Writes a day if pushed | Share of writes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Everyday | 80,000,000 | 100 | 0.11 | 0.88 billion | 10% |
| Local | 4,000,000 | 1,000 | 0.25 | 1 billion | 11% |
| Popular | 100,000 | 10,000 | 1 | 1 billion | 11% |
| Big | 10,000 | 100,000 | 2 | 2 billion | 23% |
| Celebrity | 1,000 | 1,000,000 | 4 | 4 billion | 45% |

The total is about 8.9 billion timeline writes a day, which is 103,000 a second on average and 308,000 at the busiest moment. Chapter 21.1 warned about a flat guess of 52,000. The real number is six times larger, because the accounts that post and the accounts with many followers overlap. About 11,000 accounts, 0.01% of all accounts, cause two thirds of all timeline writes.

The fan-out fleet is planned for 150,000 writes a second, which is half of its 300,000 maximum (chapter 2.5). Pure push needs twice the plan at the peak. The celebrity group averages 1 million followers, and its largest account, Karim, has 20 million. One post from him would hold the whole fleet for 133 seconds at the plan rate, or 67 seconds at the maximum.

A separate queue for big accounts would keep ordinary posts flowing while Karim's post runs. It does not make his post faster or cheaper, and it adds a rule about which queue a post goes to.

</GoDeeper>

## Drag the line between push and pull

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m21-fanout-play" />

Drag the line along the five groups. Accounts right of the line are pulled, the rest are pushed. Watch two bars. Timeline writes must stay under the planned 150,000 a second. Feed opens that meet a slow lookup must stay under one in five. Find the only place where both bars stay green.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the model behind the line">

The write bar counts timeline writes at the peak for the groups that are pushed. The read bar counts lookups for one feed open: one for the reader's own timeline, plus one for each pulled account the reader follows. Each reader follows 80 everyday, 40 local, 10 popular, 10 big and 10 celebrity accounts on average, which adds up to 150. The read bar is the chance that at least one lookup is slow, 1 minus 0.99 to the power of the lookups [S8].

| Pull accounts with at least | Writes a second at peak | Share of plan | Lookups per feed open | Opens that meet a slow lookup |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nobody (push all) | 308,000 | 206% | 1 | 1.0% |
| 1,000,000 followers | 169,000 | 113% | 11 | 10.5% |
| 100,000 followers | 100,000 | 67% | 21 | 19.0% |
| 10,000 followers | 65,000 | 44% | 31 | 26.8% |
| 1,000 followers | 31,000 | 20% | 71 | 51.0% |
| Everyone (pull all) | 0 | 0% | 151 | 78.1% |

The limits are 150,000 writes a second (the plan) and 20% of feed opens meeting a slow lookup (a course choice). Only the row for 100,000 followers passes both. The answer is not a law. It comes from these numbers. If posts double, the write bar moves up and the line moves left. If lookups get faster, the read bar relaxes and the line can move right.

</GoDeeper>

## The hybrid: push most, pull the stars

<MermaidDiagram title="The hybrid" chart={`flowchart TD
  P["A post is saved"] --> D{"100,000+ followers?"}
  D -->|"no: push"| W["Workers add the id to each follower's timeline"]
  D -->|"yes: pull"| S["Only the star's own recent list changes"]
  R["A feed is opened"] --> M["Merge: own timeline + each followed star's list"]
  W -.-> M
  S -.-> M
  M --> O["A page of posts, newest first"]
`} legend="Writes split by follower count. Reads always merge the reader's timeline with the few star lists the reader follows." />

Accounts with 100,000 or more followers are pulled. Everyone else is pushed. A star's post is saved once and goes on that star's own list, which is cached. At feed time the service merges the reader's pushed timeline with the lists of the few stars the reader follows. Chapter 21.5 writes that merge.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: running the hybrid, and the trade-off">

**The reader side.** The feed service needs to know which of the reader's follows are stars. Hikaya keeps a short list per reader, updated when the reader follows or unfollows a star. At the 100,000 line the average reader follows about 20 stars (10 big and 10 celebrity accounts), so a feed open is 1 + 20 = 21 lookups, made in parallel.

**The hot key.** Every follower of Karim reads the same few lists. With 20 million followers, half active, 10 opens a day each, that is about 100 million reads a day, 1,157 a second on average and 3,472 at the peak. A single DynamoDB partition is designed for 3,000 reads a second [S40], so Karim's list must sit in a cache with several copies. Facebook's memcache paper fixed a related hot-key problem with leases [S24], covered in chapter 5.6.

**Crossing the line.** An account grows from 90,000 to 110,000 followers. New posts switch to pull. The ids already pushed stay valid, so nothing is rebuilt. To stop an account near the line from flipping back and forth, use two thresholds: switch to pull at 100,000 and back to push at 80,000.

**The trade-off.** The choice is to push below 100,000 followers and pull above. It is right now because pure push needs twice the planned write capacity and pure pull makes most feed opens slow. It buys a write load at 67% of plan and 21 lookups for the average feed open. It costs a merge on every read, two code paths to test, and a threshold to watch: a bug there shows as missing posts. Two alternatives are credible. One is pure push with a slower queue for big accounts, which is simpler if followers of stars can wait a few minutes. The other is pure pull with a cached merged page per reader, which is simpler for a small app, such as the gate case in chapter 21.8. Move the line when posts per day, the fleet or the lookup speed changes by a factor of two.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say:** "There are two ways to build a feed. Fan-out on write copies each post into every follower's timeline: a feed open is one lookup, but a post costs one write per follower. Fan-out on read gathers posts from every followed account at read time: a post is cheap, but a feed open is about 150 lookups, and if each is slow 1% of the time, most opens meet a slow one. Reads outnumber posts 50 to 1, so I push by default. But follower counts are skewed: about 11,000 accounts cause two thirds of timeline writes, and one post from a 20-million-follower account would hold the fan-out fleet for over two minutes. So accounts above about 100,000 followers are pulled. Their recent posts are cached, and the feed service merges them with the reader's timeline."

**Expect:** "How do you pick 100,000?" **Answer:** "From two limits: planned write capacity and the share of feed opens that meet a slow lookup. I would measure both and move the line when traffic changes."

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Sources">

The slow-call arithmetic is from Dean and Barroso, "The Tail at Scale" [S8]. The per-partition limit of 3,000 read units a second is from the DynamoDB partition key guidance [S40]. Facebook's memcache leases for hot keys [S24]. The trade-off between push and pull is standard, and this course teaches it as a trade-off without describing any company's design: the only first-party source the research found, a 2012 talk on Twitter's timelines, was a video whose summary did not describe the fan-out [S103]. Hikaya, Karim, the account groups, the 150,000 writes a second plan and the 20% read limit are course assumptions or arithmetic on them.

</GoDeeper>
