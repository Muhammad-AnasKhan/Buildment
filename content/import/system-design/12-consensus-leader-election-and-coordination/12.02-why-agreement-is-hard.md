Tijara's standby scheduler listens for the main one's beat. How long should it wait before it takes over? Wait too little, and a healthy scheduler is replaced for nothing. Wait too long, and a crashed one leaves the nightly bills undone. This chapter shows both prices, and why one node's guess is never enough.

## Silence has two possible causes

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-slow-or-dead" />

The monitor pinged B and C and heard nothing for 200 ms. Then B's answer arrived, late. C had crashed and never answers. During the wait, the two silences look exactly the same. The monitor cannot know the cause. It can only wait for a while and then guess, and a guess can be wrong. This is the core problem of the whole module.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: every reason for silence">

A message that never arrives can have many causes. The node may have crashed. The program may be running but stuck in a long pause. The network may have dropped the request, or the answer. A router in between may be busy and holding the packet in a queue. From the monitor's chair, all of these produce the same observation: nothing came back.

This is more than a nuisance. A known result called FLP (named after Fischer, Lynch and Paterson) says that when messages can take any amount of time, no algorithm can promise to reach agreement if even one node may crash. The reason is the same as in the picture: you cannot tell a crashed node from a very slow one. Real systems get past this by adding a time assumption, such as "a healthy node answers within 200 ms", and by using randomness, as Raft does in the next chapters [S67].

So a timeout is a guess, and a guess can be wrong in two directions. If the monitor says "dead" about a node that was only slow, that is a false alarm. If it says "fine" about a node that has crashed, every client waits for nothing. The rest of the chapter is about choosing the guess well.

</GoDeeper>

## Drag the timeout, watch the price

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-timeout-slider" />

Drag the timeout from short to long. At 1.05 s the monitor spots a real crash fast, but it also raises about 340 false alarms an hour. At 1.2 s it raises about 2. At 1.5 s it never does, but a real crash now takes over a second to notice. Find the point where false alarms nearly stop, and read the cost.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the model behind the slider">

The model is simple on purpose. A node sends a beat every 1,000 ms. Each beat takes 10 ms plus a random extra time with an average of 30 ms, so most beats arrive in about 40 ms and a few arrive much later. The monitor suspects the node when the gap between two arrivals is longer than the timeout.

The gap between two arrivals is the beat interval plus the difference between two random delays. For this kind of delay, the chance that the gap is more than 100 ms over the interval is half of e to the power of minus 100 over 30, about 1.8 percent. That is where the 64 false alarms an hour at a 1.1 second timeout come from. A simulation of 720,000 beats gives the same figures as the formula.

| Timeout | False alarms per hour | Average time to notice a real crash |
|---|---|---|
| 1.05 s | about 340 | about 590 ms |
| 1.1 s | about 64 | about 640 ms |
| 1.15 s | about 12 | about 690 ms |
| 1.2 s | about 2.3 | about 740 ms |
| 1.3 s | about 0.08 | about 840 ms |
| 1.5 s | about 0 | about 1,040 ms |
| 2 s | about 0 | about 1,540 ms |

The detection time is the timeout minus about 460 ms, because on average the crash happens half a beat after the last beat was sent. Every extra 100 ms of timeout buys a large drop in false alarms at first, then almost nothing. The best timeout sits where the curve flattens. These are course numbers, not measurements of a real network. Real delays have heavier tails, so real timeouts are longer.

Two real systems handle this trade-off. Raft needs the time to send a message to be much smaller than the election timeout, and the election timeout much smaller than the time between failures. Its authors give a broadcast time of 0.5 to 20 ms and an election timeout between 10 and 500 ms [S68]. The phi accrual failure detector does not use one fixed timeout. It learns the pattern of past beats and reports a suspicion level. The Pekko documentation gives a default threshold of 8 and suggests 12 on EC2, where delays vary more [S73].

</GoDeeper>

## A paused node cannot tell

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-paused-wakes" />

Node A froze at 2 seconds and woke at 14. While it was frozen, B pinged it, got nothing, and began to replace it. A saw only its clock jump from 2 seconds to 14. Nothing in its memory says "you were away". A still believes it is the main. That belief is the danger, and the next chapters build the cure.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the node itself cannot be the judge">

When a program is paused, none of its code runs. It cannot count the pause or look at a clock during the pause. After the pause, it can compare two readings of a clock, but only if the code was written to do so. Even then, the pause could end right after the check and before the next action. There is always a gap between "I checked that I am the main" and "I act as the main".

This is why the standby's takeover rule cannot be fixed by making the main scheduler smarter. The decision about who is the main has to be made by others, and the old main has to be stopped from outside, even if it does not know it was replaced. Chapter 12.4 shows how a lease and a fencing token do that.

Kleppmann describes the same problem in his article on distributed locking: a client takes a lock, then pauses for a long garbage collection, the lock expires, and the client writes after the pause as if it still held the lock [S70].

</GoDeeper>

## So the group votes

<MermaidDiagram title="What the module builds" chart={`flowchart LR
  T["One node guesses: 'it is dead'"] --> Q["A majority of nodes agrees"]
  Q --> L["One leader per term, with a token"]
  L --> G["One shared log, in one order"]
  G --> S["Services use it: locks, configs, discovery"]
`} legend="Each step trades speed for safety: more nodes must agree before anything counts." />

One node's guess cannot be trusted, so a group decides. Think of asking five witnesses instead of one. The group is safe as long as a majority is alive. Each step costs time, because more nodes must answer before anything counts. The next chapters build these steps one by one: first the majority, then the leader, then the log.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: three jobs, one tool">

A cluster needs to answer three questions, and the same tool answers all of them. Who is alive? Who is the leader? In what order did things happen? A failure detector gives a first guess for the first question. A vote of a majority turns the guess into a decision. A leader then gives the order, so the third question has one answer.

Consensus is the name for a group of nodes agreeing on one value, even when some nodes fail and messages are lost. The leader election in Raft and the lock service in etcd both use it. When you use a lock from such a service, you borrow its consensus group's majority decision.

This costs something. Every decision waits for a majority to answer. With nodes in three zones of one region, that is a round trip of a few milliseconds. With nodes across continents, it is hundreds of milliseconds. And when a majority is not reachable, the group stops answering rather than guess. That choice is the heart of the later chapters.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

Say: "I cannot tell a slow node from a dead one, so I use a timeout, and a timeout is a guess. A short timeout means false alarms, a long one means slow failover. To be safe, I do not let one node decide: a majority of the cluster agrees on who is leader."

An interviewer may ask: "What timeout would you pick?" A good answer names the trade, gives a starting point from your measured delays (for example, a few times the 99th percentile of beat delay), and says that you would also fence the old leader in case the guess is wrong.

</GoDeeper>
