A store keeps three copies of every value, so one machine can fail. A write must reach some of the copies, and a read must ask some of them. How many is enough? The answer is a rule about overlap, and it explains why clusters have 3, 5 or 7 nodes.

## Two groups of two always meet

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-quorum-overlap" />

The store has three copies, R1 to R3. A write reaches R1 and R2. A read asks R2 and R3. Both groups hold 2 of 3, and two such groups must share a node: here it is R2. R2 has the new value, so the read finds it. A group like this, big enough to overlap with any other, is called a quorum.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the groups must overlap">

Take N copies. A write waits for W of them. A read asks R of them. If W plus R is more than N, the two groups cannot fit side by side without sharing a node. That is the pigeonhole rule: there are not enough copies to keep the two groups apart. At least W plus R minus N nodes are in both groups. In the picture, 2 plus 2 minus 3 is 1 shared node.

The shared node saw the write, so it has the new value. But the read also hears from nodes that missed the write. So every value carries a version number or timestamp, and the reader keeps the answer with the highest version. Without a version, the reader cannot choose between "v1" and "v2".

A majority is the smallest group that always overlaps with another majority. For N copies, a majority is N divided by 2, rounded down, plus 1. For 3 it is 2, for 5 it is 3, for 7 it is 4 [S52]. If both reads and writes use a majority, W plus R is always more than N.

The Dynamo paper describes a store that lets you pick N, R and W, and says (3, 2, 2) is a common choice. The delay of an operation is set by the slowest of the W or R replicas it must wait for, so a smaller R or W is faster [S45].

</GoDeeper>

## Move N, W and R, find the gaps

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-quorum-explorer" />

Set N, W and R, then tap nodes to fail them. When W plus R is more than N, the picture shows a shared node and says reads are safe. When it is N or less, it shows two groups that can be apart. Try N = 5 with W = 2 and R = 3. Then raise W to 3. Watch how many failures each setting survives.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the three numbers the explorer shows">

For any N, W and R, the explorer shows three things. First, whether reads must see the latest write: yes when W plus R is more than N. Second, how many failures writes survive: a write needs W nodes, so it keeps working while N minus W nodes are down. Third, how many failures reads survive: N minus R.

Look at the extremes. With W = N and R = 1, every write needs all copies, so one failed node blocks writes, but any one copy can answer a read. With W = 1 and R = N the opposite holds. With W = R = majority, reads and writes survive the same number of failures, and that number is the most any quorum choice can give both at once.

| N | W | R | Reads see latest write? | Writes survive | Reads survive |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 2 | 2 | yes | 1 failure | 1 failure |
| 3 | 1 | 3 | yes | 2 failures | 0 failures |
| 3 | 1 | 1 | no | 2 failures | 2 failures |
| 5 | 3 | 3 | yes | 2 failures | 2 failures |
| 5 | 2 | 3 | no | 3 failures | 2 failures |
| 5 | 1 | 5 | yes | 4 failures | 0 failures |

In the explorer the nodes have different response times: 10, 15, 25, 40 and 70 ms for five nodes. A write that waits for W nodes finishes when the W-th fastest of them answers. With W = 3 that is 25 ms, and with W = 5 it is 70 ms. This is why a bigger quorum is slower, not just stricter [S45].

</GoDeeper>

## Five nodes: two can fail

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-majority-survives" />

With N4 and N5 down, N1 still gets the write onto N2 and N3. That is 3 of 5, a majority, so the write counts. Then N3 fails too. Now only 2 of 5 can answer, which is not a majority. The cluster stops instead of guessing. It tolerates two failures, not three.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why 2f + 1, and why stopping is right">

A cluster that must survive f failures needs 2f + 1 nodes: f can fail and f + 1 are left, which is still a majority. Three nodes survive 1 failure, five survive 2, seven survive 3. An even number does not help. Four nodes have a majority of 3, so they also survive just 1 failure, and they cost one more machine than three nodes [S52].

Why stop when a majority is gone? Imagine a network split that cuts five nodes into two groups of 2 and 3. Only the group of 3 is a majority. If the group of 2 kept accepting writes, the two sides could accept different values for the same key, and there would be no way to merge them safely. Stopping the smaller side is the price of one single truth.

This is the choice behind the word CAP. When a network partition happens, a system must either keep answering on both sides, and risk two truths, or keep one truth and refuse some requests. Kleppmann points out that this is a choice made during a partition, not a menu of any two out of three, and most real systems fit neither label strictly [S55].

</GoDeeper>

## A half-finished write splits the readers

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-two-readers" />

The writer sends v2 but crashes after R1 stores it. Reader A asks R1 and R2 and sees v2. Reader B asks R2 and R3 and sees only v1. One value, two answers, at the same moment. Overlap alone did not help, because the write never reached a quorum of 2.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: overlap is necessary, not sufficient">

The rule W plus R is more than N is only a promise about writes that finished. A write that reached fewer than W nodes is in a gray zone. A reader that happens to ask the one node that has it will return the new value. Another reader may return the old one. If the first reader then asks again and gets the old value, time seems to go backwards.

Kleppmann's notes say that plain quorum reads and writes are not linearizable by themselves, which is the strong promise that the store behaves like one copy (you meet it in module 11). The ABD algorithm adds read repair: a reader that sees different versions writes the newest one back to a quorum before it returns [S52]. That closes the gray zone for the value the reader returns.

Two more things break the overlap. A sloppy quorum, used by Dynamo, accepts writes on stand-in nodes when the usual nodes are down, so the groups need not overlap (module 13) [S45]. And a quorum by itself does not decide who may write or in what order. For that, a group needs a leader and a shared log. That is what Raft adds in 12.5 and 12.6.

</GoDeeper>

## Choose W and R for the load

<MermaidDiagram title="Choosing W and R with N = 3" chart={`flowchart TD
  Q["Which operation is more common?"] --> RH["Reads: set R = 1, W = 3"]
  Q --> WH["Writes: set W = 1, R = 3"]
  Q --> BAL["Both, and you want to survive failures: W = 2, R = 2"]
  RH --> RC["Reads are fast. One failed node blocks every write."]
  WH --> WC["Writes are fast. One failed node blocks every read."]
  BAL --> BC["Each side survives 1 failure. Reads see the latest write."]
`} legend="W plus R stays above N in all three choices. Only the split changes." />

You can shift the cost. A read-heavy service makes reads cheap and writes expensive. A write-heavy one does the opposite. The balanced choice with W = 2 and R = 2 is the common one, because both sides survive a node failure.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: when the numbers are not enough">

A quorum gives you overlap, not an order of operations. Two writes to the same key that reach different groups have no agreed order. Module 13 shows how a store lives with that using version vectors and merge rules. This module goes the other way: it adds a leader, so that all writes pass one gate and get one order.

A common rule of thumb is to pick the majority for both W and R when you do not know your load. It is the safest default, because the bigger quorum is slower but never wrong.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

Say: "With N copies, a write waits for W and a read asks R. If W plus R is more than N, every read group meets every write group, so a read sees the latest finished write. A majority for both is the default, and it survives the loss of fewer than half of the nodes."

An interviewer may ask: "Does R plus W above N give you linearizable reads?" The answer is no. A write that fails halfway can show up for one reader and not another. You need read repair or consensus.

</GoDeeper>
