Tijara is a small subscription shop (a made-up app). Every night at 02:00 a <GlossaryTerm term="scheduler" definition="A program that starts jobs at set times, for example 'charge every customer whose plan renews today'." /> bills the customers whose plan renews. A scheduler that fails would mean no bills, so the team runs two: a main one and a standby. The standby takes over if the main one goes quiet. Look at the picture below and ask yourself one everyday question: what would you check first?

## Two schedulers, one bill

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-two-schedulers" />

The main scheduler sends a "beat" to the standby to say it is alive. Then it freezes for 12 seconds. The standby hears nothing for 5 seconds, decides the main one is gone, and starts billing. The main one wakes up, does not know it was replaced, and bills again. Dana pays twice.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why this is not a rare bug">

Two copies of a scheduler are a sensible start. One copy alone is a single point of failure, a part whose failure stops the whole job. The standby is the fix for that. The new danger is that both copies may run at the same time. For a billing job, that is the worst case: every customer is charged twice.

A freeze of 12 seconds sounds unusual, but it is not. A program can stop for a long time because of a long garbage collection pause, a virtual machine that is paused while it moves to another host, a disk that stalls, or a busy machine that swaps memory to disk. While the program is frozen it does not run any code, so it cannot check the time or see that a newer scheduler exists. When it wakes up, its next line of code runs as if nothing happened.

The standby's rule, "no beat for 5 seconds means take over", looks reasonable. It is also the cause. Chapter 12.2 shows why: from outside, a frozen node, a slow node and a dead node all look the same.

A real company met the same shape of problem. On 2018-10-21 a network partition of 43 seconds separated two US data centres of GitHub. The tool that manages failover promoted databases on the other coast to be the main ones. Both sides then held writes that the other side lacked, and the service ran in a degraded state for 24 hours and 11 minutes. One of the follow-up changes was to stop promoting databases across regions automatically [S134].

Tijara, its schedulers and Dana are a course story, a composite and not a real company. The 12 second freeze and the 5 second wait are course numbers.

</GoDeeper>

You cannot tell a slow node from a dead one, so one node's opinion is not enough. A group has to decide, and a majority is the smallest group that can. Then the group must pick one leader and keep one shared order of events. That is called consensus. You build it in challenge 11.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the module covers">

Chapter 12.2 explains why silence is not proof of failure, and what a timeout costs in both directions. Chapter 12.3 introduces quorums: groups that must overlap, so two groups can never disagree. Chapter 12.4 shows leases and fencing tokens, the way to stop an old leader even when it does not know it is old.

Chapters 12.5 and 12.6 teach Raft, an algorithm that makes a group of servers agree on a leader and on a log, a list of commands in one fixed order. Chapter 12.7 shows Paxos, the older algorithm that Raft was designed to replace as a teaching tool. Chapter 12.8 covers gossip and the SWIM failure detector, which large clusters use to know who is alive. Chapter 12.9 shows how coordination services such as etcd and ZooKeeper give you these tools as a service, and how service discovery uses them.

Then you write the voting rules yourself in 12.10, and run a whole Raft cluster on a simulated network in challenge 11. At the end, the gate asks the question this story started: how does your job scheduler make sure only one copy runs?

Module 8 showed failover and split brain from the database side (8.5). This module shows the tools that make failover safe.
