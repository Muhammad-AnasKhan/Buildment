Tijara needs exactly one scheduler to run the nightly bills. Picking one is called leader election. The hard part is not the pick. The hard part is stopping the old leader after you pick a new one, even when the old leader has no idea it was replaced.

## A lease is a lock that expires

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-lease-handover" />

Client A asks the lock service for the lock and gets token 33 with a 10 second lease. A crashes. The ring fills while the lease runs out. Now the lock is free, and B gets it with token 34. A lease is a lock with an expiry time, so a crashed holder cannot block everyone. The token is a number that grows with every grant.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the lock needs an expiry">

A plain lock has no clock. If the holder crashes while holding it, nobody can ever take it. A lease fixes this by giving the lock a limited life. The holder must renew it, often with a small "keep-alive" message, before it ends. If the holder stops, the lease runs out and the lock moves on.

Real systems use leases everywhere. In etcd, a client asks for a lease with a time to live (TTL) and attaches keys to it. The keys are deleted when the lease expires. The etcd documentation says lease expiry is measured by wall-clock time [S54]. In Spanner, the Paxos group leader holds a leader lease of 10 seconds [S60]. In DynamoDB, each partition's replication group has a leader that holds a lease, and only the leader serves writes and strongly consistent reads [S44].

The token is the number that makes the rest of the chapter work. Every time the lock service grants the lock, it hands out a token larger than the last. Kleppmann's example uses 33 and 34 [S70]. Chubby, Google's lock service, calls the same idea a sequencer and says it carries the lock's generation number [S71].

</GoDeeper>

## The old leader still writes

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-stale-write" />

A froze, its lease ended, and B took over with token 34 and wrote x = B. When A wakes it still holds token 33 and writes x = A. Storage accepts the write, because nothing tells it that 33 is old. The old leader overwrote the new one. The lease worked. It just did not reach A.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why a check before the write is not enough">

Look at what A's code does: it checks "do I hold the lock?", then it writes. Between the check and the write, the process can freeze. A check is only true at the moment it is made. By the time the write leaves, the lease may be over. This gap between checking and acting cannot be closed from inside the client.

Kleppmann shows the same story with a file write: a client takes a lock, then pauses in a long garbage collection, the lock expires, another client takes it and writes, and then the first client writes too. The two writes corrupt the file [S70].

Clocks make it worse. The lock service counts the lease on its own clock, and the client counts on its own. If the client's clock runs slow, it believes it has time left when the service has already moved the lock. A scheme that relies on well-behaved clocks, such as Redlock, is open to this problem, and Kleppmann criticises it for depending on timing assumptions [S70].

The GitHub incident in 12.1 had the same structure: a tool decided another node was the main one, while the old main was still running [S134].

</GoDeeper>

## Turn on fencing, drag the pause

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-fencing-play" />

Drag the pause from 0 to 20 seconds. Up to 8 seconds, A wakes inside its lease and all is fine. From 9 seconds, A writes after B. Turn fencing off and A's old write wins. Turn it on and storage rejects token 33, because it has already seen 34. Find the shortest pause that breaks things.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the timeline behind the picture">

The model has a lease of 10 seconds. A gets the lock at 0 and freezes at 2 seconds. The lease ends at 10 seconds. B gets the lock at 10.5 seconds with token 34 and writes at 11 seconds. A wakes at 2 seconds plus the pause, and writes at once.

| Pause | A wakes at | Without fencing | With fencing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 s | 7 s | Fine: still in the lease | Fine |
| 8.5 s | 10.5 s | A writes, B has not written yet; nothing is lost | Same |
| 9 s | 11 s | Tie with B's write: either can win | Rejected if B came first |
| 14 s | 16 s | A overwrites B | A is rejected |
| 20 s | 22 s | A overwrites B | A is rejected |

Without fencing, the damage depends on timing. That is what makes these bugs rare, hard to reproduce, and costly. With fencing, the result does not depend on timing at all. Storage compares numbers, and the larger number wins.

The numbers are course numbers. A 14 second pause is a lot, but a long garbage collection or a stalled virtual machine can pause a process that long.

</GoDeeper>

## Storage must check the token

<MermaidDiagram title="Fencing with a token" chart={`sequenceDiagram
  participant A as Client A
  participant L as Lock service
  participant S as Storage
  participant B as Client B
  A->>L: get lock
  L-->>A: token 33
  A->>S: write x=A, token 33
  S-->>A: ok (highest token seen: 33)
  Note over A: A freezes. Its lease ends.
  B->>L: get lock
  L-->>B: token 34
  B->>S: write x=B, token 34
  S-->>B: ok (highest token seen: 34)
  Note over A: A wakes up
  A->>S: write x=A2, token 33
  S-->>A: rejected: 33 is older than 34
`} legend="Storage remembers the highest token it has seen and rejects anything lower." />

The lock service gives each holder a token. The client sends it with every write. Storage keeps the highest token it has seen, and rejects any write with a lower one. It does not matter what the old leader believes. The stale write is stopped at the last step, where the damage would happen.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what fencing needs, and what it cannot do">

Fencing needs three things. The lock service must give out increasing tokens. The client must attach the token to every write to the protected resource. And the resource must check it. If the resource is a database, you can add a column for the token and make the write conditional on it. In etcd, a transaction can compare a key's revision before it writes [S53], which gives the same guarantee: the write goes through only if the number is still the one you hold.

Fencing cannot help when the resource cannot check a token. If the protected action is calling an outside service, for example sending an SMS through a provider that has no such check, a stale call goes through. For those cases the protection is to make the action safe to repeat, with an idempotency key (module 6), and to keep the window small.

Fencing also does not choose the leader. It makes a wrong choice harmless. That is a good thing to say in a design discussion: the lease picks the leader quickly, and the fencing token makes sure a late or wrong pick cannot hurt the data [S70, S71].

</GoDeeper>

## The lock service needs agreement too

<MermaidDiagram title="Who guards the lock?" chart={`flowchart LR
  C1["Client A"] --> LS
  C2["Client B"] --> LS
  subgraph LS["Lock service: 3 or 5 nodes"]
    N1["Node 1"] --- N2["Node 2"] --- N3["Node 3"]
  end
  LS --> ST["Storage checks tokens"]
`} legend="The lock service is a small cluster. A majority of its nodes must agree before it hands out a token." />

One lock server would be a single point of failure. So the lock service is itself a small cluster, three or five nodes, where a majority must agree before each grant. Etcd, ZooKeeper and Chubby are built this way. The next chapters show how such a group agrees on one leader and one order.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the token is a log position in disguise">

If the lock service's nodes agree on an ordered log, the token can be the position in that log where the grant was recorded. It grows by itself and never repeats. That is the link to the next chapters: the agreement tool that elects a leader for your jobs also hands out the numbers that fence the old one.

Spanner's design shows the same link. A Paxos group has a leader that holds a lease of 10 seconds, and the paper reports that recovery after killing a zone took about 10 seconds, about the length of the lease [S60].

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

Say: "A lease lets a lock move when the holder crashes, but a paused holder does not know its lease ended. So every grant carries a growing token, and the storage rejects writes with an older token. The lock service is a small consensus cluster, so it is not a single point of failure."

An interviewer may ask: "Why not just check the lock before writing?" The check can be stale by the time the write lands, because the process may pause between the two. Only the storage can see the newest token, so only the storage can reject the old one.

</GoDeeper>
