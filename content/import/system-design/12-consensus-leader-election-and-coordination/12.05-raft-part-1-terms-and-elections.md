Raft is an algorithm that lets a group of servers agree on one leader and one log. Its authors designed it to be easier to learn than Paxos, and a study with students supports that [S68]. This chapter covers the first half: how the group picks a leader. The log comes in 12.6.

## Three roles, one leader

<MermaidDiagram title="The three roles of a Raft server" chart={`flowchart LR
  F["Follower: answers the leader"] -->|"no leader heard, timer ends"| C["Candidate: asks for votes"]
  C -->|"majority of votes"| L["Leader: sends heartbeats"]
  C -->|"tie: timer ends, new term"| C
  C -->|"hears a leader or a larger term"| F
  L -->|"sees a larger term"| F
`} legend="Every server starts as a follower. At most one leader exists in each term." />

Every server starts as a follower. A follower waits for a heartbeat, a small message that the leader sends often. If none comes before its timer ends, the follower becomes a candidate and asks for votes. With a majority of votes, it becomes the leader.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what each role does">

A follower is passive. It answers messages from leaders and candidates, and does nothing else. A leader handles every client request and sends heartbeats to all followers. A heartbeat is an AppendEntries message with no new entries in it; chapter 12.6 shows the full message. A candidate is a follower that has decided to run for leader.

All three roles are the same program. Any server can be in any role at any time. A cluster of five has one leader and four followers in the normal case, and the roles change when the leader fails.

The timer is the key. Raft requires the broadcast time (how long a message round takes) to be much shorter than the election timeout, and the election timeout to be much shorter than the time between server failures. The authors measured broadcast times between 0.5 ms and 20 ms and suggest election timeouts between 10 ms and 500 ms [S68].

</GoDeeper>

## The first timer to fire wins

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-election-after-crash" />

S1, the leader of term 1, crashes. Each follower waits a random time. S3's timer is shortest, 190 ms, so it fires first. S3 starts term 2, votes for itself, and asks the others. S2, S4 and S5 say yes. With its own vote that makes 4 of 5, so S3 becomes the leader.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: terms and the random wait">

A <GlossaryTerm term="term" definition="In Raft, a numbered period of time. Each term has at most one leader. A candidate starts a new term by adding 1 to the number it knows." /> is a number that grows. It acts as a logical clock for the cluster, like the Lamport clocks of module 10 [S68]. Every message carries the sender's term. A server that sees a larger term than its own adopts it at once.

When S3's timer ends, it adds 1 to its term, votes for itself, and sends a RequestVote message to every other server. A server grants a vote on a first come, first served basis, and at most one vote per term. A candidate needs a majority of all servers, not of the servers it can reach. In a cluster of five, that is 3.

The wait is random. The paper's example is a value between 150 and 300 ms, chosen again each time [S68]. Randomness spreads the timers out, so usually one follower fires well before the others and wins before they even start. Without it, all servers would fire together. Challenge 11 measures this.

</GoDeeper>

## A tie wastes a round, then a retry

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-split-vote" />

S2 and S3 time out together, and the votes split 2 and 2. Nobody has a majority, so the round ends. Both wait a new random time: 160 ms for S2, 290 ms for S3. S2 fires first and starts term 3. S3 sees the larger term, steps back, and votes for it. S2 wins term 3.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why a split vote is cheap, and when it is not">

A split vote costs one election timeout, about 150 to 300 ms with the paper's numbers. It does not break safety, because nobody became leader. The cluster just has no leader for a little longer. With random timers the chance of a tie each round is small, and the chance of many ties in a row is much smaller.

Challenge 11 measures it. On five nodes with messages that take 5 to 20 ms, a 150 to 300 ms range elected a leader in about one round, and the middle failover took 218 ms. A range of only 10 ms (150 to 160 ms) took about 6.8 rounds and a middle failover of 952 ms. With every timeout the same, a cold start never elected anyone in 5 seconds. The random part is not decoration.

There is a trade-off in the range. A wide range makes ties rarer but makes the slowest case slower. The lower end must stay above the heartbeat interval and the message time, or healthy followers will start elections for nothing.

</GoDeeper>

## Three questions before a vote

<MermaidDiagram title="How a server answers a RequestVote" chart={`flowchart TD
  R["RequestVote arrives: term T, candidate's log"] --> A{"Is T smaller than my term?"}
  A -->|yes| N1["Say no, and tell them my term"]
  A -->|no| B{"Did I already vote for someone else in term T?"}
  B -->|yes| N2["Say no"]
  B -->|no| C{"Is their log at least as up to date as mine?"}
  C -->|no| N3["Say no"]
  C -->|yes| Y["Say yes, and remember the vote"]
`} legend="If T is larger than my term, I first adopt T and forget any older vote." />

A server says yes only if three things hold. The candidate's term is not old. The server has not already voted for someone else in that term. And the candidate's log is at least as complete as its own. The last rule keeps a server with missing entries from becoming leader. You write these rules in 12.10.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: each rule protects something">

The first rule keeps old candidates out. A candidate from term 3, in a cluster already in term 5, is a server that missed the news. Its message gets a no and the cluster's term, so the candidate learns it is behind.

The second rule gives the guarantee called Election Safety: at most one leader per term [S68]. Two leaders in one term would need two different majorities of voters. Any two majorities share a server, as you saw with quorums in 12.3, and that server votes only once per term. So both candidates cannot win.

The third rule protects committed entries. A server compares the term of the last entry in each log first, and if the terms are equal, the longer log is more up to date. Entries that a majority has already stored cannot be lost, because any majority that votes must include a server that has them, and that server refuses a candidate that lacks them. The next chapter returns to this.

If a vote is granted, the server also resets its own election timer, because a leader is likely coming. It does not reset the timer when it says no. Otherwise a candidate with a stale log could keep everyone from running.

</GoDeeper>

## A larger term always wins

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-old-leader-steps-down" />

S1 was the leader of term 1 but got cut off. The others elected S3 in term 2. When the link returns, S3's heartbeat carries term 2. S1 compares 2 with its own 1 and becomes a follower. Any server that sees a larger term obeys it. A message with a smaller term is rejected.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: terms are the fence for Raft">

Terms do for Raft what fencing tokens did in 12.4. The old leader may still think it leads, and may even keep sending messages, but followers compare terms and ignore it. When it sees a newer term it steps down.

There is a cost on the other side. A follower that is cut off keeps timing out, starting elections, and raising its term. When it comes back, its large term makes the real leader step down, and the cluster holds an election it did not need. You can see this in the playable below. Real implementations add a pre-vote step, in which a server first asks whether it could win before it raises its term.

## Kill, split and cut: drive the cluster

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-raft-election-play" />

Five servers, one leader. Press Kill the leader and watch the terms and the votes. After a Reset, press Force a tie to see a split vote and a retry. Cut off S4 and watch its term climb alone. Then reconnect it and see what the old term does to the leader. Read the "no leader" counter each time.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what to look for">

After you kill the leader, the cluster has no leader for about one election timeout plus one round of messages. In challenge 11 the middle value was 218 ms, and the 99th percentile was 572 ms. During that time, writes wait. If a majority of servers is down, there is no leader at all, and the live servers keep starting elections with growing terms, but they can never win.

When you cut a follower off, the cluster stays healthy, because it still has a majority. The cut-off follower's term rises by 1 every timeout. When it returns with a large term, the leader steps down. The returning server has an old log, so it cannot win, but the election it triggers costs the cluster a short pause.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

Say: "Raft has one leader per term. A follower that hears no heartbeat for a random time becomes a candidate, adds 1 to the term, and asks for votes. It needs a majority. Each server votes once per term, so two leaders in one term would need two majorities, which must overlap. Random timers make a tie unlikely."

An interviewer may ask: "What stops an old leader from doing damage?" Followers reject any message with a smaller term, and the old leader steps down when it sees a larger one. For storage outside the cluster, use the term, or a fencing token, as a check.

</GoDeeper>
