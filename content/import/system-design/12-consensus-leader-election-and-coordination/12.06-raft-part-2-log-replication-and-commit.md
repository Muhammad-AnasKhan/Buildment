A leader alone is not enough. The cluster must also agree on what happened, in what order. Raft keeps this in a log: a list of commands, each with a number and a term. Every server applies the same commands in the same order, so they end in the same state.

## Copy it, count it, commit it

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-log-commit" />

A client sends x = 5 to the leader. The leader adds it to its log and copies it to S2 and S3. S2 answers first. Now 2 of 3 servers hold the entry, a majority. The leader commits it and tells the client. S3 stores it a moment later. The leader never waits for the slowest server.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: log, commit and apply are three steps">

An entry first goes into the leader's log. That is not enough to be safe, because the leader may crash. The entry is committed when the leader has copied it to a majority of the servers. A committed entry will not be lost: any later leader must have it, as the election rule in 12.5 ensures [S68].

Once committed, the entry is applied to the state machine, which is the real data the service keeps: here, a table where x becomes 5. Only then does the leader reply to the client. Followers learn the commit point from the next message the leader sends, which carries the leader's commit index.

A new leader starts by adding an empty entry of its own term. When that entry commits, every older entry before it commits too. This is how a new leader finishes entries that its predecessor had stored on a majority but not yet marked as committed.

{/* source needed: the empty entry at the start of a leader's term and the leaderCommit field (S68, section 8 and Figure 2); not in the dossier. */}

A leader only counts copies to commit entries of its own term. It never commits an older term's entry just because a majority holds it [S68]. The older entry commits indirectly, when a newer entry of the current term commits after it.

</GoDeeper>

## A cut-off leader cannot commit

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-old-leader-cut-off" />

The network splits. S1 and S2 are on one side, S3, S4 and S5 on the other. S1 still thinks it leads and stores y = 9 from the client. It copies it to S2, which is 2 of 5. That is not a majority, so it cannot commit. The other side elects S3 for term 2 and commits z = 7 with 3 of 5.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: two leaders at once, but only one can commit">

For a while the cluster has two servers that call themselves leader: S1 in term 1 and S3 in term 2. Raft allows this. What it forbids is two leaders in the same term, and two leaders that both commit. S1 cannot commit anything, because it cannot reach 3 servers. The side with the majority is the only one that makes progress.

The client of S1 gets no answer, since the leader only replies after a commit. After a timeout it retries, often against the new leader. The retry can produce a duplicate. Raft aims for linearizable behaviour, so a retried command must run only once [S68]. A common way is to tag each command with a client id and a serial number, so a server can skip a command it has already run.

In challenge 11, part 3, five writes sent to a leader that was cut off with one friend were stored on 2 of 5 servers and never committed, in 100 of 100 runs. This is the same trade as in the quorum chapter: the smaller side stops, so only one truth exists.

</GoDeeper>

## Healing erases the uncommitted entry

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-log-overwritten" />

The link returns. S3 sends its term 2 entries to S1 and S2. They do not match what S1 and S2 hold, so both drop y = 9 and then copy z = 7. All five logs now agree. The client of S1 never got a "saved" reply, so nothing that was promised has been lost. Only an entry on a majority is promised.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why overwriting is safe">

Overwriting sounds scary, but it only ever removes entries that were not committed. A committed entry is on a majority of servers. Any server that can become leader must collect votes from a majority, and one of those voters has the committed entry. The voter refuses a candidate whose log is behind, as you saw in the third voting rule. So a leader always holds every committed entry. This is called Leader Completeness [S68].

Because of this, a leader never needs to change its own log. It only appends. Followers adjust to match it. The term numbers in each entry let a follower see whether its log is the same as the leader's at a given place.

In challenge 11, part 3, after the split healed, all five logs were the same in 100 of 100 runs, every one of the five writes the cut-off leader had accepted was gone, and every write committed before the cut survived.

</GoDeeper>

## The leader repairs a follower's log

<MermaidDiagram title="Finding where a follower's log matches" chart={`sequenceDiagram
  participant L as Leader
  participant F as Follower
  L->>F: AppendEntries: prev index 5, prev term 2, entry 6
  F-->>L: No: my index 5 has term 1
  L->>F: AppendEntries: prev index 4, prev term 2, entries 5 and 6
  F-->>L: Yes: index 4 matches, I replaced my entry 5 and stored 6
  Note over L,F: From here on, the logs are the same
`} legend="Each message names the entry just before the new ones. The follower refuses unless it has that entry." />

Each AppendEntries message says: "the entry just before these new ones is index 5 with term 2." The follower refuses unless its own log has exactly that. The leader then backs up one entry and tries again, until the two logs match. After that, the follower deletes anything that conflicts and copies the rest.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the consistency check, step by step">

The check gives a strong guarantee. If two logs have an entry with the same index and term, they hold the same command, and the logs are the same in every entry before it [S68]. The leader creates at most one entry per index in a term, and entries never move. By following the check one message at a time, the property carries forward through the whole log.

The leader keeps a nextIndex for each follower: the next entry to send. It starts at the end of the leader's own log. When a follower refuses, the leader lowers nextIndex by 1 and tries again. When a follower accepts, the leader raises it. Backing up one entry at a time is slow after a long outage, so real implementations let the follower report a hint, such as the first index of the conflicting term. Challenge 11 backs up one at a time.

The follower also needs to be careful. It deletes its own entry only when an existing entry conflicts with a new one: same index, different term. If it already holds the same entry, it keeps it. A late or repeated message must not cut off entries that already match.

</GoDeeper>

## Send, cut, heal: watch logs repair

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-raft-log-play" />

Five servers show their logs as boxes, each marked with its term. Press Send an entry and watch it reach a majority and turn solid. Press Cut S1 and S2 off, then send an entry to S1. See it stay dashed. Wait for S3 to lead, send an entry to S3, then press Heal and watch the new entries overwrite the dashed ones.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: questions to try on the playable">

Try these in order. First, send three entries with no failure and read the commit line: it moves right after a majority holds each entry. Second, kill two followers and send another entry: it still commits, because 3 of 5 are alive. Third, kill a third server: the entry stays hollow, and the leader keeps retrying. Fourth, cut the network with the leader on the small side, and send entries to both sides. Last, heal, and compare the logs.

The rule to remember is simple. An entry is promised only once it is on a majority. Before that, it may be lost, and nobody was told it was safe.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

Say: "The leader appends a command to its log and sends it to the followers. Once a majority has stored it, the leader marks it committed, applies it, and answers the client. A follower only accepts entries that match the entry before them, so logs repair themselves. A cut-off leader can store entries but never commit them, and the new leader overwrites them."

An interviewer may ask: "What if the leader crashes after committing but before replying?" The client times out and retries. The cluster must recognise the retry as a duplicate, so each command carries a client id and a serial number.

</GoDeeper>
