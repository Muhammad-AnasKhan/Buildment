## Your gate answer: only one copy runs

This is the question that started the module. Tijara has a job scheduler that bills customers every night. You run two copies, so a crash does not stop the bills. How does your design make sure only one copy runs at a time? In an interview this comes up whenever a design has a job that must not run twice: billing, email campaigns, report generation, or a database failover. In a real team it comes up the first time a customer is charged twice.

A weak answer says "the standby takes over if it stops hearing from the main one" and stops. You know from this module why that fails: silence does not tell a slow main scheduler from a dead one, so the main one may wake up and run too. A strong answer is built from the tools of the module, in order. First, say how the two copies decide who leads. They should not decide by their own timers. They race to take a lease in a small coordination service such as etcd, which is itself a group that needs a majority of its nodes, and only the copy that wins runs the job. Say how long the lease is, and what a short lease and a long lease each cost, using the trade-off from chapter 12.2.

Second, say what happens to a copy that is paused when its lease ends. This is the part most answers skip. The lease cannot reach a frozen process. So the job must be protected where it does its damage: the lock service hands out a token that grows with every grant, and the billing database or payment step rejects a write that carries an older token. If the final step cannot check a token, for example a call to an outside payment provider, protect it with an idempotency key built from the customer and the date, so that a second attempt for the same night is recognised and skipped.

Third, say what the design does when the coordination service has no majority. The honest answer is that no copy gets a lease, no copy runs, and the bills are late until the service is back. That is a choice, and it is the right one for billing: late is better than double. Name the number that decides how late: how long your lease is, how long an election takes. Your challenge 11 part 1 numbers give a feel for the election part, a few hundred milliseconds when the cluster is healthy.

Fourth, name one failure that your design does not remove, and what you do about it. For example, the two coordination nodes in one data centre may be cut off from the third, and the lease may be lost for a while. Or the fencing token may be added to the database but not to the email step. A design that claims to have no gaps is not finished.

Use the numbers you measured in challenge 11 where they help. For example, you can say that a cut-off leader still believed it led in all 100 runs, which is why a belief inside the process can never be the check. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required; a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.

A good cost line is short and has a unit. For the lease it can read: "a 10 second lease means a crashed scheduler is replaced within 10 seconds, and a pause longer than that needs the token check." For the fencing token it can read: "one extra column and one extra comparison on every billing write." For the majority rule it can read: "if two of three coordination nodes are down, nothing is billed until one returns." Compare your own lines with these. If a line says "some delay" or "a bit more complexity", it is not finished.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right choice. Two students can choose different tools and both pass, as long as each one names how the leader is chosen, how a stale leader is stopped, what happens without a majority, and what the design still cannot do. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens if the clock on the lock service runs slow, or how you would test the design. Answer them by editing and resubmitting.
