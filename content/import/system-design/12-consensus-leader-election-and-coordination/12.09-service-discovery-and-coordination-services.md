Tijara's payments service runs on four instances, and the list changes all day. One is added when traffic grows, one is removed in a deploy, one crashes. A caller must find the instances that are alive right now. Service discovery answers the question: where is the payments service at this moment?

## Where is the payments service?

<MermaidDiagram title="Register, look up, watch" chart={`flowchart LR
  I1["payments-1"] -->|"register and heartbeat"| R["Registry: a small consensus cluster"]
  I2["payments-2"] -->|"register and heartbeat"| R
  C["Caller"] -->|"look up, or watch for changes"| R
  C -->|"calls a live instance"| I1
`} legend="Instances write their address into the registry. Callers read it. If an instance stops beating, its entry expires." />

Each instance writes its address into a registry when it starts, and renews it on a timer. If it crashes, the renewal stops and the entry expires. A caller reads the registry to find live instances, or asks to be told when the list changes. The registry is a coordination service: a small, reliable store that many services share.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what a coordination service offers">

A coordination service is a small cluster that keeps a little data safely and agrees on it with a consensus algorithm. It is not a database for your business data. It holds things such as who the leader is, which instances are alive, and configuration that must be the same everywhere. A majority of its nodes must be alive for it to make progress, as in 12.3.

Three tools make it useful. Leases let a key vanish when its owner stops renewing. Watches tell a client when a key changes. Transactions let a client write only if the key still has the value it expects. etcd offers all three: keys can be attached to a lease, a watch streams changes, and a transaction is an atomic If, Then, Else with comparisons on revision numbers [S53].

ZooKeeper and Consul offer the same ideas in their own forms. ZooKeeper has ephemeral nodes, which are deleted when the client's session ends, and watches. Consul has a service catalog with health checks.

## Register, beat, expire, watch

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-registry-play" />

Three instances each renew a 10 second lease every 3 seconds. Tap Crash on one right after its beat and count the seconds until its entry disappears. Then tap Lose beat twice on a healthy one and see it stay. A third tap removes it. Find the shortest lease that survives two lost beats.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: lease length, beat interval and the stale window">

Three numbers matter. The lease length is how long an entry lives without a renewal. The beat interval is how often the instance renews. The stale window is how long a dead instance stays in the list.

If an instance crashes right after a beat, its entry stays for the whole lease: 10 seconds here. If it crashes just before the next beat, it stays for about the lease minus the beat interval: 7 seconds. On average a dead instance lingers about 8.5 seconds. For those seconds callers may be sent to it, so callers need timeouts and retries on another instance.

A beat can be lost, so the lease must be longer than one interval. With a 10 second lease and a 3 second interval, beats are at 0, 3, 6 and 9. Two lost beats in a row (3 and 6) still leave the beat at 9, in time. Three in a row (3, 6 and 9) let the lease end at 10. A healthy rule is a lease of at least three beat intervals.

This is the same trade as in 12.2. A short lease removes dead instances fast and removes healthy but slow ones by mistake. A long lease is calm and slow.

The registry's lease is also the lock lease of 12.4. It runs on the registry's clock, and etcd says lease TTLs are counted in wall-clock time [S54].

</GoDeeper>

## A watch tells the client

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m12-registry-watch" />

The client asks to watch /pay. When payments-2 registers, the registry pushes an event and the client adds it. Later payments-2 crashes. Its lease runs out, the key is deleted, and the registry pushes a second event. The client never asked again. It was told.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: watches versus polling, and their limits">

Without a watch, every caller would poll: read the list every second. With a hundred callers that is a hundred reads a second for a list that changes a few times a day. A watch turns this around. The registry pushes a change to each watcher when it happens.

The etcd documentation says a watch event arrives in about 10 ms in a healthy cluster, and puts no bound on it otherwise [S54]. So treat a watch as fast but not guaranteed. A careful client combines a watch with an occasional full read, and also remembers the revision number it last saw, so that after a break it can ask for everything that changed since then.

etcd reads are linearizable by default [S54]: a read returns the latest committed value, not an old copy. That means a read needs the cluster's leader, and it fails when the cluster has no majority. If the registry loses its majority, callers should keep using the last list they had instead of failing every call.

</GoDeeper>

## A leader election recipe

<MermaidDiagram title="Electing a leader with a key and a lease" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Every candidate tries: write /leader only if nobody holds it, with its lease"] --> W{"Did my write succeed?"}
  W -->|yes| L["I am the leader. I keep renewing my lease."]
  W -->|no| O["Someone else leads. I watch /leader."]
  L -->|"I crash or pause: the lease ends"| D["The key is deleted"]
  D --> N["Watchers wake up and try again"]
  N --> A
`} legend="The registry decides who wrote first. The lease makes sure a dead leader's key goes away." />

This is how Tijara can pick one scheduler. Every scheduler tries to write the /leader key in one transaction that succeeds only if nobody holds it, and attaches its lease. One wins. The others watch the key. When the leader's lease ends, the key vanishes and the others race again. Add the fencing token from 12.4, and the old leader cannot do damage.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the recipe in etcd and ZooKeeper, and what is still missing">

In etcd, the "write only if nobody holds it" step is a transaction [S53]. The key is attached to the winner's lease. The winner keeps the lease alive. Others watch the key and retry when it is deleted. The revision number of the winning write grows with each election, so it can serve as a fencing token: storage rejects a write that carries an older revision.

ZooKeeper uses a similar recipe with ephemeral and sequential nodes: each candidate creates a node with a growing number, the lowest number is the leader, and each other candidate watches the one just before it, so one failure wakes only one waiter.

{/* source needed: the ZooKeeper leader election recipe with ephemeral sequential nodes; ZooKeeper recipes documentation, not in the dossier. */}

The recipe does not remove the pause problem. A leader that freezes loses its lease, and the key is deleted, but the frozen process does not know. It needs the fencing check at the storage, as in 12.4. The coordination service makes the pick fast and safe. The token makes a late pick harmless.

</GoDeeper>

## Often it is just a name

<MermaidDiagram title="Discovery through names" chart={`flowchart LR
  C["Caller"] -->|"payments.shop.svc"| D["DNS"]
  D -->|"addresses of healthy instances"| C
  K["Kubernetes or Cloud Map"] -->|"keeps the healthy list"| D
`} legend="The caller only knows a name. The platform keeps the list behind the name current." />

Many teams never touch a registry directly. In Kubernetes, each Service gets a DNS name such as my-svc.my-namespace.svc. In AWS, Cloud Map maps names to resources and answers with healthy ones only. The caller uses a name and the platform keeps the list. The cost is the DNS cache: a caller may keep an old answer until its time to live ends.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: when to use which, and the AWS view">

Use plain DNS names when callers can live with a short delay and you do not need leader election. Use a registry with watches when callers need to react within a second, or when you also need locks and leader election. Use a load balancer in front of instances, as in module 4, when you want fast removal of failed instances by health checks without any change on the caller's side.

A registry has one more risk: if all instances fail their health check at once, for example because a shared dependency is down, the registry may remove them all and send callers nowhere. Module 4 explained this risk, and why local health checks and failing open are safer [S19].

**In AWS.** Cloud Map keeps a registry of names and resources and, by default, returns only healthy ones [S130]. Each DynamoDB partition runs a replication group with a Multi-Paxos leader that holds a lease [S44], which is the same pattern as an etcd cluster, run for you. When you need leader election for your own jobs on AWS, you can build the recipe above on a store that supports conditional writes with an expiry, or use a coordination service you run yourself.

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

Say: "Instances register in a registry such as etcd with a lease and renew it on a timer. If one stops, its lease ends and its entry is deleted, and watchers are told. The registry is a small consensus cluster, so it survives a minority of failures. For a single active worker, instances race to write a leader key with a lease, and I add a fencing token."

An interviewer may ask: "What if the registry itself is down?" Callers keep using their last known list and retry on failure. They do not crash. Writers and leader election wait until the registry has a majority again.

</GoDeeper>
