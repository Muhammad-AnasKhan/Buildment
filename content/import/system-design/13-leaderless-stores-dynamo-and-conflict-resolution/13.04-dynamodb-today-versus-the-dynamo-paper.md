Dynamo is a 2007 paper from Amazon. DynamoDB is the AWS service you can use today. They share a name and a goal. They are different machines inside. It is easy to mix them up, so this chapter makes the difference exact. By the end you can explain in two sentences why DynamoDB can refuse a stale write, and why the store in the paper could not.

## Same name, two different designs

<MermaidDiagram title="The paper and the service" chart={`flowchart LR

The Dynamo paper describes a leaderless store with sloppy quorums, hints and vector clocks, where the application merges siblings. The DynamoDB service is built differently: each partition has three replicas and a leader. Never say that DynamoDB uses sloppy quorums and vector clocks. That describes the paper.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the service kept, and what it changed">

The 2022 paper on DynamoDB says it was motivated by the team's experience with its predecessor, Dynamo [S44]. It kept the goals: a store that stays fast and available as it grows. It changed the machinery. Instead of a leaderless ring that every customer would have to run and tune, DynamoDB is a multi-tenant managed service. Each table is cut into partitions by hashing the partition key [S40]. Each partition has three replicas that form a replication group, which runs Multi-Paxos with a leader holding a lease [S44]. You met Paxos and leases in chapters 12.4 and 12.7.

| | Dynamo paper (2007) | DynamoDB service |
|---|---|---|
| Who takes a write | Any node in the key's list | The leader of the key's partition |
| How a write is acknowledged | W nodes confirm | A quorum stores the log record |
| Conflicts | Siblings with vector clocks | None inside a partition; refused if a condition fails |
| Membership | Gossip | Managed by the service |
| You run it | Yes, inside Amazon | No, AWS runs it |

The service handled 89.2 million requests per second at its peak on Prime Day 2021, according to the 2022 paper [S44]. That is why the managed design matters: it hides the partitions, the elections and the repairs from the customer.

</GoDeeper>

## In DynamoDB the leader takes the write

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m13-ddb-write" />

The client sends a put to the partition's leader. The leader sends a log record to its two replicas and answers OK when a quorum has stored it: itself and one replica. One leader puts every write to a partition in one order, so there is no sibling to merge.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: quorum, leader, and what happens when the leader fails">

A write is acknowledged when a quorum of the replication group has stored the log record [S44]. With three replicas, a quorum is two. The leader is one of them, so it needs just one more. The third replica catches up from the log.

Only the leader serves writes and strongly consistent reads [S44]. It holds a lease, so a replica that cannot reach it for a while can start an election and become the new leader. During the election, writes to that partition wait. That is the cost of having a leader, the one Dynamo avoided with no leader at all. Chapters 8.5 and 12.4 describe the same trade: a leader gives one order of writes, and a failure costs a short pause.

</GoDeeper>

## Eventual reads may lag, strong reads do not

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m13-ddb-reads" />

A write needs a quorum, but a read may go to any copy. An eventually consistent read may return the older value, as the replica does here. A strongly consistent read goes to the leader, which always has the latest value. You choose per request.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: two kinds of read, and other places that lag">

DynamoDB offers both kinds of read [S44]. An eventually consistent read can be served by a replica that has not yet applied the latest log record. This is the replication lag of chapter 8.4, inside the service. A strongly consistent read is served by the leader, so it sees every acknowledged write.

Other parts of the service lag too. A global secondary index, a second view of the table keyed on another attribute, is updated asynchronously and is eventually consistent [S41]. A query on it can miss a write that the table already holds. In an interview, say which read you used when you claim a result is fresh.

</GoDeeper>

## A conditional write refuses a stale update

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m13-ddb-conditional" />

Because one leader orders the writes, DynamoDB can check a condition: change this item only if it still has version 4. Dina's stale save is refused instead of overwriting Hana's. Dina reads again and retries. This is the compare-and-set of chapter 7.5, applied to a cart.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a refusal instead of a sibling">

A conditional write stops the lost update [S42]. A version number that the app keeps is the usual condition. DynamoDB also accepts a condition on any attribute. A conditional write is safe to repeat when its condition checks the attribute it changes. An unconditional atomic counter, such as "add 1", is not safe to repeat: run it twice and you add 2 [S42].

Notice the difference from the Dynamo paper. The paper's store accepted both writes and kept two siblings, because it never refused. DynamoDB can refuse because a leader sees every write to the partition. Refusing keeps one history and costs the app a retry loop. Siblings keep every write and cost the app a merge.

</GoDeeper>

## A hot partition stays capped

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m13-hot-partition" />

Drag the hot key's writes per second. Without adaptive capacity, the busy partition is held to its even share, 500, and the rest is rejected. Turn adaptive capacity on: the partition can borrow what the others leave unused, up to its own ceiling of 1,000. Push past 1,000, and nothing helps.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: throughput dilution, adaptive capacity and the ceiling">

The DynamoDB paper names hot partitions and throughput dilution as the most common early problems [S44]. Throughput dilution is what the prediction showed: a table's throughput is divided across its partitions, so as a table grows into more partitions each partition's share shrinks, and one busy key can be throttled while the table as a whole is far below its limit.

{/* source needed: the table's provisioned throughput is split evenly across its partitions, and adaptive capacity lets a partition use unused throughput from the others (DynamoDB documentation on adaptive capacity and the 2022 paper's section on throughput dilution; the dossier cites only "hot partitions and throughput dilution"). The 500 share and the 2,000 total in the picture are illustrative. */}

Adaptive capacity softens this by letting a busy partition use throughput that other partitions are not using. It cannot lift the ceiling of a single partition, which is designed for up to 3,000 read units and 1,000 write units per second [S40]. A key that needs more than that is beyond what any one partition can do. The fix is in the key, not in the table: split it into several keys, as in chapter 9.4, or put a cache in front. Consistent hashing balances key ranges, not a single hot key [S45, S44].

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

Say: "DynamoDB is not the Dynamo paper. A table is split into partitions by the hash of the partition key. Each partition has three replicas and a leader; the leader takes writes, and a quorum stores each log record. Reads are eventually consistent or, from the leader, strongly consistent, and conditional writes stop lost updates. The paper's sloppy quorums and vector clocks are not in the service."

A follow-up an interviewer may ask: "Why does a hot key still throttle?" A good answer: the ceiling is per partition, about 1,000 writes a second, not per table. Spare table capacity and adaptive capacity cannot lift it, so I spread the key over several keys or cache the reads.

</GoDeeper>
