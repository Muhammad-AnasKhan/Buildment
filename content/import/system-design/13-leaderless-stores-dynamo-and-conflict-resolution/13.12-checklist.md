## Your gate answer: three features, three conflict strategies

This is the question that started the module. Two people change the same thing at the same time, in a store that never says no. What should the thing look like afterwards? The answer depends on the feature, not on the store. In an interview this comes up whenever a design says "eventual consistency" or "last write wins": the interviewer wants to hear you separate the features and pay for each one with a different cost.

Choose a conflict strategy for each of these three features of Souq (a made-up shop). The first is the shopping cart: shoppers add and remove items from a phone and a laptop. The second is the like counter on a product page. The third is a customer's delivery address, one value that a person types and expects to keep.

A weak answer picks one strategy for all three, or says "use timestamps". A strong answer picks per feature and gives a reason that uses a number. For the cart, you have the strongest evidence of the module: your part 3 and part 4 results show last write wins losing adds and siblings bringing removed items back, and the OR-Set getting every cart right at the price of tags that grow. For the like counter, the question is whether a lost like matters, and what a G-Counter or PN-Counter costs in slots. For the address, ask whether two people can really change it at once, and what happens if one change is dropped silently: that might be a parcel to the wrong house, so a conditional write or a single writer may be the honest choice, even though it can refuse.

Name the cost of each choice in words and, where you can, in numbers from challenge 12. A cost line is short and has a unit or a count. For last write wins it can read: "176 of 200 adds lost in part 3 when one clock was 100 ms ahead." For an OR-Set it can read: "1,001 tags for a cart of one item after 1,000 add and remove rounds, and no cleanup without every replica agreeing." For a conditional write it can read: "a retry loop in the client, and a refused save when two people edit at once." If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished.

Then say when you would change your mind. For each feature, give one trigger: a number, an event or a change in the product that would make you pick another strategy. For example: "if likes become a paid feature that is audited, I would move the counter behind a leader." A mentor reads for method, not for one right answer. Two students can pick different strategies for the same feature and both pass, as long as each one names what is lost or paid, gives a number, and says what would change the choice.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is calling a CRDT the answer for everything: it cannot hold a rule like a balance above zero or a unique name. The second is using last write wins without saying which writes it is allowed to lose. The third is confusing the Dynamo paper with the DynamoDB service when you cite an example: sloppy quorums and vector clocks belong to the paper.

A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens to your cart choice if a shopper has a third device, or how you would test that the counter loses nothing. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required; a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.
