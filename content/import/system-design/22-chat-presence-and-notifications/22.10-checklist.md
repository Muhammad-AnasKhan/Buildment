## Your gate answer: Rafeeq

This is the case study again on a different product. Rafeeq (a made-up service) is a food delivery app. When an order is placed, the customer and the rider get a small chat room for that order, so they can say "I am at the blue door". The room closes when the order is delivered. The app also sends the customer four alerts for each order: accepted, rider assigned, rider nearby and delivered. Here are the assumptions. There are 2 million orders a day, and at dinner time the rate is 4 times the daily average. An order lasts about 40 minutes. A room holds about 12 messages in that time. Chats are kept for 30 days. Each message is stored with 300 bytes of detail. About 20% of customers have no valid push token, and for them the "rider nearby" alert must go by SMS. An SMS provider accepts 100 messages a second. In an interview, this is the "design a chat and an order tracker" question, and it is the same loop you just ran.

A weak answer lists boxes: a WebSocket server, a database, a push service, and stops. A strong answer starts from the numbers and lets them decide which boxes to draw. Work out the orders a second at dinner time. Then use Little's law from chapter 2.5: the number of orders in progress is the rate of new orders times how long each lasts. Each order in progress has a customer and a rider, and each of them may hold a line. From that, count the lines and the gateways at 50,000 lines each, and the messages a second. Chapter 22.1 showed that Dardasha's lines decided almost everything. Does Rafeeq have that problem, or is something else the biggest number? Say so, and say when you would change your mind.

Then decide how a room works. Say how a message is numbered and how a retry is recognised, and whether a room needs the full machinery of chapter 22.4 or something simpler. A room has two people, so ask what could still go wrong with order. Say what a customer in a basement sees, and what happens when their phone comes back. Decide whether presence ("the rider is online") is worth a feature here, and what it would cost. For the alerts, say how the service stays inside the provider's limit, what happens to a "rider nearby" SMS if the provider fails for 5 minutes, and how long that alert is worth sending. Say what happens to your most important block when it fails.

Be explicit about one trade-off. Write it in the same shape each time: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would switch. A good cost line has a number or a name. For example: "a lookup of about 0.5 ms for each delivery, and a registry to keep alive", or "an alert that can arrive up to 10 seconds late, because the notifier waits to see if the customer has already read the chat". If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished.

A good order for 250 words is the order of the loop. Numbers come first, in two or three lines. Then the room and the alerts, which are the sketch and the deep dive. Then the trade-off, and last the failure. If you run short of words, cut the sketch, not the numbers or the trade-off.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is copying Dardasha's design without checking the numbers, so a presence system and a 60-gateway tier appear that nothing in the estimate asks for. The second is sending an SMS alert after a provider outage without a time limit, so a customer gets "your rider is nearby" 15 minutes after the food arrived. The third is forgetting the dedupe key: whichever queue you pick, a duplicate event must not send two alerts.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right design. Two students can choose differently and both pass, as long as each one starts from the numbers, ties the design to the biggest number, names the failure that the choice answers, and says when they would change their mind. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens when the registry is down, or how you would stop a rider from being shown as online after they close the app. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, about 250 words. A recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.
