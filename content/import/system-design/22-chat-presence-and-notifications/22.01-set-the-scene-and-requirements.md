Dardasha is a chat app (a fictional service). People write to one friend or to a group, see when a message has arrived, and get an alert when the app is closed. This module runs the design loop from chapter 1.4 on Dardasha, then on the alert system behind it. This first chapter does the first two steps: say what Dardasha must do, then turn that into numbers.

## A message is easy to send

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m22-send-a-message" />

Layla sends a message. Dardasha saves it and tells her: one tick. Then it reaches Sami's phone, and Layla sees two ticks. With two people, nothing here is hard. The hard part is doing it for 10 million people a day, and for a phone that is switched off. What would you try first for the phone that is off?

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: clarify first, the way chapter 1.4 does">

Before any numbers, ask what Dardasha must do and what it will not do. Here is the scope the product owner agreed. All of it is a course choice, because Dardasha is made up.

**Dardasha does.** It sends text messages (up to 4,000 characters) in one-to-one chats and in groups of up to 500 people. It shows three facts about each message: sent, delivered and read. It delivers messages to a phone that was switched off, when the phone comes back. It shows who is online. It sends an alert to a phone when the app is closed. Its accounts system also sends email and SMS (text messages) for login codes and a weekly summary.

**Dardasha does not, for now.** It has no voice or video calls, no photos or files, no search, and no end-to-end encryption. Each is a real feature. A real chat app needs a plan for encryption in particular. The course leaves them out so the design stays readable.

**Fast.** A message to a friend who is online should arrive in under 200 ms at p99 (chapter 2.4), measured from the sender's phone to the friend's phone.

**Reliable.** Once the sender sees one tick, the message is never lost. Dardasha is available 99.95% of the time, about 22 minutes of trouble in a month.

**Ordered.** Everyone in a chat sees the messages in the same order.

**Honest about.** Online status may be a minute late, and a read tick may arrive late. Neither one hurts anybody.

**Limits.** A team of six and a cloud account.

The "never lost" line and the "same order" line will decide most of chapter 22.4. The "online" line is much more expensive than it looks, and chapter 22.4 shows why.

</GoDeeper>

## Drag the users, find the load

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m22-estimate-play" />

Drag the daily users, the messages each person sends, and the share of phones online at the busiest time. Messages are about 4,600 a second on average and 14,000 at the busiest moment. But the open lines are the bigger surprise: 3 million phones are connected at once. The ringed card is the number that drives the design.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the working, and what is an assumption">

Nobody gave Dardasha these numbers, so each one is written down as an assumption: 10 million daily users, 40 messages sent by each user a day, a busiest second that is 3 times the average, an average of 3 recipients for each message (a one-to-one chat has 1, a group has more), 1.5 phones or tablets for each user, 20% of devices connected at the busiest time, and 300 bytes stored for each message with its details. The method is the one from chapter 2.3: start from people, divide by seconds, keep the unit next to every number.

| Quantity | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Messages a day | 10,000,000 x 40 | 400 million |
| Messages a second, average | 400,000,000 / 86,400 | about 4,630 |
| Messages a second, peak | 4,630 x 3 | about 13,900, planned as 14,000 |
| Deliveries a second, peak | 13,900 x 3 recipients | about 42,000 |
| Devices | 10,000,000 x 1.5 | 15 million |
| Open connections, peak | 15,000,000 x 20% | 3 million |
| Gateway servers | 3,000,000 / 50,000 per gateway | 60 |
| Storage a year | 400,000,000 x 365 x 300 B | about 44 TB |
| With three copies | 44 TB x 3 | about 131 TB |

A gateway is a server that holds the open connections to phones (chapter 22.2). In the plan, one gateway holds 50,000 connections, and the most it can hold is 100,000. Both are course numbers. Chapter 3.5 says that with WebSockets the number that sizes your servers is connected users, and chapter 3.6 explains what limits one server.

Now ask which number drives the design. The message rate is real but modest: 14,000 writes a second is the same kind of problem as the 11,000 operations a second in chapter 20.2, and a split store handles it. Storage of 44 TB a year is large, but it grows slowly and spreads over many disks. The open connections are different. A design that was only about requests would not even see them. Three million lines cannot sit on a few servers, and every one of them is a piece of state that must be found, kept alive and moved. Dardasha is a connection problem first, a message problem second and a storage problem third.

There is a second number hiding behind the 3 million. It grows in step with it and it is easy to miss. Chapter 22.4 finds it.

These are averages and guesses. Say them out loud and invite correction. If only 10% of devices are online at the busiest time, the gateways halve. If users send 160 messages a day, the message rate quadruples and the design needs more partitions, but the shape stays the same.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say:** "Let me fix scope and numbers first. One-to-one and group text chat, groups up to 500, sent, delivered and read ticks, offline delivery, online status and push alerts. No media and no encryption for now. I will assume 10 million daily users sending 40 messages each. That is about 4,600 messages a second, 14,000 at peak, and about 44 TB a year. The number I care about most is open connections: about 3 million at peak, so about 60 gateways at 50,000 lines each. This is a connection problem first."

**Expect:** "Why do you say 20% online?" **Answer:** "It is an assumption. I would check it against real logs, because the gateway count moves in a straight line with it."
