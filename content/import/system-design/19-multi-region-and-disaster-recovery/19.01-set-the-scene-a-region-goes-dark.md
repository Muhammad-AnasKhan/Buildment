Tawsil is a made-up courier app. Customers order a meal, couriers deliver it, and everyone pays inside the app. Everything runs in one cloud region: the app servers, the database and the queue. At 14:02 on a Friday, that region loses power.

Watch the next picture once. Then ask yourself: what would you try first?

## At 14:02, every request fails

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m19-region-dark" />

The customer's order worked at 14:01. At 14:02 the region goes dark. The courier taps "delivered" and gets no answer. The customer's next tap gets none either. The card on the right says "none": there is no second place to go. Nothing in the app is broken. The place it lives in is gone.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what a region is">

A cloud provider runs its service in many regions, each in a different part of the world. A region is made of several availability zones. An <GlossaryTerm term="availability zone" definition="One or more data centres in a region with their own power, networking and internet connection, built so that a fault in one zone does not reach another." /> is the smaller unit. Chapter 19.2 shows how zones protect you from a single data centre failing.

A fault that takes down a whole region is rare. A region is built so that its parts fail one at a time. Rare is not never. A shared system inside the region can fail, a network cable can be cut, or a mistake can spread across the region. Chapter 3.3 told the story of one such day, when a DNS fault left one region's database unreachable by name.

Tawsil, its numbers and the timings in this module are a course story. They are not from a real company.

</GoDeeper>

Look at what sits inside the box: the app, the data and the queue. DNS gives out one address, and it points into the same box. One cause, one box, and every part fails at once. Before you read on, think about two things. What would you try first? And what do you wish you had set up last month?

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the hour in numbers, and the road through this module">

Say Tawsil takes 120 orders a minute on a Friday afternoon (a made-up number). An outage of one hour is 7,200 orders that never happen, plus every courier who cannot confirm a delivery and every customer who does not know if their meal is coming. The cost is not only money. It is also trust, and the calls to support afterwards.

Two numbers decide how Tawsil should prepare, and they are not the same number. The first is how much data it can afford to lose: the orders written in the last minute, or in the last hour. The second is how long it can afford to be down. Chapter 19.3 gives them names and shows how to read them from a plan.

The rest of the module follows the same story. Chapter 19.2 spreads one region across zones and cuts the app into cells so one fault reaches fewer customers. Chapter 19.4 adds a second region, either waiting or working. Chapter 19.5 moves users from the failed region to the working one. Chapter 19.6 asks what changes when the law says where data may live, and what a global database costs in delay. Each choice buys safety and charges money, time or complexity. Your job is to choose with numbers.

</GoDeeper>
