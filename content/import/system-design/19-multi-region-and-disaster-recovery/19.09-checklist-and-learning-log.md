## Learning log

1. **Your shared parts:** Think of an app you use. Name one thing every zone of it must share, and what a mistake in that thing would do to the app. What would you change so a mistake reaches fewer users?
2. **Your timeline:** In the chapter 19.3 picture, drag the failure to just before 02:00. With a nightly backup only, how much data is lost and how long is the recovery? With the log plan at the same moment, which number got worse, and why?
3. **Your wait:** Pick a health check interval, a failure count and a TTL for a service you know. Add them. If the answer is longer than your RTO, which of the three would you change first, and what would it cost?
4. **Your button:** Who may declare a disaster in a team you know? What two signals would you want to agree first? What stops the old region from writing once you have failed over?
5. **Your rule:** For an app you use, name one kind of data a rule could pin to a region. Name one copy of it that is easy to forget, such as a backup, a log or a search index.

## Your gate answer: a disaster recovery plan for Tawsil's orders

Disaster recovery is where a design meets the worst day and the budget at the same time. A weak plan says "we have a second region". A strong plan says what is lost, how long users wait, what it costs and how the team will know it works. It uses numbers, shows how they add up and names what it accepts. This is also the answer an interviewer wants when they ask "what happens if a region goes down?": a short chain of numbers, not a feeling.

The app is Tawsil, the made-up courier app from this module. It takes about 120 orders a minute at its Friday peak, which is about 300 writes a second to the database. The database is 1 TB. A rule says European customers' data must stay in the EU, and the app serves customers in two EU regions and one North America region. You choose the size and the assumptions. A mentor checks your reasoning, not whether your numbers match anyone else's.

A good answer has six parts. First, the objectives: an RPO and an RTO for orders and payments, and one sentence on why those limits, for example what an hour of outage costs. Second, the plan: which rung of the ladder you choose, and the steps that add up to your recovery time, each with a duration. Show the sum. Third, the data lost: the write rate times the copy lag, compared with your RPO. Fourth, the bad day that is not a lost region: how a wrong delete or a bad release is undone, how long you keep the change log, and how often you restore it as a drill. Fifth, the people and the switch: the health check time plus the cache time, who decides, what two signals must agree, how the old region is stopped from writing, and whether you will fail back or fail forward. Sixth, the cost and the rule: how big the second region is, how much more than one region the plan costs, and where every copy and backup lives.

Watch for four common mistakes. The first is to give an RTO without adding up its steps: "15 minutes" means nothing until the decision, the promotion, the scale-up and the DNS wait are on the page. The second is to forget the bad write, and to rely on copies for a problem that copies repeat. The third is to size the second region for half the load, so the survivor melts down on the day it is needed. The fourth is to put the standby, the backups or the logs outside the area that a residency rule allows.

Your plan does not need to be long, but each sentence must carry a decision. A sentence such as "we use a second region for safety" says nothing a reviewer can check. A sentence such as "a person calls the failover after two signals agree, which adds 10 minutes to the 3 minutes of DNS" can be tested against your numbers.

Before you submit, test the plan the way a reviewer would. Pick a moment, such as 14:07 on a Friday, and walk through the next two hours minute by minute. Then pick a different disaster, a wrong delete at 14:07, and walk through that. If any step is "the team handles it", add what the team does and how long it takes.

A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what changes if the database grows to 10 TB, or what you do if the second region is also unhealthy. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write the plan, record yourself explaining it, or both. The written answer is required; a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.
