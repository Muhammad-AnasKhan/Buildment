Tawsil (the made-up courier app from 19.1) has three zones inside its one region. Spreading across zones is cheap and helps a lot, but only against some faults. This chapter shows which ones, what the extra zones cost, and how to cut your own system into pieces so one bad day reaches fewer customers.

## A fault hits a part, not the whole

A failure domain is a set of things that fail together. A server is one. A zone is a bigger one, with its own power and network. The region is the biggest. Put copies in different domains, and one fault reaches only part of the app. The size of that part is the blast radius.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what a zone really is">

AWS describes an Availability Zone as one or more discrete data centres with separate and redundant power, networking and connectivity in a Region. Zones in a Region are meaningfully distant from each other, up to about 100 km, so a flood or a fire does not reach two of them. They are still close enough for synchronous replication with single-digit millisecond latency. When AWS deploys updates to its own services, it separates deployments to different zones in time, so one bad update does not reach two zones together.

So zones are built for faults that live in a place: a power cut, a cooling failure, a cut fibre, a flood. That is the first kind of failure domain.

The second kind is easy to forget. Your own software crosses every zone: the code you deploy, the settings in a config file, a shared database, a DNS record, a certificate that expires. A mistake in any of these reaches all zones at once, however far apart the buildings are. The next beats show why that matters.

Inside a region, a round trip takes about 0.25 ms in the course's latency table [S3]. That is why spreading across zones costs so little in speed, and why a synchronous copy to another zone (chapter 8.3) is usually affordable. Between regions, the round trip is 60 to 160 ms in the same table, a cost chapters 19.4 and 19.6 return to.

## Add zones and watch the gain stop

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m19-add-zones" />

Drag to choose how many zones the app uses, then tap a zone to fail it. One zone means any zone fault is an outage. Two zones survive a zone fault. The third and fourth add almost no uptime, because shared parts now cause most of the downtime. Watch the capacity bill too: it is what you pay to carry the load after a zone is lost.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the model behind the picture">

The picture uses a simple model with made-up numbers. Each zone is up 99.9% of the time, and zone faults are independent, so they never happen together. A shared layer, such as the load balancer rules and the deploy tool, is up 99.95% of the time. The app is up when the shared layer is up and at least one zone is up.

Availability = shared x (1 minus (1 minus zone) to the power n), where n is the number of zones. The results, with 525,600 minutes in a year:

| Zones | Availability | Downtime a year | Capacity to carry the load after losing a zone |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 99.850% | about 788 min (13.1 h) | 1.00 times peak, and no zone can fail |
| 2 | 99.950% | about 263 min (4.4 h) | 2.00 times peak |
| 3 | 99.950% | about 263 min (4.4 h) | 1.50 times peak |
| 4 | 99.950% | about 263 min (4.4 h) | 1.33 times peak |

Look at what each row says. Going from one zone to two cuts downtime by about two thirds. Going from two to three gains about half a minute a year, because the shared layer alone causes 263 minutes. More zones cannot fix a fault that every zone shares.

The capacity column comes from one rule: after any one zone fails, the others must carry the whole peak load. With n zones, each zone needs peak divided by (n minus 1), so the total is n divided by (n minus 1) times peak. Two zones need a full copy each. Three zones need only half a copy each, so 1.5 times peak. That is the reason teams pick three: the third zone is often cheaper than it looks, because the other two can be smaller.

Three also matters for agreement. A group that needs a majority, such as a Raft group from module 12, survives one lost zone only if its members sit in three zones: two of three is a majority, but one of two is not.

The model assumes independent zone faults and ignores cross-zone data transfer cost. Use it to see the shape, not to promise an uptime number.

</GoDeeper>

## Zones share fate with what you ship

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m19-shared-deploy" />

The release reaches all three zones at once, so all three show errors. Zones are built so that power and network faults stay inside one zone. They cannot stop a fault you send to every zone yourself. Your code, your config, your database and your DNS record are failure domains too, and they cross zones.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: shared fate is where most outages hide">

Make a list of every part of Tawsil that all three zones use at the same time: the release, the config file, the single database, the certificate, the DNS name, the payment provider's API key. Each one is a failure domain that is bigger than a zone. A good design review asks, for every part, "what else fails when this fails?" and draws the answer as a box.

There are three ways to shrink a shared failure domain. You can remove the sharing, for example by giving each zone its own copy of the config. You can change slowly, so a bad change reaches one place before it reaches all places, which is the next two beats. Or you can detect and stop the change early, with checks that stop a rollout on the first errors (the canary idea from module 18).

AWS's own advice is the same: its deployments to zones in one Region are separated in time to prevent correlated failure. You get that for free from the provider for its parts. You must do it yourself for yours.

</GoDeeper>

## Cut the system into cells

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m19-cells" />

A cell is a complete copy of the app that serves only some customers. Drag to choose how many cells, then tap the poison customer. Their bad request breaks the cell it lands in. With one cell, all 100 customers are hit. With 10 cells, 10 are. The price is a router in front and a more careful way of running things.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how a cell-based design works, and what it costs">

AWS's guidance on cell-based architecture describes the parts. The workload is split by a partition key, such as the customer ID, into independent copies called cells. Each cell is a complete workload with everything it needs, and it shares no state with the others. A thin cell router sends each request to the right cell using the key, and shows clients one address. A separate control plane creates cells, removes them and moves customers between them. In AWS's example, 10 cells serving 100 requests means a failure in one cell leaves 90% of requests unaffected.

Cells are good at containing the failures that zones cannot: a bad deployment, an operator mistake, or a poison pill request, one that is built to crash whatever handles it. Each of these is stuck inside one cell. Building cells does not always mean paying for more machines. AWS notes that an app with 30 hosts can keep the same 30 hosts and group them into cells.

What it costs. The router is now on the path of every request, so keep it as thin as possible and test it hard; a smart router becomes the new shared part that fails everyone. Every cell must be able to run alone, so shared databases and shared caches break the idea. A big customer can fill a cell by themselves. Reports that need data from every cell have to be assembled outside the cells. And there are more things to deploy and watch.

**A refinement: shuffle sharding.** Instead of giving each customer one cell, give each customer a small random group of workers. In the Amazon Builders' Library example, 8 workers and 2 per customer make 28 different pairs. A bad customer can only break their own pair, and any other customer shares at most one worker with them, so the impact of one bad customer is about 1/28 instead of 1/4 with four fixed groups of two.

The older word for the same idea is a bulkhead: separate pools so one failure cannot use up the shared resources [S93].

## Release one cell at a time

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m19-cell-rollout" />

Cells also make releases safer. The tool sends release 42 to Cell 1 only. Cell 1 shows errors, so the rollout halts, and Cells 2 and 3 stay on release 41. A bad release hurt one third of customers, not all of them. With ten cells, it would hurt a tenth.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to roll out by cell">

Start with the smallest cell, or a cell that holds your own staff and test customers. Wait long enough for the problem to show, which is called bake time. Watch the signals that would tell you something is wrong: error rate and latency, and compare the new cell with the others, as the canary idea in module 18 does [S110]. If they look good, go to the next cell, then to larger ones. If they do not, stop and roll back only the cell you touched.

The order of the waves is a design choice. A common plan is one zone and one cell at a time inside a region, then the next region. A change that is safe in one region is much less likely to break the others.

A cell does not protect you from a change to something every cell shares. If all cells read the same config store or database, a bad value there reaches every cell at once, so a rollout by cell would not help. This is the same lesson as the last beat, one level up: find the shared parts, and either remove the sharing or change them slowly too.

</GoDeeper>
