Tawsil's database holds every order. Two questions decide its recovery plan, and they are different questions. How much data can it lose? How long can it be down? This chapter turns both into numbers you can read off a timeline.

## Pick the moment it fails

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m19-failure-time" />

Drag the failure marker along the day, and switch what protects the database. With a nightly backup, you lose everything since 02:00, up to 24 hours. With the log of changes saved every 5 minutes, you lose at most 5 minutes. With a copy in another region, you lose about a second. The time to get back changes too, and the cheapest plan is not the fastest.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind the picture">

The picture uses made-up course numbers. The database is 1 TB. A restore copies data back at 250 MB per second, so 1,000,000 MB divided by 250 is 4,000 seconds, about 67 minutes. Switching the app to the restored database takes 10 minutes. Replaying saved changes runs 10 times faster than real time. These are the three parts of the time to recover: copy the data back, replay what came after, and switch over.

| Protection | Data lost | Time to recover |
|---|---|---|
| Nightly backup only | everything since 02:00, from 0 to 24 hours | 67 + 10 = 77 minutes |
| Backup plus log saved every 5 minutes | 0 to 5 minutes | 67 + replay + 10; at 12 hours since the backup, replay is 72 minutes, so 149 minutes |
| Asynchronous copy in another region | about a second of writes, about 300 at 300 writes a second | about 1 minute to promote + 10 to switch = 11 minutes |

The first two rows show a trap. A plan with a log saves more data but can take longer to recover, because there is more to replay. The third row recovers fastest and loses least, but it needs a second region running all the time, and it does not protect against the mistake in beat 3.

Where do these figures come from? Amazon RDS uploads transaction logs to S3 every five minutes, which sets the 5 minutes in the second row. PostgreSQL's asynchronous replicas are typically under a second behind [S38], and the AWS disaster recovery guide quotes a typical latency of under a second for Aurora Global Database, with promotion of a secondary region in under a minute [S107]. Between regions the lag can never be smaller than the one-way delay, about 40 ms between EU West and North America East (half of an 80 ms round trip [S3]).

## Two clocks: how much, how long

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m19-two-clocks" />

A failure starts two clocks. The first runs back from the failure to the last good copy: that is the data you lose. The second runs forward until the service works again: that is the time you are down. A plan states a limit for each. The limit on data lost is the RPO. The limit on time down is the RTO.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: RPO and RTO, as targets and as results">

<GlossaryTerm term="RPO" definition="Recovery point objective: the most data you accept to lose, measured as the time between the last recovery point and the failure." /> and <GlossaryTerm term="RTO" definition="Recovery time objective: the longest you accept to be down, measured from the interruption of service to its restoration." /> have official definitions in the AWS disaster recovery guide. RTO is the maximum acceptable delay between the interruption of service and restoration of service. RPO is the maximum acceptable amount of time since the last data recovery point [S108].

Notice that both are measured in time, even RPO. "Twelve hours of orders" is a time. To turn it into a count, multiply by the write rate: at 300 writes a second, five minutes is 90,000 writes.

An objective is a limit you choose. A plan produces a result, and the result must stay inside the limit. The picture shows results for one failure at 14:07: 12 hours 7 minutes of data and 77 minutes down. If Tawsil's objective was an RPO of 5 minutes and an RTO of 30 minutes, this plan fails both, and a nightly backup was never going to meet them.

Objectives belong to a service, not to a company. Payments may need an RPO of seconds. A weekly report may accept a day. Every step down in RPO or RTO costs money, because it needs more copies, more machines running, or more practice. Pick the cheapest plan that stays inside the limit, and write the limit down before you design.

An RPO of zero means no acknowledged write is ever lost. That needs a synchronous copy (chapter 8.3), which makes every write wait for the copy. Between regions, that wait is a whole round trip, 60 to 80 ms in the course latency table [S3].

</GoDeeper>

## A replica copies the mistake

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m19-copy-mistake" />

The delete is a valid change, so the primary applies it and copies it. A second later both replicas have an empty orders table. Replication protects against losing a machine, not against a wrong change. A copy in another region protects against a lost region, and it copies the mistake just as fast.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: two kinds of disaster">

Disasters come in two kinds. In the first, a thing is lost: a disk, a zone, a region. A copy of the data survives, so copies are the answer. In the second, the data itself is damaged: a bad delete, a bug that writes wrong values, a migration that drops a column, or a malicious user who deletes on purpose. Every copy is damaged too, within moments.

The AWS disaster recovery guide says this directly. Continuous replication gives the shortest time to a copy, but it may not protect against data corruption or an attack such as unauthorised deletion as well as point-in-time backups do. Keep versions of your stored data, or point-in-time recovery, as well as the copy [S107].

Object storage shows the same split. With versioning on, a delete in S3 keeps the earlier version. The guide notes that S3 replication does not copy the delete marker to the recovery bucket by default, which protects that bucket from a delete in the source region [S107]. The same guide recommends copying backups to another account, which helps when the cause is an insider or a stolen login rather than a lost data centre [S107].

So a recovery plan needs both: copies for lost things, and versions or point-in-time recovery for damaged data. The next beat shows the second.

</GoDeeper>

## Rewind to the second before

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m19-pitr" />

Point-in-time recovery needs two things: a full backup, and a saved log of every change since. A new database starts from the 02:00 backup, replays the log, and stops at 14:06, the second before the delete. The damaged database is left alone. You lose nothing written before the mistake, and you pay in time to restore and replay.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how it works in practice, and its limits">

Amazon RDS shows the idea. It takes backups and uploads the database's transaction logs to S3 every five minutes. You can restore to any point in time inside the retention period, and the restore creates a new database instance without touching the source. The "latest restorable time" is the newest moment the saved logs can reach, so it can trail the present by up to about five minutes.

Four limits to remember. First, you choose the moment, so you must find out when the mistake happened. Check logs and audit trails. Second, writes made after the mistake and before you noticed it are not in the restored database. If the app kept running for an hour, that hour of good orders needs a plan: export them from the damaged database and apply them again, or accept the loss. Third, the retention window is a cost, in storage, and a limit: a mistake you notice after the window cannot be rewound. Fourth, a restore takes time, as the first beat showed, so point-in-time recovery lowers the RPO for a mistake and does nothing for the RTO.

## A backup you never restored is a hope

<MermaidDiagram title="GitLab.com, 31 January 2017" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["An engineer removes about 300 GB of data from the primary database"] --> B["Daily backup to S3: the bucket was empty"]
  A --> C["Disk snapshots: not enabled for the database servers"]
  A --> D["Replica: replication had already broken"]
  A --> E["Snapshot from staging: 6 hours old, made for another job"]
  E --> F["Copying it back took about 18 hours"]
  F --> G["About 6 hours of data lost"]
`} legend="From the GitLab postmortem. Three of the four safety nets could not be used. The fourth was never meant for disaster recovery." />

In January 2017 GitLab.com lost about six hours of data after an engineer removed a directory on the wrong server. The daily backup to S3 had been failing, and nobody noticed. The replica had broken. The only usable copy came from a snapshot made for another job, and copying it back took about 18 hours.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what failed, and what a restore drill looks like">

GitLab's own postmortem lists the failures. The nightly backup script used an older version of the database tool than the database it was backing up, so it failed. The emails that should have reported the failure were rejected, so no one knew. Disk snapshots were on for file servers but not for database servers. The replica had stopped because change files were removed before it received them, and it was then wiped to be rebuilt. The best copy was a snapshot taken about six hours earlier to copy production into staging. Its disks were slow, and copying it back took about 18 hours. GitLab estimated that at least 5,000 projects, 5,000 comments and about 700 user accounts were affected. Git repositories were stored elsewhere and were not lost.

The lesson is not that one team was careless. Each safety net looked fine until the day it was needed. The fix is to use each one before the day it is needed.

A restore drill is a scheduled test. Once a month, restore the latest backup into a fresh, empty environment. Time it from start to a working copy: that measured time is your real RTO, replacing the guess. Check the data: row counts and one known record. Then throw the environment away. Also alarm on the absence of success: a backup job should report "done" every night, and silence is the alarm. Keep backups where the failure that destroyed the database cannot reach them: another account, another region.

The AWS disaster recovery guide says the same in one sentence: your backup strategy must include testing your backups, and you should regularly test the whole strategy so you are confident to use it [S107].
