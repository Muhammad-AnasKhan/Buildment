Tawsil opens in Europe and North America. Two new facts arrive. A rule says European customers' data must stay in Europe. And a courier in one region and a customer in another sometimes touch the same order. Both facts decide where data lives and what a write costs.

## Some data may not leave its region

<MermaidDiagram title="Data that must stay in its region" chart={`flowchart LR
  EU["European customers"] --> RE["EU region: their rows live here"]
  NA["North American customers"] --> RN["North America region: their rows live here"]
  RE -. "blocked: EU rows may not be copied here" .-> RN
`} legend="Solid arrows: where each group's data is stored. Dashed arrow: a copy that the residency rule forbids." />

Data residency means a rule says where data may be stored. Here, European customers' rows stay in Europe. Their data lives in the EU region, and no copy, backup or log of it may sit in North America. The rule comes from law and contracts, such as the GDPR's limits on moving personal data out of the EU. Your legal team gives you the rule. Your design must obey it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what counts as data, and what this course does not cover">

The GDPR is the European Union's data protection law. Its Chapter V, Articles 44 to 46, sets the general principle for sending personal data to countries outside the EU: it is allowed only if the protection it had in the EU continues. It allows this when the European Commission has judged the other country adequate, or when the sender has put appropriate safeguards in place. Other countries and industries have their own rules, and some are stricter, such as a rule that certain data must never leave the country.

This course teaches how to build to a rule, not what the rule is. The rule depends on the law, the contract and the type of data, and your legal team decides it. Nothing here is legal advice.

For the design, the practical question is what counts. The rows in the main database count. So do the copies: replicas, backups, logs, caches, search indexes, analytics exports, and even a support person's screenshot. Some rules also cover who may read the data, not only where it is stored. List every place a customer's data can end up, then check each one against the rule.

## A global write waits for the nearest region

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m19-global-commit" />

The leader is in EU West. It sends the write to both other regions and says ok when one of them answers, because two of three is a majority. North America East is 80 ms away, so every write from here takes about 80 ms. That is the price of a database that is the same everywhere and keeps every confirmed write if a region is lost.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the cost of being right everywhere">

Chapter 8.3 showed that a leader that waits for a copy pays a round trip on every write. Chapter 12.3 showed that a majority of three is two. Put the two together across regions and the rule is simple: a write takes about the round trip to the nearest other copy, plus the disk write of about 0.3 ms [S3].

The course latency table gives the round trips: 60 ms between North America East and West, and 80 ms between EU West and North America East [S3]. So a leader in EU West waits about 80 ms, and a leader in North America East or West waits about 60 ms, because each has a copy 60 ms away. The leader does not wait for the farther copy.

What does the wait buy? Two things. Every confirmed write exists in at least two regions, so losing a region loses nothing that was confirmed: an RPO of zero. And reads can be consistent everywhere, which is what chapter 11 called linearizable, if they go through the leader or a majority.

What does it cost besides time? A partition between regions. A region cut off from the others cannot reach a majority, so it must stop accepting writes. The PACELC view from chapter 11.7 puts it in one line: when there is no partition, you trade latency for consistency, and this design pays latency to get consistency [S57]. Reads are the other half. A read from a local copy is fast and may be stale, as in chapter 8.4. A read that must be fresh goes to the leader and pays the trip too.

</GoDeeper>

## Write latency across regions for each choice

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m19-write-latency" />

Drag the customer between regions and switch the design. One leader in North America East is fast for nearby users and slow for far ones. A home region per customer is fast everywhere and keeps data in its region. A global database is slower for everyone and keeps every confirmed write. Watch the delay and the rule check.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the nine numbers, and what they hide">

The picture computes a write as the trip from the customer to the leader, plus the leader's own work. The leader's work is the 0.3 ms disk write plus the wait for one copy (0.25 ms in the same region, so 0.55 ms for a local leader, as in chapter 8.3) [S3]. Round trips come from the course latency table [S3].

| Design | Customer in NA East | Customer in NA West | Customer in EU West |
|---|---|---|---|
| One leader in NA East, copies elsewhere | about 0.6 ms | about 61 ms | about 81 ms |
| A home region per customer | about 0.6 ms | about 0.6 ms | about 0.6 ms |
| Global database, leader in the customer's region | about 61 ms | about 61 ms | about 81 ms |

Three things stand out. First, a global database and a single leader cost the same for far-away customers, 81 ms for Europe. The global database adds cost for the customers who were already close: North America East goes from 0.6 ms to 61 ms. Second, the home region design is the only one that is fast everywhere, because it writes only where the customer lives. Third, the rows hide what each design gives up.

| Design | Region lost | Residency for EU rows | Cross-region reads |
|---|---|---|---|
| One leader, async copies | loses the newest writes if the leader's region fails | EU rows are stored in NA East: breaks the rule | local, may be stale |
| Home region per customer | loses the newest writes of that region's customers | EU rows stay in the EU: meets the rule | a courier in another region pays the trip |
| Global database | loses nothing confirmed | copies sit in North America: breaks the rule | consistent, pays the trip |

A global database can be told to keep a customer's copies inside an area. CockroachDB, for example, offers super regions: regional data is stored only in the member regions of the super region, and its replicas stay inside them. Then the three copies sit in several EU regions, the write time falls to the delay between those regions, and you survive the loss of one EU region, though not of all of Europe. The delay between EU regions is not in our latency table, so the picture does not draw it.

The numbers are for steady conditions and one write at a time. Queueing, busy leaders and packet loss add to every figure.

## Backups and copies are data too

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m19-backup-leaves" />

A rule about where data lives covers every copy: replicas, backups, logs, caches and exports. A nightly backup to North America breaks the rule, even though the main database follows it. The recovery plan needs a second site inside the same area, so that recovering from a disaster never means breaking the rule.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: when residency and recovery pull against each other">

Disaster recovery wants a second place far from the first. Residency wants that place to be inside the area. The two fit when the area holds more than one region, as the EU does. They do not fit when it holds only one: then the second site must be a different provider, or an on-premises site in the same country, or you must accept a plan with a larger RTO and RPO and write that down.

Check each part of the plan from 19.3 to 19.5 against the rule. Backups and their copies: where do they go? The standby in 19.4: is it inside the area? Logs and metrics, which often contain user ids or addresses: where are they shipped? A cache or search index built from the data: where is it hosted? The routing in 19.5 can help but cannot replace the check, as the location routing note said: DNS can misjudge where a user is, so the data layer must enforce the rule.

A way to keep the rule small is to split the data. Keep personal data, such as names, addresses and phone numbers, in the regional database. Keep data that is not personal, such as the menu, prices and feature flags, in a global one. Some teams replace the personal data in global tables with a token, and keep the real value in the region. The less data the rule covers, the less of the design it shapes.

</GoDeeper>

## Choose where each kind of data lives

<MermaidDiagram title="One question per kind of data" chart={`flowchart TD
  Q["A kind of data"] --> A{"Does a rule fix where it may live?"}
  A -->|"yes"| R["Regional data: a home region per customer, copies and backups inside the area"]
  A -->|"no"| B{"Must every confirmed write survive losing a region?"}
  B -->|"yes"| G["Global database: pay the nearest-region trip on every write"]
  B -->|"no, a lost second is fine"| S["One leader with asynchronous copies: cheap, fast for nearby users"]
`} legend="Ask the questions in this order for each kind of data, not once for the whole company." />

Most apps use all three, one for each kind of data. Customer profiles and addresses follow the residency rule, so they live in a home region. Payments may need every confirmed write kept, so they use a global database and pay the trip. A restaurant menu can use one leader and copies, and be a second behind. Choose per kind of data, not per company.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: cheaper ways to meet a rule, and the honest cost of this module's designs">

Before building any of this, ask the uncommon question: do we need to hold this data at all? Data you never collect is never at risk of leaving. Data you keep only as a token, or only for a short time, is a smaller problem.

The designs cost different things. A home region per customer costs the least in speed, and it turns every cross-region question into an engineering task: a courier in one region who needs a customer's address in another must ask the home region. A user who moves country needs a planned move of their data. A global database gives the cleanest behaviour and charges the nearest-region trip on every write, plus operations work to run it. One leader with copies is the simplest and the cheapest, and it tells every far user to wait for the trip and accepts a small loss when a region fails.

Chapter 11.7 warned against calling a whole system CP or AP. This module shows why: Tawsil's payments choose consistency during a partition, and its menu chooses to stay available with stale data, in the same system [S55]. Choosing per kind of data is how real systems do it.

</GoDeeper>
