Tawsil now has a second region, Region B. There are two ways to use it: keep it waiting, or use it all the time. Each answers four questions differently. How long are users down? How much data is lost? What does it cost? And what happens when both regions write?

## A ladder from cold to hot

<MermaidDiagram title="Four ways to use a second region" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Backup and restore: Region B holds only copies of the data"] --> B["Pilot light: the database runs, the app servers are off"]
  B --> C["Warm standby: everything runs, at a small size"]
  C --> D["Active-active: both regions serve users"]
`} legend="Left to right: faster recovery and a bigger bill. The first three are active-passive: Region B waits for a disaster." />

Four plans sit on a ladder. At the left, Region B holds only backups. Next, its database runs but the app servers are off. Then everything runs, at a small size. At the right, both regions serve users. Each step shortens recovery and raises the bill. The first three are active-passive: the passive region waits.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what each rung means">

The AWS disaster recovery guide names the four plans in this order, from lowest cost to lowest recovery time [S107].

**Backup and restore.** Region B holds copies of the data and the code to rebuild everything. After a disaster you must deploy the infrastructure again, ideally from code, and restore the data. It is cheap, and it is also the right plan against data loss or damage. The guide says that for the loss of one data centre in a well-built app, this may be enough.

**Pilot light.** The data is replicated to a live database in Region B, and the core infrastructure exists. Servers hold the code but are switched off. Think of the small flame in a gas heater, which can light the whole fire.

**Warm standby.** A scaled-down but complete copy runs all the time and can serve traffic at once, at reduced capacity. You scale it up when the disaster comes. The guide notes that the difference from pilot light is that warm standby can handle traffic immediately, while pilot light needs servers switched on first.

**Active-active.** Both regions serve users. The guide calls it the most complex and costly plan, which can cut recovery to near zero. It also notes that with this plan there is no failover step as such: you test how the system reacts to losing a region, and whether the other region can carry all the traffic.

A choice that changes the picture is whether the standby is sized for the full load. Full size and idle is called hot standby, and the guide notes that most teams who build a full second environment decide to use it too.

One more idea from the guide. Cloud services split into a data plane, which serves traffic, and a control plane, which changes the setup. Control planes are usually less available than data planes. A failover that depends on starting servers or changing settings depends on the control plane, which may be struggling in the very disaster you are recovering from. Use only data plane actions during failover where you can. The cost of avoiding scale-up is paying for capacity that sits idle.

</GoDeeper>

## Fail a region, compare the four

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where the picture's numbers come from">

All numbers are course examples built from parts you have seen before. A person takes 10 minutes to decide to fail over. Building the infrastructure again from code takes 30 minutes. A 1 TB restore at 250 MB per second takes 67 minutes (chapter 19.3). Promoting a database copy takes 1 minute; the AWS guide says an Aurora global database can promote a secondary region in under a minute [S107]. Starting and scaling the app servers takes 10 minutes. Moving users takes 2.5 minutes: a health check every 30 seconds with three failures is 90 seconds, plus a 60 second DNS cache (chapter 19.5).

| Plan | Steps added up | Users down | Writes lost | Cost, in regions' worth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Backup and restore | 10 + 30 + 67 + 2.5 | about 110 minutes | up to 1 hour of writes, with backups copied hourly | 1.1 |
| Pilot light | 10 + 1 + 10 + 2.5 | about 24 minutes | about 300 writes | 1.2 |
| Warm standby | 10 + 1 + 2.5 | about 14 minutes, at reduced capacity | about 300 writes | 1.5 |
| Active-active | 2.5, no decision and no promotion | about 3 minutes for Region A's half of users; none for the rest | about 150 writes in flight | 2.0 |

The 300 writes are 300 writes a second times a 1 second copy lag. Active-active loses half as many because Region A only took half of the writes. The cost column is made up, but the last row follows a rule: after Region A fails, Region B alone must carry all the users, so each region has to be sized for 100% of the load. Two full regions cost about twice one.

Read the table by rows and by columns. Down the downtime column, the biggest drop is at the first step, because the 30 minutes to rebuild and the 67 to restore vanish once a live database is waiting. Down the cost column, the biggest jump is the last, because it doubles the machines. The honest question is which row your RTO and RPO allow, and not which row sounds best.

</GoDeeper>

## The copy lag is the data you lose

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m19-lag-loss" />

Region A confirms each order at once and copies it to Region B a little later. When A crashes, the copies still on their way never arrive. Lost writes equal the write rate times the lag: 300 writes a second times 1 second is 300 orders that customers were told were saved. A synchronous copy would remove the loss and add a round trip to every write.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: asynchronous copies across regions, and how to watch the lag">

Chapter 8.3 gave the trade: a leader that waits for a copy loses nothing it has acknowledged, and pays the round trip on every write. Between regions that trip is long: 60 ms between North America East and West, 80 ms between EU West and North America East, 160 ms between EU West and Singapore [S3]. Most teams copy between regions asynchronously and accept an RPO above zero, then measure the lag.

How big is the lag? There is no general number. The AWS guide quotes a typical latency of under a second for Aurora Global Database, and says it can replicate to up to five secondary regions [S107]. For objects, S3 Replication Time Control aims to replicate most objects in seconds and 99.9% of them within 15 minutes. A floor exists: the lag cannot be less than the one-way delay between the regions, which is half of the round trips above. Above the floor, lag grows when the primary is busy or the network is slow.

So treat the lag as a number to watch. Measure it all the time, and alarm when it nears your RPO. Aurora can monitor the lag of its secondary clusters so that at least one stays inside a target window [S107]. A plan that says "RPO 1 second" without an alarm is a hope, like the backup in 19.3.

## Who presses the failover button?

<MermaidDiagram title="From a bad signal to users on Region B" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Region A looks unhealthy"] --> B{"Real fault or false alarm?"}
  B -->|"false alarm"| C["Failing over loses time and data for nothing"]
  B -->|"real fault"| D{"Who decides?"}
  D -->|"automatic"| E["Fast, but a false alarm costs the same as a real fault"]
  D -->|"a person, with a script"| F["Minutes slower, but a person checks first"]
  E --> G["Stop Region A from writing: fencing"]
  F --> G
  G --> H["Promote Region B"]
  H --> I["Move users to Region B"]
`} legend="Whoever decides, the old region must be fenced before Region B is promoted. That prevents two regions from both accepting writes." />

Before any switch, someone must decide the fault is real. A false alarm costs the same time and data as a real disaster. AWS advises care with fully automatic failover, and says many teams press a button instead, backed by a script. Whoever decides, the old region must be stopped from writing before Region B is promoted. That step is fencing, from chapter 8.5.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: automatic or manual, and a runbook that works">

The AWS guide is direct about this. Failover started automatically by health checks or alarms should be used with caution. Even with good practice, recovery time and recovery point will be greater than zero, so a failover that was not needed still loses time and data. Manually started failover is therefore often used, and the guide says to automate the steps so that starting it is like pushing a button [S107]. AWS offers switches for this: Route 53 Application Recovery Controller gives routing controls you flip yourself, as a data plane action [S107].

Chapter 8.5 told the GitHub story of 2018. A network cut lasted 43 seconds. The failover tool promoted databases on the other coast, both coasts then held writes the other lacked, and service was degraded for 24 hours 11 minutes [S134]. One follow-up was to stop the tool from promoting across regional boundaries. This is the same lesson from the other side: the faster the detection, the more false alarms you will act on.

A good runbook answers these before the bad day. Who may declare a disaster, and who is their backup? Which two independent signals must agree, for example the health check and a human on the phone to the provider? How long do we wait before we decide? How do we fence Region A? Who tells customers? Write the answers down, and rehearse them, as 19.3 said about restores.

</GoDeeper>

## Failing back is a second, planned move

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m19-failback" />

When Region A returns, it is not the truth any more. It holds 300 writes that B never saw, and B has been taking orders for an hour. A must first be reset to be a copy of B. Only then, in a quiet hour and on purpose, do users move back. Many teams stay on B and make A the new standby.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: fail forward, fail back, and the trap in both">

There are two honest choices. **Fail forward:** keep Region B as the primary and rebuild Region A as its standby. Nothing moves twice. GitHub took this path in 2018, because failing back would have made most database calls cross the country and left the site largely unusable [S134]. **Fail back:** after A is a healthy copy of B, move users and writes back, usually in a quiet period. You do this when A is the better place to live, for example because it is closer to most users, or has more capacity.

In both choices you first deal with A's extra writes. You can discard them, or export them and re-apply what is still valid, with a person checking. This is the same reason chapter 8.5 gave for why an old leader cannot simply rejoin; PostgreSQL offers a tool, `pg_rewind`, that speeds up rebuilding the old primary [S38].

The trap is to treat failback as the easy half. It is a second failover: a decision, a fence, a promotion and a switch of users, with its own chance of going wrong, but with no emergency to excuse a mistake. Rehearse it. Replication must also run the other way, from B to A, before any user moves, and you should check that its lag is small.

</GoDeeper>

## Active-active: avoid fighting over one record

<MermaidDiagram title="Each record has a home region" chart={`flowchart LR
  AU["Amna: home Region A"] -->|"her writes"| RA["Region A"]
  BU["Omar: home Region B"] -->|"his writes"| RB["Region B"]
  RA <-->|"copy each other's rows, a little later"| RB
`} legend="Both regions work, and each row has one home, so the same row is never written in two places at once." />

In active-active, both regions accept writes. If both change the same record within the copy lag, they conflict, and a rule such as last writer wins silently drops one edit. The safest plan gives each record a home region, so all its writes go to one place. Both regions work, and one record is never written in two places at once.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: three ways to write in two regions, and the cost of each">

The AWS guide describes three write strategies for active-active, with reads served from the nearest region, called read local [S107].

**Write global.** All writes go to one region, and another is promoted if it fails. Aurora Global Database fits this, with promotion in under a minute. It is simple and has no conflicts, but users far from the writer pay the round trip.

**Write local.** Each region takes writes from its nearby users. DynamoDB global tables work this way, and they settle concurrent updates to an item by last writer wins [S43]. Writes are fast, and conflicts are possible. Chapter 8.6 showed how last writer wins loses an edit.

**Write partitioned.** Each record belongs to a region by a key, such as the user ID, so writes to one record are never in two places. This is the home region idea in the picture. It keeps writes local and avoids conflicts, at three costs. A user who travels still writes to their home region and pays the distance. If the home region fails, its users need a new place to write, and the data copied so far, which brings back the lag-loss from beat 3. And a record shared by users from both regions, such as a courier job board, still needs one owner.

Even then, active-active does not replace backups. The AWS guide stresses that a data disaster, such as corruption or deletion, is copied to both regions, so you still need point-in-time recovery [S107]. And you must test it: loss of a region, whether traffic moves away, and whether the other region can handle all the traffic [S107].

</GoDeeper>
