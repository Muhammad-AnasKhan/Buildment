Wasla is a link shortener (a fictional service). You paste a long address and get a short one, such as `wasla.example/aB3x9Kp`. Shops print it on posters, and friends share it in chat. This module runs the design loop from chapter 1.4 on Wasla, from clarify to failure. This first chapter does the first two steps: say what Wasla must do, then turn that into numbers.

## A short link is one extra hop

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m20-click-a-link" />

Someone taps the short link. Their phone asks Wasla for `/aB3x9Kp`. Wasla looks up the long address and sends the phone on with a redirect, which means "go to this other address". That is the whole product. Wasla stores a code and a long address, and answers "what is behind this code?" very fast, and very often.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: clarify first, the way chapter 1.4 does">

Before any numbers, ask what Wasla must do and what it will not do. Here is the scope the product owner agreed. All of it is a course choice, because Wasla is made up.

**Wasla does.** It makes a short link from a long address (`http` or `https`, up to 2,048 characters). It opens a short link and redirects. It lets the owner pick an optional custom name, called an alias, such as `summer-sale`, and an optional expiry date. It counts clicks per link.

**Wasla does not, for now.** It has no accounts or billing, no editing of a link's destination, no QR codes, no search, and no scanning for bad sites. Each is a fair feature, and each would add work to every later step. A real service must scan for bad sites. The course leaves it out so the design stays readable.

**Fast.** A redirect should take under 20 ms at the service, measured as p99 (chapter 2.4), not counting the user's own network.

**Reliable.** Opening a link works 99.95% of the time, about 22 minutes of trouble a month (0.05% of 43,200 minutes). Creating a link may be a little lower, at 99.9%. A saved link must never be lost, because it may be printed on a poster.

**Honest about.** Click counts may be a few seconds late and slightly off. Links are public: anyone who has the code can open the link, and Wasla says so in its rules.

**Limits.** A team of four, a cloud account, and a budget.

The line about public links matters later. Because links are public, Wasla does not need codes that nobody can guess. That one answer will decide a trade-off in chapter 20.3.

## Drag the volume, find the load

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m20-estimate-play" />

Drag the new links per month, then the reads per link. Creating stays small, about 190 a second. Opening is the load: about 19,000 a second on average and about 96,000 at the busiest moment. Storage is the second driver: 30 billion links over five years is about 9 TB. The ringed card is the number that drives the design.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the working, and what is an assumption">

Nobody gave Wasla these numbers, so each one is written down as an assumption: 500 million new links a month, 100 redirects for each link created, a busiest second that is 5 times the average, links kept for 5 years, and about 300 bytes stored per link. The method is the one from chapter 2.3: start from people, divide by seconds, keep the unit next to every number.

| Quantity | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Seconds in a month | 30 x 86,400 | 2,592,000 |
| New links a second, average | 500,000,000 / 2,592,000 | about 193 |
| Redirects a second, average | 193 x 100 | about 19,300 |
| Redirects a second, peak | 19,300 x 5 | about 96,500, planned as 100,000 |
| New links a second, peak | 193 x 5 | about 965, planned as 1,000 |
| Links stored in 5 years | 500,000,000 x 60 months | 30 billion |
| Storage | 30,000,000,000 x 300 B | 9 TB |
| Redirects in one day | 19,300 x 86,400 | about 1.7 billion |
| Network out at peak | 100,000 x 500 B | 50 MB/s, or 400 Mbit/s |

The 500 bytes is the size of one redirect answer with its headers. It is a course number.

Now ask which number drives the design. Creating at 1,000 a second at most is small: one ordinary database server can take it. Opening at 100,000 a second is not. Chapter 2.2 gave a query to a database about 0.5 ms, so one connection serves about 2,000 simple reads a second, and one server perhaps a few thousand. Reads at this size need many copies of the answer, kept close, which is a cache. Storage matters too. 9 TB is possible on one disk, but a single machine would hold the only copy and carry all the traffic. So the design must spread the data. Wasla is a read problem first and a storage problem second.

These are averages and guesses. Say them out loud and invite correction. If the real ratio is 10 reads per link, the cache matters far less. If it is 1,000, it matters far more.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say:** "Before I draw, let me fix scope and numbers. I will support create, redirect, an optional alias and expiry, and click counts. I will leave out accounts and editing. I will assume 500 million new links a month and 100 redirects per link. That is about 200 creates and 20,000 redirects a second, about 100,000 at peak, and about 9 TB over five years. So this is a read-heavy system, and I will design the read path first."

**Expect:** "Why 100 to 1?" **Answer:** "It is an assumption. Links are made once and opened many times. I would check it against real traffic, because it decides how much the cache matters."
