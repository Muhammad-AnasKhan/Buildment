## Your gate answer: Hadra Paste

This is the case study again on a different product. Hadra Paste (a made-up service) lets developers paste code or logs and share a short link to the text. Here are the assumptions. There are 30 million new pastes a month. Each paste is read about 20 times. The average paste is 6 KB and the largest is 512 KB. Pastes are kept for one year, and the author can mark a paste unlisted, which means that only people who know the link can read it. A paste never changes after it is saved. In an interview, this is the "design a pastebin" question, and it is the same loop you just ran.

A weak answer lists boxes: a load balancer, a cache, a database, a CDN, and stops. A strong answer starts from the numbers and lets them decide which boxes to draw. Compute the creates a second, the reads a second at a busiest second that is 3 times the average, and the storage for a year. Then ask what those numbers need. Chapter 20.1 showed that Wasla's reads were 100,000 a second at peak and that decided almost everything. Does Hadra Paste have that problem? If the peak is a few hundred reads a second, a cache may be a decision you can defend leaving out, as PasteBox did in chapter 1.4. Say so, and say when you would change your mind.

Then pick a code method and a length, and tie both to the requirements. Unlisted pastes change the answer from Wasla's, so say how. Say where the text is stored and what the read path looks like. Because a paste never changes, say whether a CDN helps. Say what happens when your one most important block fails.

Be explicit about one trade-off. Write it in the same shape each time: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would switch. A good cost line has a number or a name. For example: "an extra database read of about 2 ms on a miss, and a cache to keep warm," or "a 9-character code, which is one character longer than people like to type." If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished.

A good order for 250 words is the order of the loop. Numbers come first, in two or three lines. Then the code and the read path, which are the sketch and the deep dive. Then the trade-off, and last the failure. If you run short of words, cut the sketch, not the numbers or the trade-off.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is copying Wasla's design without checking the numbers, so a cache and a queue appear that nothing in the estimate asks for. The second is choosing counter codes for unlisted pastes, which anyone can guess. The third is forgetting the unique key: whichever code method you pick, the database must refuse a second row with the same code.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right design. Two students can choose differently and both pass, as long as each one starts from the numbers, ties the code method to the requirement, names the failure the choice answers, and says when they would change their mind. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens when the object store is slow, or how you would stop people from guessing unlisted codes. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, about 250 words. A recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.
