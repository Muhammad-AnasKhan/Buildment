## Your gate answer: estimate a photo-sharing app

Estimation is the first number-heavy step of every system design discussion. In an interview it usually comes right after the requirements. In a real team it comes before anyone chooses a database. A weak estimate gives one big number with no working. A strong estimate shows each step with its unit, names its assumptions so anyone can change them, and ends by saying which number drives the design.

The app for your answer is a photo-sharing app, similar to the ones on your phone: people upload photos, and other people scroll through a feed and view them. You choose the size of the app and your assumptions. There is no single right number. A mentor checks your method and your reasoning, not whether you guessed the same daily users as someone else.

A good answer has five parts, each one or two lines. First, the assumptions: daily users, how many upload and how many photos each, the size of an uploaded photo, how many photos each user views a day, and your peak factor. Second, uploads per second, average and peak. Third, views per second at peak, because reads usually outnumber writes. Fourth, storage per year, with the number of copies you keep. Fifth, bandwidth at peak for views, in Mbit/s or Gbit/s.

Then mark the driving number. Write it in bold or put the word "driver" next to it, and say in one sentence what it forces in the design. You met both kinds in chapter 2.3: a rate, which stays the same when users do, and a total, which grows every year. A photo app has a large total and a large rate at once, so you must choose and defend your choice.

Watch for four common mistakes. The first is dividing by 24 or by 1,440 when you meant 86,400 seconds in a day: write the unit and the mistake shows itself. The second is mixing bytes and bits for bandwidth: storage is in bytes, network links are in bits, and the difference is 8 times. The third is forgetting copies: a photo stored once is a photo lost with one disk. The fourth is using the same size for uploads and views: apps usually store the full photo but show smaller versions in a feed, and that changes the bandwidth a lot. You may assume a smaller viewed size if you say so.

Before you submit, check your answer the way a reviewer would. Read each line and ask whether its unit follows from the line above it. Pick your least certain assumption, double it in your head, and see whether the driving number changes; if it does, say so in one sentence, because that is the assumption the team should measure first. Finally, compare one number with something you have measured or read in this module. Your peak views per second, for example, can be set against the knee you found in challenge 1: if one small server bent at about 60 requests per second, how many such servers would your peak need, before any headroom? A rough answer is fine. The point is to connect the estimate to a real limit.

Add the link to your challenge 1 repository folder, or to its part 1 `results.md`, in the link field. Your mentor will open your knee chart next to your estimate, because the two skills belong together: estimating what a design needs, and measuring what a real server can do. If the mentor asks a follow-up question, answer it by editing and resubmitting.

You may write the estimate, or record yourself explaining it at a whiteboard or on paper, or both. The written answer is required; a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.
