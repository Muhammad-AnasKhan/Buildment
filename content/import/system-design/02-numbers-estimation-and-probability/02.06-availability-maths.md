A vendor promises "three nines" for its database. Your load balancer has four nines. What does the whole page get? This chapter turns availability into hours a year, and shows how chains and copies change it.

## Nines become hours and minutes

<MermaidDiagram title="Nines to downtime per year" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["99% up: 1% of 8,760 hours"] --> A2["about 88 hours (3.7 days) down a year"]
  B["99.9% up: three nines"] --> B2["about 8.8 hours down a year"]
  C["99.99% up: four nines"] --> C2["about 53 minutes down a year"]
  D["99.999% up: five nines"] --> D2["about 5 minutes down a year"]
`} legend="A year has 8,760 hours. Downtime is the share that is not up, times 8,760 hours. Each extra nine cuts downtime ten times." />

<GlossaryTerm term="Availability" definition="The share of time a system is working for its users, for example 99.9%. One minus availability is the share of time it is down." /> is the share of time a system works. Its opposite, the share of time it is down, is what users notice. A year has 8,760 hours, so 99.9% up means about 8.8 hours down. Each extra nine divides the downtime by ten.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the working, and what a nine costs">

The arithmetic is one multiplication. A year has 365 times 24 = 8,760 hours, or 525,600 minutes. Downtime is (1 - availability) times that.

- 99%: 0.01 times 8,760 hours = 87.6 hours, about 3.7 days.
- 99.9%: 0.001 times 8,760 = 8.76 hours.
- 99.99%: 0.0001 times 525,600 minutes = 52.6 minutes.
- 99.999%: 0.00001 times 525,600 = 5.3 minutes.

Each nine is ten times less downtime, and usually much more than ten times the effort. Five nines leaves about five minutes a year: less time than it takes a person to notice an alert, open a laptop and log in. Systems at that level must recover by themselves.

The Google SRE book calls a 100% target "unrealistic and undesirable", and suggests a different way to think about the gap. With an availability target, the allowed failure is a budget. A 99.999% target leaves an error budget of 0.001%. A problem that fails 0.0002% of requests spends 20% of a quarter's budget. While budget remains, the team can ship changes; when it is spent, releases stop until reliability improves. You will set targets like this in module 17.

Availability can be measured by time (minutes up) or by requests (share of requests that succeeded). For a service with steady traffic the two are close. For a service whose traffic varies, a 10-minute outage at the evening peak hurts far more users than one at 4 am, and a request-based number shows that.

</GoDeeper>

## A chain fails if any link fails

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m2-chain-fails" />

The page needs the load balancer, the app and the database, one after another. While all three are up, it works. The moment the database goes down, every page fails, even though the other two are fine. Parts in a chain are said to be in series. Each one you add is one more way to fail.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: series means all must work">

Two parts are in <GlossaryTerm term="series" definition="Parts that a request needs one after another: the request works only if every part works. A load balancer, an app server and a database on one request path are in series." /> when a request needs both. Most request paths are chains: DNS, then a load balancer, then an app server, then a cache or database, sometimes then another service. Each part has its own failures: a bad deploy, a full disk, a lost network link, an expired certificate.

The chain is weaker than its weakest link. Even if the load balancer is almost perfect, the page can never be more available than the database it needs. And each extra link lowers the total a little more, which is a real cost of splitting one service into many small ones that call each other (module 16).

The maths in this chapter assumes the parts fail independently: one part going down does not make another more likely to go down. That is often roughly true for different kinds of parts. Beat 5 shows when it is badly false.

</GoDeeper>

## Series: multiply the availabilities

<MermaidDiagram title="Availability of a chain" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Load balancer 99.99%"] -->|"then"| B["App 99.9%"]
  B -->|"then"| C["Database 99.9%"]
  C --> D["0.9999 × 0.999 × 0.999 = 0.9979"]
  D --> E["99.79% up: about 18 hours down a year"]
`} legend="In series, multiply the availabilities. The total is always lower than the worst part." />

For parts in series, multiply their availabilities. 0.9999 times 0.999 times 0.999 is about 0.9979, so the page is up 99.79% of the time: about 18 hours down a year. That is more than double the 8.8 hours of the database alone. A chain is always less available than its weakest part.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why multiplying works, and a quick shortcut">

The page works only if the load balancer works and the app works and the database works. For independent events, the chance that all of them happen is the product of their chances. That is the series rule: A(series) = A1 times A2 times ... times An. {/* source needed: a citable reference for the series and parallel availability formulas under independent failures */}

The working: 0.999 times 0.999 = 0.998001. Times 0.9999 is about 0.997901. So the page is down 0.002099 of the time, and 0.002099 times 8,760 hours is about 18.4 hours a year.

**A shortcut.** When every part is close to 100%, you can add the downtimes instead of multiplying: 0.01% + 0.1% + 0.1% = 0.21% down, so about 99.79% up. The exact product and the shortcut agree to the precision an estimate needs. The shortcut also shows where the downtime comes from: the two three-nines parts give almost all of it, and improving the load balancer from four to five nines would change almost nothing.

In code, the series rule is one line: multiply the list. You will write `seriesAvailability` in chapter 2.7, including what it should return for an empty chain.

</GoDeeper>

## Two copies: both must fail

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m2-two-copies" />

Now there are two copies of the app, and the load balancer checks both. App A goes down, the load balancer stops sending it traffic, and App B serves the next page. The user sees nothing. Copies side by side are in parallel: the service fails only if every copy is down at the same moment.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the parallel rule">

Copies are in <GlossaryTerm term="parallel" definition="Redundant copies where any one of them can serve the request: the service fails only if every copy fails at the same time." /> when any one of them can do the job. Then the service is down only if all copies are down together. For independent copies, the chance that all are down is the product of their down chances, so A(parallel) = 1 - (1 - A1) times (1 - A2) times ... times (1 - An).

The working for two app copies at 99.9%: each is down 0.001 of the time. Both are down together 0.001 times 0.001 = 0.000001 of the time, one millionth. So the pair is up 99.9999% of the time: about 30 seconds a year, down from 8.8 hours. One extra copy added three nines.

The picture hides two costs. First, the load balancer must notice the failure. Health checks run every few seconds, so some requests still reach the dead copy before it is removed, and they fail or must be retried (module 4). Second, the remaining copy must handle all the traffic alone. If each copy normally runs at 70% busy, the survivor would need 140%, so it falls over too. Copies only help if each has room to take the others' load: chapter 2.5's knee again.

</GoDeeper>

## Copies on one host fail together

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m2-shared-power" />

App A and App B look like two copies, but both run on the same physical machine. The machine loses power, and both copies go down in the same second. The parallel rule promised 99.9999%, but it assumed the copies fail independently. They shared a cause, so they behaved like one copy.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: shared causes and how designs avoid them">

The parallel rule is only as good as its independence assumption. Two copies that share anything that can fail are not independent: one host, one rack, one power feed, one network switch, one data centre, one cloud zone. A shared cause can also be software: the same bad deploy pushed to both copies at once, the same expired certificate, the same bug triggered by the same input.

Designs spread copies across failure domains to keep them independent. Copies go on different hosts, then in different racks, then in different availability zones (separate data centres in one region), and for the most important systems in different regions (module 19). Each step costs more: more network distance between copies (chapter 2.2) and more complex failover. Deploys roll out to one copy at a time, so a bad change hits one copy before it hits all (module 18).

When you estimate, say which failures your copies share. "Two copies, 99.9999%" is only honest with "in different zones, deployed one at a time". Without that sentence, the real number may be closer to a single copy's 99.9%.

</GoDeeper>

## Find the weakest link

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m2-availability-builder" />

Tap + copy under App. The two copies count as one very reliable part, and the page reaches 99.89%, about 9.6 hours down a year. The caption names the database as the weakest link. Add a third App copy: almost nothing changes. Add a second Database instead and the downtime drops under an hour. Copies help this much only if they fail independently.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: working through the next step">

Treat each parallel group as one part, then multiply the chain. The load balancer is 0.9999. The app pair is 0.999999. The database is 0.999. The product is about 0.998899, so the page is up 99.89% and down 0.0011 of the time, about 9.6 hours a year.

Now find where those hours come from, using the shortcut of adding downtimes: the load balancer gives about 0.9 hours, the app pair about 30 seconds, and the database 8.8 hours. The database is the weakest link. This is the availability version of the driving number from chapter 2.3: the one part that decides the result.

If the database also gets a second copy that can take over (a replica with failover, module 8), and the two copies are independent, the database becomes 99.9999% too. Then the page is about 0.9999 times 0.999999 times 0.999999, about 99.99%, close to 53 minutes down a year. Now the load balancer is the weakest link, and making it redundant is the next step.

Real failover is not perfect. Promoting a database replica can take seconds to minutes, and may lose the last writes if replication was asynchronous (module 8). So a replicated database is not quite as good as the formula says. Still, the method is the same every time: draw the chain, collapse each parallel group, multiply, and fix the part with the most downtime.

</GoDeeper>
