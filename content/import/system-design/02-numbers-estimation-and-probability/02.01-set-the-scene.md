A student team launches Daftar, a notes-sharing app for their university (a fictional app). Students photograph their lecture notes and upload them. The team buys one server with a 500 GB disk. Nobody multiplies anything.

```predict

correctOptionId: weeks
explanation: "2,000 students times 10 photos times 2 MB is 40,000 MB, which is 40 GB every day. 500 GB divided by 40 GB a day is 12.5 days. The disk is full during day 13."

```

## The disk fills on day 13

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m2-disk-fills" />

Every day, 40 GB of photos land on the disk. On day 6 it holds 240 GB. On day 12 it holds 480 GB. Halfway through day 13 it is full, uploads fail, and students lose the notes they tried to save. Nothing was broken in the code. The design simply did not fit the numbers.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what else would have gone wrong">

The disk is the first thing to fail here, but it is not the only thing that does not fit. The same numbers predict two more problems.

**Upload traffic.** 2,000 students times 10 photos is 20,000 uploads a day. If most students upload in the hour after their last lecture, that is about 20,000 uploads in 3,600 seconds, about 5.6 uploads a second, each 2 MB. That is about 11 MB of photos arriving every second in that hour. The team would need to check that their server's network link and its disk can take that. You will learn to turn "per day" into "per second" in chapter 2.3.

**Downloads.** Students do not only upload. If each student opens 30 photos a day, the server sends 2,000 times 30 times 2 MB, about 120 GB a day, three times more than it receives. Reads are often bigger than writes, and they drive different choices, such as a cache or a CDN (module 5).

**The fix is cheap if it comes first.** With the numbers in hand, the team has real options: shrink each photo before upload (a 2 MB photo of handwriting can often be much smaller), store photos in object storage that grows without a fixed disk size, or delete old drafts after the term. Each option costs an hour to decide before launch. After launch, the same decision is made in a hurry, with students' notes already lost.

This is a course story, so its numbers are assumptions chosen to make the arithmetic easy. A real disk also holds the operating system, logs and the database, so it would fill even sooner.

</GoDeeper>

## The line of maths nobody wrote

<MermaidDiagram title="Will 500 GB last the term?" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["2,000 students"] -->|"× 10 photos a day"| B["20,000 photos a day"]
  B -->|"× 2 MB each"| C["40,000 MB = 40 GB a day"]
  C -->|"500 GB / 40 GB a day"| D["12.5 days: full on day 13"]
`} legend="Three multiplications and one division, with a unit at every step, show the disk lasts under two weeks." />

Three steps and a unit at every step: that is the whole estimate. It takes two minutes and would have changed the design before launch. This module teaches you to make estimates like this quickly. You will feel how long things take, find the number that drives a design, and measure a real server to see where it breaks.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what this module covers">

System design decisions are usually decided by one or two numbers. How many requests arrive in the busiest second? How much data piles up in a year? How long does one trip to the database take? A design that ignores these numbers can look clean on a whiteboard and still fail on day 13.

The module builds the numbers in order. Chapter 2.2 gives you a feel for how long common operations take, from a CPU cache to a round trip across the world. Chapter 2.3 turns daily users into requests per second, storage per year and bandwidth, with units at each step, the same way as the picture above. Chapter 2.4 explains why the slowest requests matter more than the average. Chapter 2.5 shows why a server that is 90% busy makes requests wait far longer than one that is 50% busy, and gives you Little's law, a one-line formula that links traffic, waiting time and the number of requests in flight. Chapter 2.6 turns "three nines" into minutes of downtime a year.

Then you practise. In 2.7 you write the functions behind these numbers in JavaScript, with tests. In 2.8 you load-test a small Node.js server in your own repository and find, with your own measurements, the point where it starts to fall over.

A word on precision. An estimate is not a promise. It is a quick calculation that is right to within a factor of two or so. That is enough to tell 12 days from 120 days, which is the difference that matters here.

</GoDeeper>
