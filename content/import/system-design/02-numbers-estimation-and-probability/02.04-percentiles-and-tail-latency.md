Your dashboard says the average response time is 110 ms, and that sounds fine. Some users still say the app hangs. Both can be true at once. This chapter shows how, and which number to watch instead.

## The average hides the slow ones

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m2-average-hides" />

Ten requests: nine come back in 8 to 15 ms and one takes a full second. The average is 110 ms, a time not one request took. Half the requests finished in 11 ms or less, and one user waited a second. To see what users feel, sort the times and read them by rank.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the mean misleads">

The mean (average) adds every time and divides by the count: 8 + 9 + 10 + 10 + 11 + 12 + 12 + 14 + 15 + 1,000 = 1,101 ms, divided by 10 is 110.1 ms. One very slow request pulled the mean up ten times above what most users saw, and still far below what the slow user saw. It describes nobody.

Latency is almost always shaped like this: most requests are fast and a few are very slow. The slow few are called the <GlossaryTerm term="tail" definition="The slowest requests in a latency distribution, for example the slowest 1 in 100 or 1 in 1,000. Called the tail because they sit at the far right end of a latency chart." />. The Google SRE book advises using percentiles rather than the mean for exactly this reason: the mean hides the tail.

The tail is not rare for real people. If a service handles 1,000 requests a second and 1 in 100 is slow, that is 10 slow requests every second, 36,000 an hour. And the users who make the most requests, often your most active users, are the most likely to hit a slow one.

</GoDeeper>

## Percentiles: sort, then count

<MermaidDiagram title="Reading percentiles by nearest rank" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["10 times in ms, in arrival order: 12, 8, 1000, 10, 15, 11, 9, 14, 10, 12"] -->|"sort, smallest first"| B["8, 9, 10, 10, 11, 12, 12, 14, 15, 1000"]
  B -->|"p50: 50% of 10 = rank 5"| C["5th value: 11 ms"]
  B -->|"p90: 90% of 10 = rank 9"| D["9th value: 15 ms"]
  B -->|"p99: 99% of 10 = 9.9, round up to rank 10"| E["10th value: 1,000 ms"]
`} legend="Nearest rank: sort the values, take rank p percent of n rounded up, and read the value at that rank." />

The 99th percentile, p99, is the time that 99 of every 100 requests beat. To find it, sort the times and count. p50, the median, is the middle: 11 ms here. p99 is near the end: 1,000 ms. Watch p50 to see the usual case and p99 to see the slow users.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the nearest-rank method, sample sizes and a common mistake">

This course uses the <GlossaryTerm term="nearest-rank method" definition="One way to compute a percentile: sort the n values, compute rank = p / 100 times n, round up to a whole number, and return the value at that rank (counting from 1). The answer is always one of the measured values." />. Sort the values smallest first. Compute p / 100 times n and round up; that is the rank. Return the value at that rank, counting from 1. For p = 0 use rank 1, the smallest value. You will write this function in chapter 2.7.

Other tools use other methods. Some interpolate between the two nearest values, so their p50 of 10 and 20 is 15, a value nobody measured. For a large sample the methods agree closely; for a small one they can differ. When you compare numbers from two tools, check which method each uses. {/* source needed: documentation of a monitoring tool's percentile method (for example interpolation in a histogram) */}

**Sample size matters.** With 10 values, p99 is simply the slowest one; you would need at least 100 values for p99 to mean "1 in 100", and 1,000 for p99.9. In challenge 1 (chapter 2.8) each step sends hundreds of requests, so its p99 is close to the slowest few.

**Never average percentiles.** If server A's p99 is 100 ms and server B's is 900 ms, the p99 of both together is not 500 ms. It depends on how many requests each served and on the full shape of each list. To combine, merge the raw times (or histograms) and compute the percentile again.

**Percentiles in targets.** Service level objectives (module 17) are usually written with percentiles: "99% of requests finish within 300 ms". The Google SRE book recommends this shape, because it matches what users feel.

</GoDeeper>

## A page waits for its slowest call

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m2-fan-out-tail" />

One page calls three backends at once and needs every answer. Two reply in about 10 ms. The third is having a slow moment and takes a second. The page cannot answer until that last reply arrives, so the page takes a second too. When a request fans out, its time is the time of its slowest call.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: fan-out is everywhere">

<GlossaryTerm term="Fan-out" definition="When one incoming request triggers several calls to other services or servers, often in parallel, and needs their answers before it can reply." /> is normal in modern systems. A home page asks a profile service, a feed service and an ads service. A search query asks every shard of an index (module 9) and merges the results. A microservice calls three others, which each call two more (module 16).

Running the calls in parallel is right: the page pays the slowest call instead of the sum of all calls. In chapter 2.2, 20 queries in a row cost 20 round trips; 20 queries in parallel cost about one. But parallel calls bring the problem in this beat. Each backend may be fast almost always, yet the page waits for whichever one is slow right now.

Slow moments have many causes: a request stuck behind a queue (chapter 2.5), a background job on the same machine, a pause while the language runtime cleans up memory, a busy disk. None of them is a bug you can fix once. The next beat shows why they matter so much more under fan-out.

</GoDeeper>

## Rare slowness becomes normal

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m2-fan-out-play" />

Drag to set how many backends one page calls. Each backend is slow only 1 time in 100, and the page waits for the slowest. Press Load the page, or let it repeat, and watch the tally. At 1 backend almost every load is fast. At 20, about 1 load in 5 is slow. At 100, 63% are slow. Rare slowness becomes the normal case.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the arithmetic of 1 minus 0.99 to the n">

A page is fast only if every call is fast. If each call is fast with chance 0.99, and calls are independent, the chance that all n calls are fast is 0.99 multiplied by itself n times, written 0.99^n. The chance the page is slow is 1 minus that.

- n = 1: 1 - 0.99 = 0.01, so 1% of pages are slow.
- n = 10: 1 - 0.904 = 0.096, about 10%.
- n = 50: 1 - 0.605 = 0.395, about 39%.
- n = 100: 1 - 0.366 = 0.634, about 63%.

Dean and Barroso give the n = 100 case in "The Tail at Scale" (2013): servers that are slow (1 second) for 1 request in 100, and a request that needs 100 of them, make 63% of user requests take more than a second.

This changes how you set targets. If a page fans out to 100 backends and you want the page fast 99% of the time, each backend must be fast far more often than 99%: about 99.99% of the time, because 0.9999^100 is about 0.99. That is why large systems watch p99.9 and p99.99 of their backends, not only p99.

The independence assumption matters both ways. If slow moments are linked, for example one overloaded network switch slows many backends at once, the arithmetic is different: fewer pages see a slow call, but those that do may see many. The formula shows the shape of the problem, not an exact forecast.

</GoDeeper>

## Ask a second copy

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m2-hedged-request" />

Replica A is stuck. Instead of waiting, the page waits a short time, then sends the same request to replica B and uses whichever answer comes first. B answers fast, and the slow call no longer sets the page's time. This trick is called a hedged request, and it cuts the tail for a little extra load.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: hedged requests and their cost">

A <GlossaryTerm term="hedged request" definition="Sending the same request to a second copy of the data after a short wait, and using whichever answer arrives first. It cuts tail latency at the cost of some extra load." /> is one of the tail-cutting techniques described in "The Tail at Scale". It works because slow moments are usually short and usually hit one machine at a time. When replica A is in a slow moment, replica B probably is not.

The key choice is how long to wait before sending the second copy. Send it at once and you double the load on every replica. Wait until most requests would normally have finished, and only the slow few get a second copy, so the extra load stays small. Pick the wait from your own latency percentiles, then measure the extra load.

Hedging has limits. It only works when the request is safe to send twice, such as a read. Sending a payment twice is not safe unless the server can tell the two copies apart (idempotency keys, module 6). It also needs real copies of the data (replicas, module 8). And if every replica is slow because the whole system is overloaded, a second copy adds load exactly when there is least to spare, so hedging needs a limit on how many extra requests it may send.

**In AWS.** Amazon CloudWatch can report percentile statistics such as p99 for a metric, so you can watch the tail instead of the average. {/* source needed: Amazon CloudWatch documentation on percentile statistics */}

</GoDeeper>
