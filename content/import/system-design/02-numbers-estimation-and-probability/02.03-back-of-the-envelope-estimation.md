Sada is a voice-notes app for study groups (a fictional app). Students record short voice notes and play their classmates' notes. Before anyone draws a box, you will estimate how much traffic, storage and bandwidth Sada needs. The numbers below are assumptions you would agree with the team; the method is what you are learning.

## From people to requests per second

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m2-estimator" />

Start from people, not servers. 1 million daily users times 20 requests each is 20 million a day. Divide by 86,400 seconds, about 100,000, and you get about 231 a second. Tap 1 million and drag it sideways: every card follows, each with its unit. The circled card is the number that drives the design; you will come back to it at the end.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: units, rounding and the per-second trick">

Writing the unit on every line is the habit that catches most mistakes. If a line says "20,000,000" with no unit, you cannot tell whether the next step should divide by 86,400 or by 24. If it says "20,000,000 requests a day", the next step is obvious.

**Rounding to powers of ten.** Estimates are fast because you round. A day has 86,400 seconds; call it 100,000 (10 to the power 5). Then "per day" to "per second" is "drop five zeros". 20,000,000 a day becomes 200 a second. The true answer is 231, so the shortcut is about 14% low. That is fine for an estimate. If a decision depends on that 14%, the estimate is too close to call and you need a measurement.

**Useful anchors.** One million a day is about 12 a second. One billion a day is about 12,000 a second. A month has about 2.6 million seconds and a year about 31.5 million. Keep these in your head and most conversions take one step.

**Daily users, not total users.** An app may have 10 million sign-ups but 1 million people who open it on a given day. Daily active users (DAU) is the number that creates traffic. When someone gives you "users", ask which kind.

**Say your assumptions out loud.** "Each user makes 20 requests a day" is a guess. Write it down as a guess, so anyone can change it and redo the arithmetic. An estimate that hides its assumptions cannot be checked.

</GoDeeper>

## Size for the peak, not the average

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m2-peak-hour" />

Students record and play notes in the evening, not evenly all day. Sada's average is about 230 requests per second, but the evening rush is about three times that: about 700. A server sized for the average falls over every evening. Size for the busiest minute, and say what peak factor you assumed.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the peak factor and what to do with 700 per second">

The <GlossaryTerm term="peak factor" definition="How many times busier the busiest period is than the average. A peak factor of 3 means the busiest minute carries three times the average rate." /> of 3 is an assumption for this example. Real apps vary a lot: a study app peaks before exams, a sports app during a match, a shop on a sale day. The right number comes from your own traffic graphs, by the minute. A daily total hides everything that matters here.

Peak requests per second turn into servers. In chapter 2.5 you will meet Little's law: the number of requests in flight equals the arrival rate times the time each request spends inside. If one Sada request needs 10 ms of one worker's time, then at 700 requests a second you need 700 times 0.01 s, which is 7 workers busy on average. Chapter 2.5 also shows why you must not plan for all of them to be busy all the time: waiting time climbs fast near full use. So you add headroom, spare capacity above the peak.

Peaks can also be sudden. A teacher shares a voice note with 5,000 students at once, and they all press play in the same minute. Averages, even hourly ones, never show that. This is why later modules add queues (module 15) and rate limits (module 6): they protect the system when a peak is larger than the plan.

</GoDeeper>

## Storage: count, size and time

<MermaidDiagram title="Sada: voice-note storage per year" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["1,000,000 daily users"] -->|"× 1 in 10 record a note"| B["100,000 new notes a day"]
  B -->|"× 200 KB each"| C["20,000,000 KB = 20 GB a day"]
  C -->|"× 365 days"| D["7.3 TB a year"]
  D -->|"× 3 copies"| E["about 22 TB a year"]
`} legend="Storage is new items per day, times the size of each, times the days you keep them, times the copies you store." />

Storage is how many new items arrive, times their size, times how long you keep them. One in ten users records a 200 KB note each day: 20 GB a day, 7.3 TB a year. Keep three copies so one broken disk loses nothing, and Sada needs about 22 TB for every year it runs.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: sizes, copies and what people forget">

**Which kilobyte.** This course uses 1 KB = 1,000 bytes, 1 MB = 1,000 KB, and so on, because the arithmetic is easier and estimates do not need more precision. Operating systems often use 1 KiB = 1,024 bytes. The difference is 2.4% at KB and about 10% at TB, which an estimate can ignore, but say which you used.

**The working.** 100,000 notes times 200 KB is 20,000,000 KB. Divide by 1,000 to get 20,000 MB, and again to get 20 GB. Times 365 days is 7,300 GB, which is 7.3 TB. Times 3 copies is 21.9 TB, about 22 TB.

**Why three copies.** Disks and machines fail. Keeping several copies on different machines means one failure loses nothing. Three is a common choice: Amazon's Dynamo paper lists N = 3 copies as a common setting. Replication is the subject of module 8.

**What estimates often forget.** Metadata: each note also needs a database row with its owner, group, length and time, maybe 1 KB, which is small next to 200 KB but large if you have billions of rows. Indexes, which can be as big as the data they index. Backups, which are extra copies on top of replication. Growth: if users double every year, so does the yearly storage. A good estimate lists these, even if it only adds a rough percentage for each.

</GoDeeper>

## Bandwidth: bytes per second, then bits

<MermaidDiagram title="Sada: bandwidth for playing notes" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["1,000,000 daily users × 10 plays each"] --> B["10,000,000 plays a day"]
  B -->|"× 200 KB each"| C["2 TB a day"]
  C -->|"÷ 86,400 seconds"| D["about 23 MB per second"]
  D -->|"× 8 bits in a byte"| E["about 185 Mbit/s on average"]
  E -->|"× 3 peak factor"| F["about 560 Mbit/s at peak"]
`} legend="Bandwidth is bytes sent per second. Network links are sold in bits per second, so multiply by 8 at the end." />

Each user plays ten notes a day. That is 2 TB of audio leaving Sada every day, about 23 MB every second on average. Network links are measured in bits, and a byte is 8 bits, so that is about 185 Mbit/s. At the evening peak, about 560 Mbit/s. Sada sends out 100 times more data than it receives.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading bandwidth numbers">

**The working.** 10,000,000 plays times 200 KB is 2,000,000,000 KB, which is 2,000 GB or 2 TB a day. 2,000,000,000,000 bytes divided by 86,400 seconds is about 23,000,000 bytes, so 23 MB, per second. Times 8 is about 185 Mbit/s. Times the peak factor of 3 is about 556 Mbit/s, which rounds to 560.

**Bytes and bits.** Storage is counted in bytes (B). Network speed is counted in bits per second (bit/s, often written bps). Mixing them up is the most common bandwidth mistake, and it is off by exactly 8 times. Write "MB/s" or "Mbit/s", never just "M".

**Reads and writes.** Sada receives 20 GB a day of new notes and sends 2 TB a day of plays: about 100 times more data out than in. Most consumer apps look like this: many more reads than writes. That ratio is a design input. Read-heavy systems lean on caches and CDNs (module 5), which serve repeated reads from close to the user, so the origin servers do not send the same popular note thousands of times.

**Bandwidth costs money.** Cloud providers usually charge for data leaving their network, so 2 TB a day is also a monthly bill. This course does not quote prices because they change and differ by region, but an estimate of bytes out per month is the number a price sheet asks for.

</GoDeeper>

## Find the number that drives the design

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m2-storage-piles-up" />

Requests per second are a rate: if users stay the same, next year's peak is still about 700. Storage is a total: every year adds 22 TB, so year five holds 110 TB. That is Sada's driving number. It says notes belong in storage built to grow, such as object storage, not on one server's disk.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: rates, totals and the driving number">

The <GlossaryTerm term="driving number" definition="The estimate that most limits or shapes the design: the one that, if you got it wrong, would break the system first or cost the most." /> is the estimate that forces a design choice. To find it, ask of each number: what breaks first if this doubles?

**Rates do not pile up.** 700 requests per second at peak is the same next year if users and habits stay the same. You meet it with enough servers and headroom, and you can add servers when traffic grows.

**Totals pile up.** Storage grows every day the app runs, and nothing removes it unless you decide to. 22 TB a year becomes 110 TB in five years. No single disk holds that, so storage must spread across many machines. Daftar in chapter 2.1 failed on exactly this kind of number, only much sooner.

**What the driving number decides here.** Voice notes are large, written once and read many times. That points to object storage (module 5 and module 18), which spreads data across many machines for you, plus a CDN in front for the 560 Mbit/s of plays. A database still holds the small metadata rows. Without the estimate, a team might put notes in the database or on the app server's disk, and find out on day 13.

**Check the estimate against reality later.** Once Sada runs, compare these numbers with real dashboards every month. The first real numbers usually change at least one assumption, such as the share of users who record a note. Update the estimate, and see if the driving number changed.

</GoDeeper>
