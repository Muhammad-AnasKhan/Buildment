PasteBox is a small pastebin (a fictional product): you paste some text, get a short link, and anyone with the link can read it. It is small enough to design in one sitting, so you can watch every step of the method.

## Six steps, then around again

<MermaidDiagram title="The design loop" chart={`flowchart LR
  C["1. Clarify"] --> E["2. Estimate"]
  E --> S["3. Sketch"]
  S --> D["4. Deep dive"]
  D --> T["5. Trade-offs"]
  T --> F["6. Failure"]
  F -.->|what you learned| C
`} legend="Each step feeds the next. The dashed arrow is the point of the loop: what you learn late often changes an early answer." />

Every design in this course follows the same six steps. Clarify what to build. Estimate how big it is. Sketch the blocks. Dive deep into the hardest part. Name the trade-offs. Ask what fails. Then go round again, because a late step often changes an early answer. Here is the loop on PasteBox.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why a loop and not a list">

A list suggests you finish each step and never look back. Real design does not work like that. In the failure step below, you will find that PasteBox's single database is a problem. That sends you back to the sketch, and perhaps to clarify: "how much downtime is acceptable?" The second time round is faster, because most answers still hold.

The loop is also a way to stay calm when a problem is large or unfamiliar. When you do not know what to do next, ask which step you are on. If you cannot say how big the system is, you are not ready to sketch. If you are arguing about databases before anyone has said what the users do, go back to clarify.

You will use this loop in every case study in Part 4, and module 28 shows how it fits a 45-minute system design interview. There, the interviewer watches how you move through the steps as much as where you end up: whether you ask before you draw, whether your numbers support your boxes, and whether you name what you gave up.

</GoDeeper>

## Clarify: ask before you draw

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m1-paste-clarify" />

You ask the product owner three questions. How big can a paste be? Up to 1 MB. How long is it kept? 30 days. Can it be edited? No. Each answer removes work. No editing means a paste never changes after it is saved, which will make copies of it safe to keep anywhere. Write the answers down: they are the scope.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: PasteBox's full requirements">

Clarifying uses the three buckets from chapter 1.2. A good set of questions covers who the users are, what they do, how much, how fast, and what is out of scope.

**Functional.** A visitor can create a paste of up to 1 MB of text and gets a link with a short ID. Anyone with the link can read the paste. A paste disappears 30 days after it was created.

**Out of scope for now.** Accounts, editing, private pastes, custom links, search and syntax colouring. Each of these is a fair feature, and each would add work to every later step. Saying "not now" out loud is part of the design.

**Non-functional.** Reading a paste should be fast, say p99 under 200 ms on the server. Losing a paste before its 30 days is not acceptable. Short outages are acceptable: PasteBox is a free tool, so 99.9% a month is the target.

**Constraints.** One engineer, a small monthly budget, and the team knows Node.js and PostgreSQL.

**Assumptions to check later.** 100,000 new pastes a day, each read about 10 times, about 10 KB on average. Nobody gave these numbers, so they are written down as assumptions, ready for the next step.

</GoDeeper>

## Estimate: turn the day into seconds

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m1-paste-estimate" />

Divide by the 86,400 seconds in a day: 100,000 pastes a day is about 1.2 writes a second. With 10 reads per paste, reads are about 12 a second. At 10 KB a paste, kept 30 days, storage is about 30 GB. These are small numbers. One ordinary database server can hold and serve all of this.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the working, and what an estimate is for">

Here is the working, written the way you would on paper.

| Quantity | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Writes a second | 100,000 / 86,400 | about 1.2 |
| Reads a second | 1.2 x 10 reads per paste | about 12 |
| New data a day | 100,000 x 10 KB | 1,000,000 KB, about 1 GB |
| Data kept | 1 GB x 30 days | about 30 GB |
| Live pastes | 100,000 x 30 days | 3 million |

Two habits make this quick. First, round hard: 86,400 is close to 100,000, so "per day divided by 100,000" gives a per-second number within about 15%. Second, keep units next to every number, so you notice when kilobytes turn into gigabytes.

These are averages. Traffic is never flat: there is a busiest hour, and sometimes a spike. If you assume the busiest hour is 3 times the average, which is an assumption to check against real traffic, you get about 4 writes and 36 reads a second. Still small.

Why is "small" enough to decide anything? Rough 2026 measurements put a simple query to MySQL at about half a millisecond, so even one database connection could answer about 2,000 simple reads a second. 30 GB fits on one disk. So PasteBox does not need splitting, sharding or a fleet of caches, and the estimate just saved you from building them. Module 2 teaches estimation properly, with powers of ten, latency numbers and the cost of the busiest second.

</GoDeeper>

## Sketch: the simplest design that fits

<MermaidDiagram title="PasteBox, first sketch" chart={`flowchart LR
  B["Browser"] -->|create or read a paste| LB["Load balancer"]
  LB --> A1["App 1"]
  LB --> A2["App 2"]
  A1 --> DB[("PostgreSQL: pastes table")]
  A2 --> DB
  J["Nightly cleanup job"] -->|delete expired pastes| DB
`} legend="Two stateless app servers behind a load balancer, one PostgreSQL table, and a nightly job that removes pastes older than 30 days." />

Two app servers behind a load balancer, one PostgreSQL table, and a nightly job that deletes expired pastes. The second app server is not there for speed, because 12 reads a second is easy. It is there so one crash does not take PasteBox down. The cache, queue and CDN from chapter 1.3 are left out on purpose.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the API and the table">

The API has two requests:

```text
POST /pastes        body: the text          -> 201 Created  { "id": "aB3xY9" }
GET  /p/aB3xY9                              -> 200 OK with the text
                                            -> 404 Not Found if no such paste
                                            -> 410 Gone if it has expired
```

The table has four columns:

```sql
CREATE TABLE pastes (
  id         char(6)     PRIMARY KEY,   -- the short ID in the link; must be unique
  body       text        NOT NULL,      -- up to 1 MB
  created_at timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
  expires_at timestamptz NOT NULL       -- created_at + 30 days
);
CREATE INDEX pastes_expires_at ON pastes (expires_at);  -- lets cleanup find old rows fast
```

Why keep the text in the database and not in object storage? Pastes are small (10 KB on average, 1 MB at most) and there are only 30 GB of them, so one table is simpler and fast enough. If PasteBox allowed 100 MB files, the answer would flip: the database would store the ID and the file's name, and object storage would hold the file, as Daftar does with its PDFs.

The app servers are stateless: everything they need is in the database, so the load balancer can send any request to either one.

</GoDeeper>

## Deep dive: the paste ID

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m1-paste-ids" />

The hardest small part is the short ID in each link. Each app server picks 6 random letters and digits. The two servers cannot see each other's choices, so sometimes they pick the same one. The database's unique key is the only place that sees both. It refuses the second insert, and that app tries a new ID.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how often IDs clash, and the code">

With 26 lower-case letters, 26 upper-case letters and 10 digits, there are 62 choices for each character, and 62 to the power 6 is about 56.8 billion IDs. That sounds like plenty. But PasteBox keeps 3 million pastes alive, so a new random ID hits a taken one about 1 time in 19,000 (56.8 billion / 3 million). At 100,000 new pastes a day, that is about 5 clashes every day. Rare for one user, certain for the system.

Here is a near miss that many people write first: check whether the ID exists, and insert only if it does not. It looks right and passes every test on one laptop. But two servers can both check at the same moment, both see "free", and both insert. Only a unique key inside the database closes that gap, because the database handles the two inserts one after the other. The check is at best a shortcut; the key is the rule.

The insert with a retry looks like this (a sketch: `db.insert` stands for your database library):

```js
import { randomInt } from "node:crypto";

const ALPHABET = "abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyzABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ0123456789";

function newId(length = 6) {
  let id = "";
  for (let i = 0; i < length; i++) id += ALPHABET[randomInt(ALPHABET.length)];
  return id;
}

async function createPaste(db, body) {
  for (let attempt = 1; attempt <= 5; attempt++) {
    const id = newId();
    try {
      await db.insert("pastes", { id, body });
      return id;
    } catch (err) {
      if (err.code !== "23505") throw err; // 23505 is PostgreSQL's "unique violation"
    }
  }
  throw new Error("No free ID after 5 tries");
}
```

The retry has a limit, so a bug can never loop forever. Other designs exist. A single counter gives IDs that never clash, but every server must ask one place for the next number, and links become easy to guess (paste 1001 is next to paste 1000). Longer IDs make clashes rarer: 7 characters is 62 times more space. Module 10 shows how systems like Snowflake build unique IDs on many machines without a central counter.

</GoDeeper>

## Trade-offs: say no to a cache

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m1-paste-no-cache" />

A cache looks like an obvious addition. At 12 reads a second, it buys almost nothing: a simple query takes about half a millisecond, so the database is busy less than 1% of the time. A cache would cost money, add a part that can fail, and could serve stale copies. So PasteBox skips it, and writes down when that changes.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a trade-off, written out">

A trade-off is easier to defend when you write it in the same shape every time.

- **The choice:** no cache in front of the database.
- **Why now:** about 12 reads a second, and the database is under 1% busy (12 reads x about 0.5 ms is about 6 ms of work each second).
- **What it buys:** one less part to run, pay for and debug, and no stale copies.
- **What it costs:** nothing yet. If reads grow a hundred times, the database will feel it first.
- **The alternative:** a cache-aside cache such as Valkey in front of the database.
- **When to switch:** when one paste goes viral. Picture a link shared on a big social site: thousands of reads a second for one ID.

The clarify step already holds the best answer for that day. Pastes never change after they are saved. A copy that can never be stale can be kept by a CDN for a long time, with a header such as `Cache-Control: public, max-age=86400`. Then the CDN answers the viral link near each reader, and the database never sees most of those reads. This is why the loop goes round: an answer from step 1 decided a trade-off in step 5.

The napkin-math author warns that his numbers can be 2 to 3 times off. Even 3 times slower queries would leave the database under 3% busy, so the decision holds.

</GoDeeper>

## Failure: what breaks, what users see

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m1-paste-failover" />

Now ask of each block: what if it dies? When App 1 crashes, the balancer's health check notices and sends requests to App 2, and users barely notice. The database is different. There is only one, so if it dies, every request fails. PasteBox's next time round the loop starts here: a copy of the database on a second machine.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the failure table">

Go through the sketch one block at a time and write what happens when it fails.

| Block | If it fails | What users see | Plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Load balancer | No requests get in | PasteBox is down | Use a managed load balancer that runs on several machines |
| App 1 or App 2 | Health check removes it | A few errors at most, then normal | Already handled: two servers |
| Database | No reads or writes | Every request fails | Backups now; a standby copy next |
| Cleanup job | Expired pastes stay in the table | Nothing, if reads check `expires_at` | Make reads return 410 Gone after expiry, so the job only frees disk |

The last row shows a useful habit: design so that a failure does not break a requirement. If the API only trusted the nightly job, a job that stopped for a week would show week-old expired pastes. Checking `expires_at` on every read makes the job a cleaner, not a guard.

The database plan has two parts. Backups protect against losing data, but restoring one takes time. A standby replica, a copy kept up to date on a second machine, can take over much faster. PostgreSQL's built-in streaming replication is asynchronous by default, and its documentation says the standby is typically under one second behind if it keeps up with the load. So a failover could lose the last moments of writes. Is that acceptable for PasteBox? That question goes back to clarify, and module 8 is about answering it.

Health checks need care too. If App 1 checked the database in its health check, a slow database would make both apps look sick, and the load balancer could remove both at once. Simple checks that only ask "is this process alive and able to answer?" avoid that trap.

</GoDeeper>

## Play the whole loop

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m1-design-loop" />

Now run the loop on one picture. Tap the six steps in order and watch PasteBox grow: the scope, the 12 reads a second, the blocks, the ID clash. On Trade-offs, tap the cache and notice the database stays under 1% busy. On Failure, break the database, add a standby, then break it again. That fix is your next time round the loop.
