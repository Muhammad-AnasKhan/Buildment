## Your design note

Every system design in this course starts the same way: what must it do, how well, within which limits, and which number matters most. You have practised this on Daftar and PasteBox. Now do it for a real app that you use, so the method becomes yours rather than the course's.

Pick an app you know well as a user: a messaging app, a food delivery app, your university's registration portal, a mobile wallet, a ride app, or anything else. You do not need to know how it is really built, and you should not look it up. Write from what a user can see, and make your guesses clear. You are practising the first two steps of the loop, clarify and estimate, not trying to describe the company's real system.

A good note has four short parts. First, three to five functional requirements, written as actions a user performs, plus one thing you leave out of scope. Second, two or three non-functional requirements, each with a number: a percentile for speed, a percentage for availability, or a rule such as "never charge twice". Third, one or two constraints you can reasonably guess, such as the size of the team or a law about data. Fourth, the driving number: the one number that would change the design most if it were ten times bigger. Show how you got it, even if every input is a guess, and label the guesses.

Here is the kind of working the fourth part needs, for a different app. A school's exam results page might have 40,000 students who all check their results in the first 10 minutes after they are published. That is 40,000 / 600 seconds, about 67 students a second on average, and the first minute is surely busier than the average. The total number of students is not the driving number. The burst after publishing is, because a design that is fine for the rest of the year must survive those minutes.

What if you have no idea how many people use the app? Build the number from things you can guess. Start from people, not from requests: how many people in your city or university might use it, what share open it on a busy day, and how many times each one does the main action. Multiply, then divide by the seconds in the busy period. Your answer may be ten times off, and that is fine at this stage. A mentor is not checking whether your guess matches the real company. They are checking that every number has a reason, that the reasons are written down, and that you could change one input and redo the sum in a minute.

This note is also the first page of a habit you will repeat in every case study in Part 4 and in the capstone. There, the same four parts come first, before any boxes are drawn. Writing them now, for an app you know, is the cheapest practice you will get.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is listing features without a single number: "it must be fast and reliable" cannot be tested. The second is naming the total number of users as the driving number. Servers feel requests per second at the peak, not accounts. The third is choosing technology in the requirements: "it uses Redis" is a design decision, and it belongs later in the loop, once you know the numbers.
