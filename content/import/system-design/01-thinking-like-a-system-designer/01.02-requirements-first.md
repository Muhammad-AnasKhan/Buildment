Hana wants to rebuild Daftar before next term's exams. Before she draws a single box, she writes down what Daftar must do, how well it must do it, and the limits she has to work within.

## Functional: what users can do

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m1-functional-reqs" />

A student uploads notes, searches a course and opens a note. Each action is a functional requirement: something the system must do, usually a verb a user performs. Write each one as a short sentence you could test, such as "a student can find every note for CS101". If you cannot imagine a test for it, the sentence is not finished.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: writing functional requirements">

A functional requirement names an actor, an action and a result: "a signed-in student can upload a PDF of up to 20 MB, and it appears in search for its course". The actor matters, because "anyone can delete a note" and "only the owner can delete a note" are different systems. The result matters, because it is what your test checks.

Functional requirements turn almost directly into an API, the list of requests your server accepts. For Daftar:

| Requirement | Request it becomes |
|---|---|
| A student can upload a note for a course | `POST /notes` with the file and the course code |
| A student can list the notes for a course | `GET /courses/CS101/notes` |
| A student can open one note | `GET /notes/42` |

Just as important is what you leave out. Hana's first version has no comments, no likes and no private messages. Writing an "out of scope" list stops the design from growing while you are not looking, and it shows a reviewer, or an interviewer, that you chose a boundary on purpose. In an interview, three to five core features is usually enough. Say the rest are out of scope for now.

A common mistake is to write features as technology: "use Elasticsearch for search". That is a design decision, not a requirement. The requirement is "a student can find notes by course code and by words in the title". Keep the two apart, so you can still choose the technology later, when you know the numbers.

</GoDeeper>

## Non-functional: how well it works

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m1-quality-reqs" />

Both versions have search, so both meet the functional requirement. Version A answers in 4 seconds and version B in 0.3. The difference is a non-functional requirement: how fast, how available, how safe the system must be. Hana's target is search in under 1 second. A target with a number can be tested. "Fast" cannot.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the usual non-functional requirements">

Most systems need a target for each of these. You will not always get a number from the product owner, so propose one and write it down as an assumption.

- **Latency:** how long a request takes. State it as a <GlossaryTerm term="percentile" definition="p99 is the time under which 99 of every 100 requests finish. p50 is the middle value. Percentiles show the slow requests that an average hides." />, for example "p99 under 1 second", not as an average. A few very slow requests can hide inside a good average, and those are the users who leave.
- **Availability:** the share of time the system works. 99.9% sounds perfect, but a 30-day month has 43,200 minutes, so 99.9% still allows about 43 minutes of downtime. 99.99% allows about 4 minutes, and costs much more to reach.
- **Durability:** saved data is not lost, even when a disk or a machine dies. For Daftar: "a note that showed 'saved' is never lost".
- **Scalability:** the load the system must handle, usually at the busiest moment, not on average.
- **Consistency:** how quickly a change becomes visible. For Daftar: "a new note appears in search within 1 minute". Modules 8 and 11 are about this.
- **Security and privacy:** who can see and change what.
- **Cost:** what the system may cost to run each month.

The Google SRE book gives three words that you will meet all through the course. An SLI (service level indicator) is a measurement, such as the share of searches that finish under 1 second. An SLO (service level objective) is your target for it, such as 99%. An SLA (service level agreement) is a promise to a customer with a consequence, such as a refund, if you miss it. Most teams set their SLO stricter than any SLA, so they notice trouble before they owe anyone money.

</GoDeeper>

## Constraints: the walls you build inside

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m1-constraints" />

Constraints are facts you cannot change in this project. Hana's team has 2 engineers and 6 weeks. Twelve separate services are too many for 2 people to run. Building a search engine takes longer than 6 weeks. One app with a Postgres database fits. Constraints feel like bad news, but they save time: they remove options before you spend a day on them.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: kinds of constraints">

A requirement describes the result the users need. A constraint limits how you may reach it. Common constraints are:

- **People and skills:** how many engineers, and what they already know. A team that knows Postgres well should not switch databases in a 6-week project without a strong reason.
- **Time:** the deadline, which is often fixed by something outside the team, such as the exam calendar.
- **Money:** the monthly budget for servers and services.
- **Existing systems:** for example, students must sign in with the university's existing accounts.
- **Law and policy:** for example, student data must stay in a certain country.
- **Users' devices and networks:** many of Daftar's students read on phones with weak or expensive mobile data, so pages must be small. That one constraint changes image sizes, caching and how much code the page loads.

Why are 12 services too many for Hana? Each separate service needs its own deployment, monitoring and on-call care, and calls between services can fail in ways that calls inside one program cannot. Martin Fowler observed that the successful microservice stories he knew started as a monolith, one program, that grew too big and was split later. Systems built as many services from the start often ended up in serious trouble. Module 16 comes back to this decision.

Keep constraints apart from **assumptions**. "Students read on phones" is a fact you can check. "Most notes are under 5 MB" is a guess until you look at real uploads. Write assumptions down with a plan to check them, because a wrong assumption is a common reason a design fails.

</GoDeeper>

## One number drives the design

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m1-driving-number" />

At the exam peak, Daftar gets about 200 reads and 2 uploads a second, so reads outnumber uploads 100 to 1. That ratio is Daftar's driving number: the one that changes the design most. A read-heavy app can keep copies of popular notes close to students and answer from them. An app that mostly writes would need a different plan.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to find the driving number">

Start by listing the numbers that could matter: users, requests a second at the peak, the ratio of reads to writes, the size of one item, the total data stored, the latency target, and how many countries the users are in. Then ask one question of each: "if this number were ten times bigger, would my design change?" The number with the biggest yes is the one that drives the design.

For Daftar the answer is the read rate at the peak, and the shape of those reads. In exam week, many students read the same few notes for the same course. Answering those from a copy in memory, or from a server near the student, is far cheaper than asking the database 200 times a second. Chapter 1.3 names those blocks, and module 5 builds them.

Other systems have other drivers. For a chat app, it is often the number of connections that stay open at once. For a video site, it is the bytes sent each second. For a payment system, the driver may not be a rate at all but a rule: never take the same payment twice. Then correctness, not speed, shapes every choice.

Two traps are common. The first is to design for the total number of users instead of the peak rate, as the prediction above showed. The second is to design for the average. Daftar's average over a whole term may be 10 times lower than in exam week, but exam week is when students need it most. Size for the peak you must survive, and say which peak you mean.

</GoDeeper>

## Turn vague words into numbers

<MermaidDiagram title="From vague words to testable targets" chart={`flowchart LR
  F["fast"] --> F2["search p99 under 1 second"]
  A["always up"] --> A2["99.9% of each month: at most 43 minutes down"]
  M["many users"] --> M2["200 reads a second at the exam peak"]
  S["safe"] --> S2["a note marked saved is never lost"]
`} legend="Each vague word on the left becomes a target on the right that a test or a dashboard can check." />

Every vague word in a requirement hides a number. "Fast" becomes 99 of every 100 searches answering in under 1 second, written p99 under 1 s. "Always up" becomes 99.9% of each month, which still allows 43 minutes down. Now each requirement is a test you can run. The gate of this module asks you to do the same for an app you use.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: choosing a target, and what it costs">

Where does "under 1 second" come from? Sometimes from data: how long users waited before they left in the old version. Sometimes from the product owner. In an interview, from you: propose a number, say why, and move on. A stated number that is a little wrong is far more useful than no number, because everyone can now check it and argue about it.

Prefer percentiles to averages. If 99 searches take 0.2 s and one takes 30 s, the average is about 0.5 s, which looks fine, while one student in a hundred waits half a minute. A p99 target catches that student. Module 2 shows how to compute percentiles from your own measurements.

Every extra nine of availability costs more. 99.9% allows about 43 minutes down a month; 99.99% allows about 4 minutes, which leaves no time for a person to wake up and fix anything by hand, so you need automatic recovery. The SRE book calls the allowed failure an <GlossaryTerm term="error budget" definition="How much failure a target allows in a period. A 99.9% monthly target gives a budget of 0.1% of the month, about 43 minutes, to spend on outages and risky changes." />. A team that has used up its budget slows down its releases; a team with budget left can take more risk. Module 17 sets SLOs and error budgets for the capstone.

</GoDeeper>
