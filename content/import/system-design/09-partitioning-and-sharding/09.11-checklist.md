## Learning log

1. **Your keys:** Pick a table from an app you have built or use daily. What is its natural key? If you hashed it, which question would become expensive? If you ranged it, which writes would pile up?
2. **Your move:** In challenge 8 part 2, how many keys did hash mod N move, and how many did your ring move? What would that mean in hours of copying for a table of 2 TB at a copy speed of 100 MB a second?
3. **Your luck:** In part 3, which shard held the most sub-keys when you salted, and was it the same shard at 4, 8 and 16 ways? What would you do if you could choose the shard of each sub-key yourself?
4. **Your tail:** If each of 20 shards were slow 1 time in 100, about how many searches in 100 that ask all 20 would be slow? How would you avoid asking all 20?
5. **Your limit:** Think of a number you would watch to learn that a shard is getting hot before users complain. Where would you read it from?

## Your gate answer: partition a product of your own

A partition design is the step after estimation in a system design discussion. You have the numbers from module 2, and you know the single machine cannot hold or serve them. In an interview this is where you say which key you shard on, and defend it. In a real team it is where a wrong key costs months, because the key is hard to change once the data is large. A weak answer says "I would shard by user id" and stops. A strong answer ties the key to the queries, says what could go wrong, and says how the system changes when it grows.

Choose a product you know well: a chat app, a ride app, a marketplace like Suq, a school portal, a photo-sharing app. You choose the size and the assumptions. There is no single right key. A mentor checks the reasoning, not whether you picked the same key as someone else.

A good answer has six parts, each a few lines. First, the data and the main queries: the table you are splitting, its size or rate, and the two or three questions the app asks most. Second, the partition key and the rule: hash, range, or a compound key, and why it fits those questions. Third, the hot-key plan: which key could be hot in this product (a celebrity, a viral item, a big customer, a launch day), how you would notice it, whether the heat is reads or writes, and the fix. Fourth, the growth plan: what happens when you add a node, which way you keep keys from moving (ring with virtual nodes or fixed partitions), and where the partition map lives. Fifth, the queries that are not on the key: which one you would meet first, and whether you would use scatter-gather, a global index or another store. Sixth, one number from your own challenge 8 results, such as the keys moved or the p99 before and after salting, used as evidence for a choice.

Watch for five common mistakes. The first is a key with too few values, such as a status or a country: a few big values give a few busy shards, however many shards you buy. The second is a time-ordered key under range partitioning, which sends every new write to one shard. The third is forgetting skew: the busiest shard sets the limit, not the average. The fourth is believing that consistent hashing removes hot keys, when it only spreads keys and one key is one point. The fifth is salting without paying the read price: a salted key needs a read of every sub-key, and your answer should say so.

Before you submit, check your answer the way a reviewer would. Read your key and ask which of your main queries it serves in one shard, and which ones it does not. Pick your most likely hot key and say what share of traffic it would need before it overloads a shard, using a shard limit you can justify. Finally, compare one number with your challenge 8 results. Your salt count, for example, can be checked against the pattern you saw in part 3: the busiest shard did not become equal, however many sub-keys you used. A rough answer is fine. The point is to connect the design to something you measured.

Add the link to your challenge 8 repository folder, or to its part 3 `results.md`, in the link field. Your mentor will open your charts next to your design, because the two skills belong together: choosing a key on paper and checking it with a measurement.
