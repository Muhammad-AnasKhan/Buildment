Suq now hashes its keys, so the load should be even. Then a seller called Noor Crafts (a made-up seller) starts a flash sale. This chapter is about the case that hashing cannot fix: when the traffic is not spread across keys, because it all belongs to one key. Hold one number in your head: each Suq shard can take about 1,000 writes a second. Everything below is about keeping the busiest shard under that line, and about how you find out which shard that is.

## One seller, one overloaded shard

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m9-celebrity-key" />

Every order for Noor has the same key, so every one lands on shard 4. It takes 2,100 writes a second against a limit of 1,000, while the other three shards take 300 each. Hashing spread the keys evenly. It cannot spread the traffic of one key. This is called a hot partition. Look at the busiest shard, not at the average: the average here is a healthy 75%.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: skew, and where hot spots come from">

<GlossaryTerm term="skew" definition="Uneven load. Some keys or shards get much more traffic than the average, so the busiest part is the bottleneck, not the whole system." /> comes in three common forms. A hot key: one key, such as a celebrity or a viral item, gets a huge share. A hot range: keys in a sorted range are all written together, like the newest dates in chapter 9.2. And plain bad luck: with few keys, the hash puts the large ones on the same shard.

DynamoDB's engineers wrote in 2022 that hot partitions and throughput dilution were among the most common early problems they met [S44]. Each partition is designed for up to 3,000 read units and 1,000 write units a second [S40]. A table can have plenty of unused capacity overall and still reject requests, because the requests all hit one partition. The aim of this chapter is to avoid that.

Suq's numbers show it. The total of 3,000 writes a second is spread over four shards of 1,000 each, so on average everything is at 75% and looks fine. The average hides the problem. Shard 4 is at 210% and the rest are at 30%. Always look at the busiest shard, not the average, the same lesson as percentiles in chapter 2.4.

Facebook's memcache fleet met the read version of the same problem: hot keys under heavy load flooded the database, until leases cut peak queries from 17,000 to 1,300 a second in one test [S24]. Chapter 5.6 covers that fix.

</GoDeeper>

## More shards do not help; splitting does

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m9-celebrity-play" />

Drag the shard count up from 4 to 16 and watch the bars. The other shards cool down, but Noor's shard barely moves. Then tap a salt number. Splitting her key into sub-keys is what brings her shard under the limit of 1,000. Try 2, 4 and 8, and notice that the result also depends on where the sub-keys land.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind the picture">

These are the loads on the busiest shard, in writes a second, for the key `seller:noor-crafts` with 1,200 background writes a second over many keys and 1,800 for Noor. Each sub-key is `seller:noor-crafts#0`, `#1` and so on, and a shard is `hash(key) mod shards`, with the hash from chapter 9.7. The limit is 1,000.

| Shards | No salt | Salt 2 ways | Salt 4 ways | Salt 8 ways |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 2,100 | 1,200 | 1,200 | 975 |
| 8 | 1,950 | 1,050 | 600 | 600 |
| 16 | 1,875 | 975 | 525 | 300 |

Two lessons. Reading down the first column: more shards give almost nothing, because the key stays whole. Reading across: salting helps, but the sub-keys are placed by the hash, which is random, so they can collide. With 4 shards and 4 sub-keys, two sub-keys landed on shard 4 and it ran at 1,200, still over the limit. With 4 shards and 16 sub-keys the busiest shard ran at 1,088, worse than with 8 sub-keys, because of the same luck. Dropping sub-keys onto shards at random behaves like throwing balls into bins: the fullest bin is above the average.

The practical rules: use several times more sub-keys than the number of shards you want to share the key across, check the busiest shard after you change anything, and if the store lets you choose the shard of each sub-key, place them on different shards on purpose.

</GoDeeper>

## Salting: one key becomes four

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m9-salted-key" />

Noor's key becomes `noor#0` to `noor#3`, and each sub-key takes a quarter of her 1,800 writes a second. The hash sends them to shards 2, 4, 4 and 1. Shard 4 still gets two of them, so it runs at 1,200. Salting works, but the sub-keys land by luck, and reading Noor's total now means four reads.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to salt, and what it costs">

<GlossaryTerm term="salting" definition="Adding a small number to a hot key, such as noor#3, so that one key becomes several sub-keys that hash to different shards." /> has two sides. On a write, the app picks a random number from 0 to k-1 and writes to `key#number`. The writes spread over k sub-keys. On a read, the app must read all k sub-keys and combine them, for example by adding up counts or merging lists. The cost moves from the write path to the read path: writes are k times lighter on each sub-key, and reads are k times more numerous.

Choose k from the numbers. Noor's shard has a limit of 1,000 and already carries 300 of other work, so it has room for 700 more. 1,800 divided by 700 is 2.6, so you need at least 3 ways, and more for the bad luck above. Eight is a reasonable choice.

Salt only the keys that need it. Keep a short list of salted keys, so that readers know which keys to fan out. Everything else keeps a plain key and a one-shard read. A key that is salted by default makes every read k times more expensive for no reason.

Two things salting loses. Order: writes to one logical key no longer arrive in one place in order, so it does not suit a key whose updates must be applied in sequence, such as an account balance. And simple uniqueness: a rule like "only one record with this id" now spans k sub-keys.

</GoDeeper>

## Hot reads and hot writes need different fixes

<MermaidDiagram title="Choosing a fix for a hot key" chart={`flowchart TD
  H["One key or shard is too hot"] --> Q["Mostly reads, or mostly writes?"]
  Q --> RD["Reads"]
  Q --> WR["Writes"]
  RD --> R1["Cache the key in app memory or a cache tier (chapter 5.6)"]
  RD --> R2["Add read replicas for that shard (module 8)"]
  WR --> W1["Salt the key into sub-keys"]
  WR --> W2["Move the key to a shard of its own"]
  WR --> W3["Batch the writes, such as counter increments"]
`} legend="Reads can be served from copies. Writes have to land somewhere, so the key itself must be split, moved, or written less often." />

First ask whether the heat is reads or writes. Reads are the easier case: a cache or more replicas can serve copies of the same value. Writes must land somewhere, so the key has to be split into sub-keys, moved to a shard of its own, or written less often by batching.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: finding the hot key before it melts a shard">

Batching is the cheapest write fix when the writes are small counters. If a page counts views, the app can add the views in memory and write the total once a second. A key that took 1,800 writes a second now takes one. The price is that the counter can lose up to a second of counts if the app crashes, which is fine for views and wrong for money.

You cannot fix what you cannot see. Watch the load per shard and report the busiest, not the average. A simple alarm is "busiest shard is more than twice the average". Also keep a count of the busiest keys per shard, over a short window. A load test with all traffic on one key tells you your shard limit before a real sale does.

Some skew can be predicted. A sale, a launch, a celebrity account and a viral link are all known in advance. For these, salt or move the key before the event, not during it.

**In AWS.** DynamoDB tells you how it spreads load: the partition key is hashed, and each partition has a design limit of 3,000 read units and 1,000 write units a second [S40]. Its own paper treats hot partitions as a main early lesson [S44]. A good partition key has many values and an even share of traffic.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

Say: "Hashing balances keys, not the traffic of one key. For a hot key I first check whether it is reads or writes. Reads go to a cache or replicas. Writes I would split with a salt, writing to key plus a random number and reading all the sub-keys back, or give the key its own shard."

The follow-up is usually: "What does salting cost you?" Answer: reads fan out to all the sub-keys, ordering per key is lost, and you need to know which keys are salted. Mention that the hash can still put two sub-keys on one shard, so you check the busiest shard.

</GoDeeper>

explanations:
  - "The other keys spread over more shards, but the hot key is one key. It still goes to one shard, so that shard's load barely changes."
  - "A key never splits by itself. Only the app can split it, for example by salting it into sub-keys."
  - "Right. 40% of all writes still belong to one key, so one shard still takes them. The fix is to split the key, not to add shards."
  - "Moving data between shards does not remove the hot key's traffic. After the move, the same key still has the same load."
