Chapters 11.3 to 11.6 showed promises and what they cost. CAP and PACELC are the shortest way to name that cost. They are also the most often misquoted idea in system design, so this chapter reads them slowly.

## Cut the cable: refuse or answer

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m11-cut-the-network" />

Cut the cable between the regions. Then switch between "Refuse" and "Answer". With "Refuse", the cut-off region says try again later, and the copies never disagree. With "Answer", both regions take writes, and after the link returns the copies hold two values. You cannot have both.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what CAP says, and the one choice it is about">

**CAP** names three properties. C is consistency, and it means linearizability, the strict promise from 11.3. A is availability: every request to a node that has not failed gets an answer that is not an error. P is partition tolerance: the network may drop or delay messages between nodes. Partitions are not optional, because networks do fail. So the real choice is what to give up while one is happening: consistency or availability [S55].

In the picture there are three copies. Two are in the West and one is in the East. When the cable is cut, the West side has a majority, so it can keep writing and stay consistent. The East side cannot reach a majority, so under "Refuse" it says no. That is consistency without availability on the East. Under "Answer", the East accepts a write too, and the copies diverge. That is availability without consistency. Chapter 12.3 explains majorities.

The choice is only made during a cut. Brewer wrote in 2012 that the "2 of 3" way of telling it was always misleading, because partitions are rare and the choice does not have to be the same everywhere [S56].

</GoDeeper>

## The choice is per feature

<MermaidDiagram title="Choosing during a cut" chart={`flowchart TD
  F["A feature, during a network cut"] --> Q{"Is a stale or conflicting answer worse than no answer?"}
  Q -->|"yes: balance, unique username, seat hold"| C["Refuse: keep consistency, show an error or ask to retry"]
  Q -->|"no: like count, feed, profile photo"| A["Answer: stay available, fix differences afterwards"]
`} legend="The question is about harm. A wrong balance can cost money. A like count that is off for a minute costs nothing." />

CAP is not a choice for a whole system. The choice can be made for each feature, even for each request. A balance or a unique name should refuse during a cut. A like count can answer and settle later. Brewer said the same in 2012: the choice can be made many times in one system, at a fine grain.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how to decide, and why the labels CP and AP mislead">

Ask three questions for each feature. What is the harm of a stale or conflicting answer? What is the harm of an error? How hard is it to fix the differences afterwards? A bank balance fails the first: a double spend costs real money. A like count passes: two copies can add their counts together later.

Brewer's point is that a system does not pick one side forever. It can refuse writes to the balance table and keep serving the feed, from the same database, in the same cut [S56].

Kleppmann argues against the labels "CP" and "AP". Under CAP's exact definitions most real systems are neither: a store that is linearizable for writes but serves stale reads from replicas is not C, and a store that makes the minority side refuse writes is not A [S55]. Say what the system does in a cut instead: which side refuses writes, whether reads may be stale, what happens when the link returns.

</GoDeeper>

## No cut: pay in time or in freshness

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m11-latency-or-fresh" />

Now there is no cut. A write in the West must reach a copy in the East. Choose how far away that copy is. If the write waits for the East copy, East readers always see it, and the write takes a round trip. If it does not wait, the write is fast, and East readers can be stale for one trip.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers behind the picture">

The distances come from napkin-math: a round trip within a region takes about 0.25 ms, across North America (East to West) about 60 ms, EU West to North America East about 80 ms, and EU West to Singapore about 160 ms [S3]. A disk write with fsync takes about 0.3 ms [S3]. These are rounded numbers for reasoning, not benchmarks.

| Copy is | Write waits for it | Write does not wait | Stale window if it does not wait |
|---|---|---|---|
| in the same region (0.25 ms) | 0.55 ms | 0.3 ms | about 0.1 ms |
| North America East to West (60 ms) | 60.3 ms | 0.3 ms | about 30 ms |
| EU West to North America East (80 ms) | 80.3 ms | 0.3 ms | about 40 ms |
| EU West to Singapore (160 ms) | 160.3 ms | 0.3 ms | about 80 ms |

The waiting write costs the full round trip. The fast write is stale for one one-way trip, which is half the round trip. Both costs grow with distance, so a faraway copy makes the choice sharper.

This is the same trade as in chapter 8.3 (wait for a follower or not) and in chapter 10.6 (Spanner waits about 2 times epsilon so that order is safe). In each case, buying consistency means waiting.

</GoDeeper>

## Else: latency or consistency

<MermaidDiagram title="PACELC in one picture" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["Is there a partition?"] -->|"yes (P)"| PAC["Choose: stay available (PA) or stay consistent (PC)"]
  P -->|"no, else (E)"| ELC["Choose: be fast (EL) or be consistent (EC)"]
  PAC --> CLS["Your class is the two choices, for example PA/EL or PC/EC"]
  ELC --> CLS
`} legend="CAP covers only the top branch. PACELC adds the other one, which is the case that happens almost all the time." />

PACELC adds the part CAP leaves out. If there is a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency. Else, with no partition, choose Latency or Consistency. The "else" is the usual case, so it is the one you pay for every day. Dynamo and Cassandra are PA/EL, fully ACID systems are PC/EC, and PNUTS is PC/EL by default.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: reading a PACELC class, and a note on AWS">

Abadi proposed PACELC in 2010 because CAP says nothing about the normal case [S57]. A class has two parts. PA/EL means "in a partition choose availability, otherwise choose low latency": the system always leans toward answering fast, and accepts stale reads and conflicts. PC/EC means "always lean toward consistency": it waits for other copies and refuses when it cannot reach them. PC/EL, as in PNUTS by default, refuses in a partition but takes the fast path otherwise.

The labels are rough. Many systems let you choose per request, which is the finer grain Brewer asked for. Treat a class as a starting description, and check it against what the system does in your tests.

**In AWS.** DynamoDB lets a caller choose per read between eventually consistent and strongly consistent reads [S44]. The strong read waits, the eventual read is cheaper and may be stale: the EL or EC choice, per request. Global tables in their default mode (multi-Region eventual consistency) let every Region accept writes and settle concurrent writes by last writer wins. A multi-Region strong consistency mode offers strong consistency across Regions [S43]. Global secondary indexes are updated asynchronously [S41].

</GoDeeper>

## What the letters really mean

<MermaidDiagram title="CAP's words are narrow" chart={`flowchart LR
  C["C: consistency means linearizable (11.3)"] --> R["Partitions happen, so the real choice is C or A while one lasts"]
  A["A: every request to a working node gets a non-error answer"] --> R
  P["P: the network may drop or delay messages between nodes"] --> R
`} legend="The theorem uses these exact meanings. Everyday words like 'consistent' or 'available' are much looser." />

CAP's words are narrower than they sound. C means linearizable, the strict promise from 11.3, not "some consistency". A means every working node answers with no error. P means the network can drop messages, and it will. So the real question is what to give up in a cut. Most real systems meet neither definition exactly.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: three common mistakes">

**"Pick any two of three."** You cannot pick "no partitions". The honest reading is: while a partition lasts, choose between C and A [S55, S56].

**"If I need availability I must accept eventual consistency."** There are promises between the two. Causal consistency and read-your-writes can stay available in a cut if each client keeps using one server ("sticky available") [S46, S49]. Chapter 11.4 drew the ladder.

**"Our database is CP."** Which operations? Reads from replicas are often stale, and a minority side often refuses writes. Replace the label with a description of what each side does in a cut and what the reader sees. That is what an interviewer wants to hear, and it is what a design review needs.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

Say: "CAP says that during a network partition you choose between linearizability and availability. Partitions are not optional, so the choice is made for each feature: a balance or a unique name refuses during a cut, a like count answers and settles later. PACELC adds that even with no partition, I trade latency against consistency, usually by deciding whether a write waits for a faraway copy."

A follow-up an interviewer may ask: "Is your database CP or AP?" A good answer: neither label fits exactly. Say what happens in a cut: which side refuses writes, whether reads may be stale, and how conflicts are settled when the link returns.

</GoDeeper>

  - "Photos refuse writes and payments accept them"
  - "Both apps refuse every request until the link returns"

explanations:
  - "A wrong or doubled payment is expensive, and fixing it later may mean refunds. This plan treats money like a photo."
  - "Right. A photo that appears a moment late is harmless, so answering is fine. A payment that conflicts with another is costly, so the cut-off side should refuse."
  - "This is backwards. Refusing photos costs users for no gain, and accepting payments on both sides invites double spending."
  - "Refusing everything keeps consistency but gives up availability for photos, where a stale answer would have been fine."
