## Your gate answer: a consistency choice for a social app

Choosing a consistency model is where a design meets what users can see go wrong. A weak answer says "we use strong consistency" or "we use eventual consistency" for the whole app. A strong answer goes feature by feature. For each one it names the strange thing a user could see, picks the weakest promise that prevents it, says how the system provides it, and says what it costs.

The app for your answer is Rawi, the fictional social app for students from module 8. It has four features. **Likes:** a counter on each post. **Posts:** a student writes a post, then reloads their profile, and other students read the feed. **Balance:** each student has a small wallet used to pay for pinning a post. **Username:** every student has one, and no two may share it. The data is in two regions, with replicas that lag by a fraction of a second. You choose the numbers and the mechanisms. A mentor checks your reasoning, not whether your choices match anyone else's.

A good answer has four parts for each feature. First, the promise: pick a rung of the ladder from chapter 11.2, such as linearizable, causal, a session guarantee or eventual, and say it in a phrase. Second, the strange thing it prevents, or that you accept: a stale count, a post missing on reload, a balance that goes back, a duplicate name. Third, the mechanism: a leader read, a compare-and-set, a session token, a dependency carried by a reply, or nothing. Fourth, the price and the cut: what it costs in latency or leader load, and whether the feature refuses or answers if the link between regions is cut.

Watch for four common mistakes. The first is to give every feature the strongest promise, which makes likes as slow as payments. The second is to give every feature eventual consistency without saying what happens to a username, which lets two students register the same name. The third is to say "eventual" without a merge rule, so a concurrent edit is silently lost. The fourth is to forget the second device: a token that lives in one app does not protect a student who switches phones.

Your answer does not need to be long, but each sentence must carry a decision. "Likes use eventual consistency" says little. "Likes are a counter that each region increments and syncs later, so a reader may see a count that is a few seconds behind, and nobody is harmed" can be checked. Before you submit, test your answer the way a reviewer would. Take the balance, and follow one payment from the tap to the copy. Say which node answers, and what a second tap a moment later would show. Then cut the link between regions and say what the balance does for a student in the smaller region.
