Mariam uses a bank app (a made-up one). She deposits 500 and looks at her balance. A moment later she looks again. Watch the picture once, then ask yourself two everyday questions. What looks wrong? And what would you check first?

## Her balance went back in time

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m11-balance-back" />

Mariam deposits 500. Copy A of the data has the deposit, so the app shows 1,500. She looks again, and copy B answers. Copy B has not heard about the deposit yet, so it says 1,000. Nothing crashed, and each copy told the truth as it knew it. Still, her money seems to have vanished and come back.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why a bank keeps copies, and what went wrong here">

A bank cannot keep its data on one machine. If that machine fails, nobody can see a balance. If it is busy, every page is slow. So the data is copied to several machines, and any copy can answer a read. You met this in module 8: a leader takes the writes and copies them to replicas, a little later.

"A little later" is the whole problem. For a short time, copy B holds the old balance. If the app sends Mariam's second look to copy B, she sees the past. In chapter 8.4 the same thing happened to a forum comment, and a routing rule with a position token fixed it for one person. That fix answers one question: how do I stop Mariam from seeing this?

This module asks a bigger question: what does a system promise, in general? A balance that goes back in time is only one of many strange things a reader could see. Some promises forbid all of them and cost a lot of speed. Some forbid only a few and are cheap. You need a name for each promise, a way to check a recorded run against it, and a way to choose one per feature.

Mariam, the app and the numbers are a course story, not a real bank. Replication lag is usually well under a second: the PostgreSQL documentation says typically under one second [S38]. A balance that is wrong for a fraction of a second is still wrong.

</GoDeeper>

## Six promises, one question each

Every promise in this module answers one question: what may a reader see, given what was written? They run from strict to loose, and the strict ones cost time. By the end you will pick one for each feature of an app, and write down why. You already have two tools: session tokens from chapter 8.4, and clocks from module 10.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the road through this module, and how it uses what you know">

Chapter 11.2 gives you the picture used in the whole module: a history, which is a record of reads and writes drawn as bars on a timeline. A promise is then a rule that says which histories are allowed. Chapter 11.3 covers the strictest everyday rule, linearizability, where the system behaves as if there were one copy and every operation took effect at one instant. It also shows why compare-and-set, locks and unique usernames need it.

Chapter 11.4 relaxes the rule step by step. Sequential consistency drops the link to real time. Causal consistency keeps only the order between things that could have caused each other, and it fixes the case where a reply shows up before its question. That case was left open in chapter 8.4, and the reason is that it needs two related writes. Chapter 11.5 turns the per-user promises of 8.4 into code, with session tokens. Chapter 11.6 asks what "eventually" really promises. Chapter 11.7 puts a price on each choice with CAP and PACELC.

Module 10 matters here because many promises are about order, and order needs a clock or a substitute for one. In 10.6 you saw Spanner wait out its clock doubt so that a write is visible to everything that starts after it. That guarantee has a name in this module, and you will use it. Lamport and vector clocks from 10.3 and 10.4 come back as the way a system tracks "this could have caused that".

Then you practise. In 11.8 you write a linearizability checker. In challenge 10 (11.9) you measure three anomalies on lagging replicas and remove them with session tokens. The quiz and the gate ask you to choose a promise for each feature of a social app: likes, posts, a balance and a username.

</GoDeeper>
