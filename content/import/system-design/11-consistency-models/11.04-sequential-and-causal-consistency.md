Linearizability, from 11.3, is strict: every operation takes effect at one instant in real time. Two weaker promises drop parts of it. **Sequential** consistency keeps one order that every client agrees on, and keeps each client's own order, but drops the link to real time. **Causal** consistency keeps only the order between things that could have caused each other.

## One history, three verdicts

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m11-one-history-three-verdicts" />

Pick a history, then read the three verdicts. A stale read fails linearizable but passes sequential and causal. Two readers who see two writes in opposite orders fail sequential but pass causal. A reply seen without its question fails all three. Tap a read to change its value and watch the verdicts change.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the exact rules behind the three verdicts">

**Linearizable** (11.3): one legal order that also respects real time. **Sequential**: one legal order of all operations in which each client's own operations stay in the order it issued them. There is no real-time rule, so a read can be placed "earlier" than a write that finished before it [S48]. **Causal**: build a "could have caused" relation. A client's earlier operation comes before its later one. A write comes before any read that returned its value. Chain these together. Then each client needs its own legal order of all the writes plus its own reads that keeps this relation. Two writes that are not related may be ordered differently for different clients [S46, S49].

That gives the four histories in the picture:

| History | Linearizable | Sequential | Causal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh read | yes | yes | yes |
| Stale read | no | yes | yes |
| Two readers, two orders | no | no | yes |
| Reply before question | no | no | no |

Each row fails everything the row above fails, and one more. That is the ladder from 11.2 in action.

A useful link back to 8.4: a store with one leader and lagging replicas, where each client keeps a read-your-writes token and a monotonic-reads token, is sequentially consistent. The leader's log is the one order. The tokens keep each client's own reads and writes in order. It is not linearizable, because a read may still be behind a write that finished. We checked this on 2,000 random small histories (three clients, two replicas, seven operations each, a course experiment). With both tokens, all 2,000 were sequential and causal, and 677 were linearizable. With read-your-writes and a sticky replica but no monotonic-reads token, only 1,975 were sequential, because a fallback to the leader followed by a replica read could still send a client back in time. This is a check on small cases, not a proof.

</GoDeeper>

## Unrelated writes, two orders

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m11-two-orders" />

Ada writes 1 and Bao writes 2, with no link between them. Reader C gets 1 first, then 2. Reader D gets 2 first, then 1. Causal consistency allows this, because the two writes are unrelated. Sequential consistency forbids it: it needs one order that everyone sees.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why allowing two orders makes a system cheaper, and what it leaves open">

"Unrelated" means concurrent in the sense of chapter 10.4: neither write could have caused the other, because no message or read links them. A causal store may deliver such writes to each copy in whatever order they arrive. It only delays a write if something that write depends on has not arrived yet. No global order has to be agreed first, so a copy can keep working while other copies are cut off.

That is why causal consistency can stay available during a network cut when clients stay on one server (a "sticky available" model [S46, S49]). A sequential store cannot: to agree one order for everyone, copies must talk.

The price is shown by the end of the picture. Reader C last saw 2 and Reader D last saw 1. If both writes were to the same key, the copies now disagree on its value. Causal consistency does not say how to settle that. Something else must: last writer wins, or a merge rule. Chapters 13.5 and 13.6 take that up.

</GoDeeper>

## A reply before its question

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m11-reply-first" />

A question and its reply live on two shards. The reply's copy is up to date at once, and the question's copy is slow. The reader sees the reply, then finds no question. The first write caused the second, but the reader saw them in the wrong order. This is a consistent prefix violation.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what consistent prefix means, and why 8.4 could not show it">

Terry's guarantee called **consistent prefix** says a reader sees a prefix of the sequence of writes: if you see write number 7, you see every write before it [S51]. Here the writes are "ask the question" and then "post the reply". Seeing only the second one is not a prefix.

Chapter 8.4 introduced the idea, but its picture had one writer and one record, so it could not show two related writes in the wrong order. This chapter reproduces it. In the history terms of this chapter, the picture is the last preset: the replier reads the question, then writes the reply, so the question comes before the reply in the causal relation. A reader who sees the reply and then the question list without the question breaks causal consistency, and so also sequential and linearizable.

This cannot happen inside one log. If the question and the reply are two entries of one leader's log, a replica applies them in log order, so it never has the reply without the question. It happens when related writes live in different places that copy at different speeds: two shards (module 9), two regions with their own leaders (8.6), or a cache in front of a database. The forum and its shards are a course example.

</GoDeeper>

## The reply carries its question

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m11-reply-waits" />

The reply names the question it follows. The reader sees "after question 1", holds the reply back, and asks the questions copy for question 1. It waits until the copy has it, then shows both. The reader pays a short wait. In return it never sees an answer without its question.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: three ways to keep related writes in order">

**Keep related writes in one log.** Choose the shard by thread, so the question and its replies share a key and a shard (module 9). One log keeps their order for free. The cost is that a very busy thread becomes a hot shard.

**Let each write carry what its writer had seen.** The replier read question 1, so the reply carries "after question 1". A reader that sees the reply learns that it needs question 1, and reads only from a copy that has reached it, or waits, as in the picture. This is the writes-follow-reads promise from Terry's session guarantees [S50]; the causal model implies it [S46]. The writer's knowledge can be a log position per shard, as in 11.5, or a vector clock from chapter 10.4, which can also tell concurrent writes apart. The cost is a small piece of metadata on every write, and a wait or a trip to the leader when a copy is behind.

**Read everything from one snapshot.** Read the question and the reply from the leader, or from one replica at one position. This is simple and costs leader load.

Do not fix this with timestamps. A reply's timestamp can be smaller than its question's when two servers' clocks differ, as chapter 10.2 showed. Cause and effect need a rule that follows the causes, not a clock that only approximates real time.

</GoDeeper>

## Causal can stay up during a cut

<MermaidDiagram title="Which promises survive a network cut" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["Strict serializable, linearizable, sequential: some requests must wait or fail in a cut"] -->|"weaker"| B["Causal and read your writes: sticky available, if a client keeps one server"]
  B -->|"weaker"| C["Weaker models, such as eventual: totally available"]
`} legend="Weaker promises ask for less agreement between copies, so more of them keep answering during a network cut." />

Not every promise needs the whole network. Causal consistency can keep serving during a cut, as long as each client sticks to one server. Linearizable and sequential cannot: some requests must wait or fail. That is why causal is a popular middle: stronger than "eventually", and still available.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: sticky available, and a common mistake">

The Jepsen map sorts models into three groups. Models at or above sequential cannot be totally available. Models at or above read-your-writes can be at most "sticky available": a client keeps working if it keeps talking to the same server. Weaker models can be totally available [S46]. Causal is sticky available [S49].

A common belief is "if you need availability, you must accept eventual consistency". The map shows it is false. Between those two ends there are real promises, such as causal consistency and session guarantees, that survive a cut [S46, S49].

"Sticky" has a price. If a client's server dies, it moves to another server that may be behind. Until that server catches up, the client can see time go backwards once. Chapter 11.5 shows how a session token removes that. Chapter 11.7 returns to the cut and asks what a system should do when it cannot keep every promise at once.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

Say: "Sequential consistency keeps one order everyone agrees on, but drops real time, so a read can be stale. Causal consistency keeps only cause before effect, so unrelated writes can show in different orders. For a thread of posts I would choose causal: a reply must never appear before its question, and it can stay available during a cut."

A follow-up an interviewer may ask: "How does the system know the reply depends on the question?" A good answer: the reply carries what its writer had seen, such as a position in each shard or a vector clock. A reader waits until those dependencies are visible before it shows the reply.

</GoDeeper>
