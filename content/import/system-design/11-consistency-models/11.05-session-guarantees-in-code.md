Chapter 8.4 fixed two promises with one position token. Terry and colleagues named four of them in 1994 [S50]. They are promises to one user, so they are called session guarantees. A **session** is one user's run of requests on one device. This chapter gives each promise its own number and its own rule, so you can write them as code. The rules are short. The hard part is knowing which one fixes which anomaly, so you will switch them on one at a time.

## Four promises, four numbers

<MermaidDiagram title="What a session token holds" chart={`flowchart TB
  T["Session token"]
  T --> A["wrote: position of my last write"] --> RYW["Read your writes: read only from a copy at or past wrote"]
  T --> B["seen: highest position my reads showed"] --> MR["Monotonic reads: read only from a copy at or past seen"]
  T --> C["write number: 1, 2, 3 and so on"] --> MW["Monotonic writes: apply my writes in number order"]
  T --> D["deps: what I had seen when I wrote"] --> WFR["Writes follow reads: show a write only after its deps"]
`} legend="Each promise needs one number and one rule. The first two rules choose which copy answers a read. The last two rules order writes." />

A session token is a few numbers the user's app carries. "wrote" protects the writer. "seen" stops time going backwards. The write number keeps one user's edits in order. "deps" keeps an effect from showing before its cause.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the four guarantees in plain words">

**Read your writes.** After you write, your own later reads show it. Terry's paper uses a password change as the example: you change it on one server and then log in on another that has not heard yet [S50].

**Monotonic reads.** Once a read has shown you a state, your later reads never show an older one. It protects any reader, even one who never writes.

**Monotonic writes.** Your writes are applied in the order you sent them. If you edit your name twice, everyone ends with the second edit.

**Writes follow reads.** If you write after reading something, your write is shown only after that thing is shown. A reply follows the question it answers. This is the rule that fixed the reply in chapter 11.4.

Causal consistency implies all four, so a causal store gives them without a token [S46]. The reverse is a design question: if the store gives only eventual consistency, a token on the client can add the ones you need. Read your writes is "sticky available" at best: a client must keep using one server to stay available during a cut [S46]. Chapter 11.2 gave the ladder.

</GoDeeper>

## Switch each promise on and watch

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m11-session-reads" />

Two readers, five reads. Amna wrote the comment, and Omar only reads it. Switch on "my own writes" and Amna's reads are fixed, but Omar's are not. Switch on "no going back" and Omar's are fixed. Watch how many reads the leader has to take.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the numbers, and what each switch costs">

The story reuses chapter 8.4. Amna writes a comment at time 0, which is position 1 in the leader's log. Replica 1 gets it at 150 ms and replica 2 at 600 ms (course numbers, like a PostgreSQL replica under heavy writes [S38]). Amna reads at 100 ms and 350 ms. Omar reads at 200, 350 and 500 ms. With no promise, reads take turns between the replicas.

| Switched on | Amna's reads missing her comment | Times Omar's view went back | Reads on the leader |
|---|---|---|---|
| none | 2 | 1 | 0 of 5 |
| my own writes | 0 | 1 | 1 of 5 |
| no going back | 2 | 0 | 0 of 5 |
| both | 0 | 0 | 1 of 5 |

Read the table as three lessons. Each promise fixes its own anomaly and nothing else. "No going back" costs nothing here, because a replica that is ahead was available. "My own writes" costs one leader read: at 100 ms neither replica has her comment, so only the leader can answer. The leader read is the price of being sure.

An alternative to the leader read is to wait a few milliseconds for a replica, as the router in chapter 8.8 can. Waiting keeps the leader quiet and makes the page slower. Pick by which you can afford.

</GoDeeper>

## Writes can break too

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m11-session-writes" />

Two stories about writes. Amna edits her name twice, and the second edit overtakes the first on the way. With no write number, the older edit wins. Omar replies to a question he read, and Hana sees the reply first. Switch on each promise and see what changes.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why writes need a number and a dependency">

**Monotonic writes.** The two edits take different network paths, and the second one arrives first. The leader applies them in arrival order, so the name ends as the older edit. Each write carries a number from the session: 1, then 2. The leader holds edit 2 until it has applied edit 1. The cost is a small wait when the network reorders, and a stuck queue if edit 1 is lost, so a real system also needs a timeout and a retry.

In a single-leader store where the client waits for each reply before sending the next write, monotonic writes come for free, because the writes cannot overtake each other. They matter when a client sends writes without waiting, when retries reorder them (chapter 15.4), or when more than one node accepts writes (8.6).

**Writes follow reads.** The reply carries what Omar had seen: question 1. A reader who sees the reply inherits "I need question 1" and reads that shard from a copy at or past 1, or from the leader. The cost is a few bytes of metadata on each write and sometimes a leader read.

Both promises are examples. The same pattern works for a comment and its like, or an order and its payment.

</GoDeeper>

## The token stayed on her phone

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m11-two-devices" />

Amna posts from her phone, which now holds the token. She opens the forum on her laptop. The laptop has no token, so the router may pick a replica that is behind, and her comment is missing. A token kept in one app protects only that app.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where the token should live">

A token in a cookie, a header or app storage belongs to one browser or one app install. A second device starts empty. A cleared cookie also looks like "no token". Chapter 8.4 pointed out the same gap.

Two fixes are common. Keep the user's latest write position on the server, keyed by user ID, and let the router look it up on every request. Or treat any user who wrote in the last few seconds as a leader reader. The first is exact and needs a small shared store that is itself fast and safe. The second is simple and wrong when the lag is longer than the window.

Keep the token small and boring. It holds numbers, not user data, and the server should sign it or check it so a client cannot invent a position far in the future and force every read onto the leader. With many shards, the token grows by one entry per shard the user touched. Prune entries that no replica is behind any more.

</GoDeeper>

## The whole rule in one picture

<MermaidDiagram title="One read, step by step" chart={`flowchart TD
  R["A read arrives with the session token"] --> N["need = the largest of wrote, seen and inherited that are switched on"]
  N --> Q{"Is any replica at or past need?"}
  Q -->|"yes"| P["Pick one of those replicas"]
  Q -->|"no"| L["Read from the leader, which has every write"]
  P --> U["Raise seen to what the read showed. Add what it depended on to inherited."]
  L --> U
`} legend="Every read asks how far along a copy must be. After the read, the token only goes up." />

Every read asks one question: how far along must the copy be? The answer is the largest position that any switched-on promise needs. Pick a copy that has reached it, or fall back to the leader. After the read, raise the token. That is all four promises in about thirty lines.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the token in JavaScript, and a run you can check">

This is deliberately small. It keeps one map per number, and the functions only ever raise a position. Challenge 10 builds the full client and runs it on a lagging forum.

```js
const newToken = () => ({ wrote: {}, seen: {}, inherited: {} });
const raise = (map, shard, position) => {
  map[shard] = Math.max(map[shard] ?? 0, position);
};

// After a confirmed write: remember the position (read-your-writes).
function afterWrite(token, shard, position) {
  raise(token.wrote, shard, position);
}

// After a read: remember what it showed (monotonic reads), and what the
// entry it showed depended on (writes-follow-reads).
function afterRead(token, shard, saw, deps = {}) {
  raise(token.seen, shard, saw);
  for (const [other, position] of Object.entries(deps)) raise(token.inherited, other, position);
}

// A write carries what its writer had seen and written.
function depsForWrite(token) {
  const deps = {};
  for (const map of [token.seen, token.wrote]) {
    for (const [shard, position] of Object.entries(map)) raise(deps, shard, position);
  }
  return deps;
}

// How far along a replica must be to answer this session's next read.
function needed(token, shard, on) {
  return Math.max(
    on.ryw ? token.wrote[shard] ?? 0 : 0,
    on.mr ? token.seen[shard] ?? 0 : 0,
    on.wfr ? token.inherited[shard] ?? 0 : 0
  );
}

// First replica that has reached the position, or the leader.
function pickNode(replicas, need) {
  const ready = replicas.find((r) => r.applied >= need);
  return ready ? ready.name : "leader";
}
```

Run it with replica `r1` at position 8 and `r2` at position 5, and all three promises on:

```text
Amna after writing position 7 needs 7 -> r1
Amna after writing position 9 needs 9 -> leader
Omar after seeing 8 needs 8 -> r1
Hana needs questions >= 4
a reply written by the replier carries {"questions":4}
```

Amna's second line is the interesting one: both replicas are behind her write at 9, so the leader answers. Hana's line is writes-follow-reads: the reply she read depended on question 4, so her next read of the questions shard needs a copy at 4 or more.

Three parts are left out. Choosing among several ready replicas (the router in 8.8 picks the least used). Quarantining a replica whose position moves backwards, such as one restored from a backup. And a cap on leader reads during a lag spike. Without the cap, the promises are safe but a lag spike sends every recent writer to the leader, which may be the reason the replicas fell behind.

To debug a router, log three things for every read: the token, the node chosen, and that node's position. A broken promise shows up as a read whose node position is below what a switched-on promise needed.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

Say: "Session guarantees are per-user promises: read your writes, monotonic reads, monotonic writes and writes follow reads. I give the user's session a token with a position per shard. A read goes to a replica at or past the largest position the promises need, and falls back to the leader. After each read the token goes up, never down."

A follow-up an interviewer may ask: "What if the user switches from a phone to a laptop?" A good answer: the token must not live only in the client. Store the user's latest write position on the server keyed by user ID, or send recent writers to the leader for a short time. Name the cost: a lookup per request, or more load on the leader.

</GoDeeper>
