Souq is a small online shop (a made-up app). A customer pays, and the payments server tells the orders server. An engineer merges both servers' logs to check one order, and the story comes out in the wrong order. Look at the picture below and ask yourself one everyday question: what looks wrong, and what would you check first?

## Two logs, one story, wrong order

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m10-log-merge" />

Payments stamps "paid" .060 with a clock that runs 60 ms fast. Orders gets the message 12 ms later and stamps "confirmed" .012 with a correct clock. Sort both logs by timestamp and the effect comes before its cause. No code is wrong. Two machines each have their own clock, and the two clocks disagree.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where the 60 ms comes from">

A computer clock is a counter driven by a quartz crystal, and no crystal is perfect. Kleppmann's notes give the scale: 1 ppm (part per million) is 1 microsecond gained or lost every second, which is 86 ms a day, and most computer clocks are correct to within 50 ppm [S52]. At 50 ppm, a clock that has not been corrected for 20 minutes is off by 50 microseconds times 1,200 seconds, which is 60 ms. That is the gap in the picture.

Two things make the gap matter. First, the two events are linked: the payment caused the confirmation, and a message travelled between them. Second, a person looks at the merged log and trusts the order. Tools that merge logs from many servers sort by timestamp, so any clock gap larger than the message travel time can reverse a pair of events like this one.

Not every pair of events has a true order worth keeping. If two customers pay at the same moment on two servers, nobody can say which came first, and either order is fine. The order matters when one event could have caused the other, or when a person saw the first before starting the second. That distinction comes back in 10.3 and 10.4.

Souq is a fictional composite. The 60 ms gap and the 12 ms travel time are course numbers.

</GoDeeper>

## Five tools, one question at a time

Physical clocks are close but never exact. Counters order events by cause but know nothing about real time. Hybrid clocks join the two. TrueTime admits the doubt and waits it out. You build all of them in challenge 9.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the module covers, and why it comes before consistency models">

Chapter 10.2 shows why clocks drift, how time sync pulls them back, and why a program must use a different clock to measure how long something took. Chapter 10.3 drops real time and counts events instead: a Lamport clock gives every event a number so that a cause always has a smaller number than its effect. The price is that a smaller number does not prove a cause.

Chapter 10.4 adds vector clocks, which keep one counter per node and can tell "happened before" from "at the same time, with no link". Chapter 10.5 combines a physical clock and a counter, so timestamps stay close to real time and still respect cause and effect. Chapter 10.6 is Google Spanner's answer when no message links two events: say how wrong the clock may be, and wait that long before telling anyone the write is done. Chapter 10.7 turns to IDs, which often carry a timestamp and so inherit every problem above.

Then you write the code. In 10.8 you implement Lamport, vector and hybrid clocks with graded tests. In challenge 9 you run them on a simulated network, measure drift, and build an ID generator that survives a clock going backwards.

Module 11 asks what a database promises when it reads and writes copies of data. Those promises are all statements about order: "you always see your own write", "everyone sees a reply after its question". You cannot judge a promise about order until you know what order a system can know. That is why clocks come first.
