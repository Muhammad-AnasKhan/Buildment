## Two writes, 1 ms apart

Server A saves a write and stamps it 12:00:00.000. Server B saves another write and stamps it 12:00:00.001. Which came first? The obvious answer is "A, by 1 ms". But in 10.2 you saw that every clock drifts, and that NTP only pulls it back to roughly the right time. Say each server knows its clock could be wrong by up to 7 ms in either direction.

Then A's write really happened somewhere between 11:59:59.993 and 12:00:00.007. B's write really happened somewhere between 11:59:59.994 and 12:00:00.008. The two ranges overlap almost completely. B's write could have come 13 ms before A's, or 15 ms after it. The clocks alone cannot tell you the order. The 1 ms difference is smaller than the doubt in each reading, so it means nothing.

This is why an honest timestamp is a range and not a single number. A clock does not know the true time. It knows its own reading and, if it is built carefully, how far that reading can be from the truth. This chapter is about a system that takes that range seriously: Google's TrueTime, and the Spanner database built on it (Corbett et al., OSDI 2012). By the end you can explain what to do with two overlapping ranges. You can also explain how commit wait makes order safe, and what it costs per write.

## What the earlier clocks give you, and what they miss

You already have three tools from this module. Lamport clocks (10.3) give an order that respects cause and effect. But they only see events linked by messages, and their numbers have nothing to do with real time. Vector clocks (10.4) can tell "happened before" from "concurrent", but they also only see messages. Hybrid logical clocks (10.5) stay close to wall time and still respect cause and effect, again by passing timestamps along with messages.

All three fail in one case, shown here with a made-up example. Alice removes Bob from her friends on server A. Her app shows "done". She then posts a photo, and the request happens to go to server B. Nothing passed from A to B. The link between the two writes went through Alice's eyes and fingers, not through a message the database could stamp. If her app carried a timestamp from A's reply into the request to B, a hybrid clock could order them. But if she switched from her laptop to her phone, there is nothing to carry.

The promise Spanner makes for this case is called <GlossaryTerm term="external consistency" definition="If transaction T1 finishes before transaction T2 starts, T1 gets a smaller commit timestamp than T2. The order holds even when nothing passes between the two servers. The Spanner paper says it equals linearizability, a strong guarantee you will meet in module 11." />. In plain words: if T1 commits before T2 starts, T1's commit timestamp is smaller than T2's. Why does Alice care? A reader who asks for "the state at time t" must never see her photo while Bob is still her friend. If the photo got the smaller timestamp, a read between the two timestamps would show exactly that.

## TrueTime: a clock that admits doubt

TrueTime is an interface with three calls (Spanner paper, section 3, Table 1). `TT.now()` returns an interval, `[earliest, latest]`, and guarantees that the true time of the call is inside it. `TT.after(t)` returns true if time `t` has definitely passed. `TT.before(t)` returns true if `t` has definitely not arrived yet. Half the width of the interval is called <GlossaryTerm term="epsilon" definition="The uncertainty of a TrueTime reading: half the width of the interval returned by TT.now(). The true time is within epsilon of the clock's reading." />. A smaller epsilon means a narrower range and less doubt.

TrueTime keeps epsilon small with hardware. The paper describes a set of time master machines in every data centre. Most have GPS receivers with their own antennas. The rest, which the paper calls Armageddon masters, have atomic clocks. GPS and atomic clocks fail in different ways, so using both protects against a fault in either.

Every machine also runs a daemon, a small background program, that polls several masters. It throws out any master whose time disagrees with the others, and syncs the local clock to the rest. Between polls, the daemon assumes the local clock drifts at a worst-case rate. So the uncertainty it reports grows steadily until the next poll.

The paper reports the numbers. Epsilon rises steadily between polls and drops back at each one, a shape called a sawtooth. It moves from about 1 ms to about 7 ms over each poll interval, so it is about 4 ms most of the time. The daemon polls every 30 seconds and assumes a drift of 200 microseconds per second. Thirty seconds of that drift adds up to 6 ms. About 1 ms more comes from the network delay to the masters. The paper also notes that epsilon can spike when time masters are down or machines are overloaded. When epsilon grows, Spanner gets slower, not wrong. You will see why below.

## Predict, then run Alice's two transactions

The canvas replays Alice's story on two servers. T1 removes Bob as a friend on server A at true time 20 ms. Alice sees "done", waits a short gap, and T2 posts her photo on server B. Each server picks its commit timestamp as the latest edge of its TrueTime range. In the default run, epsilon is 4 ms and the gap is 2 ms. A's clock is 3.6 ms fast and B's clock is 3.6 ms slow. Neither server knows how wrong its clock is. Each only knows its range.

Spanner can also make a server wait before it says "done". That is called commit wait, and it is off for now.

**What this shows.** Two commits on two servers whose clocks are each off by up to epsilon. Each commit's timestamp is the top of its server's range. When the two commits are closer together than the two clocks disagree, the timestamps can contradict the real order. Commit wait removes that risk by delaying "done" by about 2 times epsilon.

**Try this.** The canvas suggests three experiments. Raise the gap with commit wait off. Turn commit wait on with a 1 ms gap. Then shrink epsilon with commit wait on. Before the first one, write down the gap at which you expect the order to flip. Then try one more on your own. With commit wait off, switch the clocks to "Both exact" and compare the overlap with the order.

**What changed.** In the default run, s1 was 27.6 ms and s2 was 22.4 ms. The photo was stamped before the unfriend, so a read at 25 ms shows the photo with Bob still a friend. Raising the gap fixes it only once the gap is larger than how far apart the two clocks are. Here that is 7.2 ms, so 8 ms in whole steps. With commit wait on, Alice sees "done" at 28 ms instead of 20 ms. The photo then commits at 30 ms with s2 at 30.4 ms, and the order is correct for every gap and every clock case. The unfriend paid 2 times epsilon, 8 ms, to get there.

**Limits of the model.** Each clock's error is fixed for the run and stays inside epsilon. Real errors change over time, and real epsilon grows between syncs. Network time between Alice and the servers is left out, and so are the locks, replication and Paxos that real Spanner also uses. The model shows why commit wait works, not how fast a real database is.

You may have noticed something odd. With commit wait on, the canvas still says the two ranges overlap: A's is 19.6 to 27.6 and B's is 22.4 to 30.4. Yet the order is correct. With "Both exact" clocks and no wait, the ranges also overlap (16 to 24 and 18 to 26), and the order is also correct. An overlap does not mean the order is wrong. It means the clocks cannot prove the order is right. Commit wait does not stop the ranges overlapping. It makes the timestamps safe whatever the clocks are doing. That is the only thing a database can rely on, since it never knows which clock case it is in.

## How commit wait works

The diagram shows the steps on server A, and the one step on server B that benefits. Follow the order: the timestamp is chosen first, the wait comes after, and "done" comes last.

<MermaidDiagram title="Commit wait on server A, then Alice's next write on server B" chart={`sequenceDiagram

    A->>A: is TT.after(s1) true yet?

  Note over B: s2 is larger than s1

The guarantee follows from three short facts. First, A picks s1 as `TT.now().latest`, the top of its range (the paper's start rule, section 4.1.2). Second, A does not reply until `TT.after(s1)` is true, that is, until even the earliest possible true time is past s1 (the commit wait rule). So when Alice sees "done", true time is already later than s1. Third, Alice's next write starts after she sees "done", so it starts at a true time later than s1. Server B picks s2 as the top of its own range, which is never earlier than true time. So s2 is larger than s1.

Notice what is missing from that argument: any message from A to B. The two servers never talk. The order comes from A refusing to reply early, plus both servers using honest ranges.

The cost is the wait. A picks s1 at the top of its range, then waits for the bottom of its range to pass s1. The range is 2 times epsilon wide. So the paper says the expected wait is at least 2 times the average epsilon (section 4.2.1). With epsilon near its typical 4 ms, that is at least 8 ms.

The paper also measured the wait. In its single-replica benchmark, commit wait was about 5 ms (section 5.1). That is less than 8 ms, so epsilon in that test must have been lower than the typical 4 ms. A separate point is how much of the wait a user feels. Spanner does other commit work during the wait, such as replicating the write with Paxos (a consensus protocol you will meet in module 12). Overlapping hides part of the wait from the total latency. It does not make the wait itself shorter.

This is why better clocks make a faster database. Halve epsilon and you halve the wait on every write. That is the reason to pay for GPS receivers and atomic clocks: they buy lower write latency. It is also why a large epsilon is safe but slow. If a time master fails and epsilon grows, every commit waits longer, but no timestamp is ever wrong.

## Commit wait in code

Here is the rule as a small JavaScript sketch. `ttNow()` is fake. It pretends `Date.now()` is the true time, adds a hidden clock error, and returns a range. Real code would get the range from a time service. The rest is the real logic.

```js
// Fake TrueTime for learning. Date.now() plays the role of true time.
const EPSILON_MS = 4;
const HIDDEN_ERROR_MS = 3; // this clock is 3 ms fast, but callers never see this

function ttNow() {
  const reading = Date.now() + HIDDEN_ERROR_MS;
  return { earliest: reading - EPSILON_MS, latest: reading + EPSILON_MS };
}

const sleep = (ms) => new Promise((resolve) => setTimeout(resolve, ms));

async function commitWithWait(saveWrite) {
  const s = ttNow().latest;          // 1. timestamp at the top of the range
  await saveWrite(s);                // 2. make the write durable at s
  while (ttNow().earliest <= s) {    // 3. commit wait: until TT.after(s)
    await sleep(1);
  }
  return s;                          // 4. only now tell the client "done"
}
```

Time it with a `saveWrite` that takes 0 ms. It returns after at least 8 ms, which is 2 times `EPSILON_MS`. Timers add a little more. On Windows, a 1 ms timer can take about 15 ms, so you may see 15 to 20 ms there. Now make `saveWrite` take 5 ms. The total does not grow, because the save happened during the wait. Notice also that `HIDDEN_ERROR_MS` never changes the length of the wait. The wait depends only on epsilon, so the code is safe without knowing how wrong the clock is.

Two mistakes are easy here. Replying before the loop ends breaks the guarantee, and nothing will fail in tests until two writes land close together. Using `Date.now()` directly as the timestamp, with no range, is the 12:00:00.000 trap from the start of the chapter.

## Back to the two writes 1 ms apart

Now answer the opening question properly. A's write reads 12:00:00.000, B's reads 12:00:00.001, and epsilon is 7 ms. There are two cases, and they need different answers.

If A's write finished before B's write started, and someone saw it finish, then a real order exists. Users can notice it. Commit wait handles this case. A waits about 14 ms (2 times 7) before replying, so anything that starts after the reply gets a larger timestamp.

Now say the two writes were running at the same time, and neither user saw the other's result. Then there is no real order anyone could observe. The writes are concurrent, in the sense of 10.4. The database may put them in either order. What it must do is pick one order and show the same one to every reader. The timestamps already do that. B's is larger, so every reader sees A's write first. That order is a choice, not a fact, and that is fine.

## Without atomic clocks

Most teams do not have GPS antennas in their data centres. They still have options, each with a cost.

The first is the hybrid logical clocks of 10.5. They order every write that is linked by a message, with no waiting. For the Alice case, the client must carry the last timestamp it received into its next request, much like the position token in 8.4. If the user moves to a second device, that link is lost.

The second is commit wait with a wider epsilon. The rule works with any honest bound. A clock synced by ordinary NTP over a normal network has a much looser bound than TrueTime, as 10.2 showed. So each write would wait longer. The guarantee is the same, and the price is higher.

The third is to let one node hand out every timestamp, so there is only one clock. That removes the uncertainty problem. But every write now makes an extra trip to that node. The node also becomes a bottleneck and a single point of failure, the same trade you saw with the leader in module 8.

## In AWS

AWS runs the Amazon Time Sync Service, which uses satellite-connected and atomic reference clocks in each Region. EC2 instances can sync to it. AWS also publishes ClockBound, an open-source daemon and client library (MIT or Apache 2.0, your choice). ClockBound returns a pair of timestamps, earliest and latest, built from the clock error bound of the local clock. That is the same shape as `TT.now()`, so you can write `TT.after`-style checks and commit wait on EC2.

How small is the bound? An AWS post from November 2023 gave typical clock error bounds of under 100 microseconds over NTP. On supported instances using the PTP hardware clock, it was under 40 microseconds. Take those as a dated example, not a promise for your instance. If they held, commit wait at 2 times the bound would be well under 1 ms. Measure your own bound before you plan around a number.
