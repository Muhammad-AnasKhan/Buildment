Souq's two servers were both set to the right time at noon. By evening they disagree, and nobody touched them. Where does the gap come from, and how big can it get?

## Clocks drift apart on their own

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m10-drift-apart" />

Every clock counts the ticks of a quartz crystal, and no crystal is exact. A clock that is 50 ppm fast gains 50 microseconds every second. After one hour it is 180 ms ahead. A clock that is 50 ppm slow is 180 ms behind. Two servers that started together are now 360 ms apart.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what a ppm costs">

The unit is parts per million (ppm). 1 ppm means a clock gains or loses 1 microsecond every second, which is 86 ms a day. Most computer clocks are correct to within 50 ppm, and temperature changes the rate [S52]. At 50 ppm that is 4.3 seconds a day: 50 times 86.4 ms.

| Drift | Per hour | Per day |
|---|---|---|
| 1 ppm | 3.6 ms | 86 ms |
| 10 ppm | 36 ms | 0.86 s |
| 50 ppm | 180 ms | 4.3 s |

The gap that matters for ordering is the gap between two clocks, not the error of one. If one clock runs fast and the other slow, their errors add up. Two clocks that are each 50 ppm off, in opposite directions, move apart by 100 microseconds every second. If both run fast by the same amount, the gap between them stays small, even though both are wrong.

</GoDeeper>

## A sync pulls clocks back, not to zero

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m10-sync-pulls-back" />

To fix drift, a server asks a time server for the time, using the Network Time Protocol (NTP). Then it corrects its own clock. The result is good, not perfect. On a quiet local network the error can stay within 1 ms. On wider networks it depends, and a few ms is common. Two clocks that both just synced still differ a little.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how a sync works, step and slew">

A sync measures the trip as well as the time. The client notes when it sent the question (t1) and when the answer arrived (t4). The time server notes when it received the question (t2) and when it replied (t3). The time spent on the network is (t4 - t1) - (t3 - t2). If the trip out and the trip back took equal time, the client's clock error is ((t2 - t1) + (t3 - t4)) / 2. The "equal time" guess is the weak point: a slow trip in one direction only looks like clock error. The NTP documentation calls the accuracy of this guess "It All Depends" on the network [S64].

{/* source needed: the four-timestamp offset formula is standard NTP, but the dossier lists only the NTP FAQ headline accuracy figures (S64), not the formula. */}

The client can fix its clock in two ways. It can step the clock, which jumps it to the new time. Or it can slew the clock, which runs it a little faster or slower until the gap closes. Slewing keeps clocks within a few milliseconds, depending on the network [S52]. A step can move the clock backwards, and the next beat shows what that does.

Between syncs the clock drifts again. So the error is not constant. It grows after each sync and drops at the next one, a shape called a sawtooth. Chapter 10.6 shows this shape for Google's data centres.

</GoDeeper>

## Drag the drift and the sync interval

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m10-drift-sync" />

Drag to set the drift and how often the clocks sync. The line is the gap between the two clocks: it climbs, then drops at each sync. Two writes land 1 ms apart. While the gap is bigger than 1 ms, the later write can get the smaller timestamp. Find how often the clocks must sync to keep that order safe.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the model behind the picture">

The picture assumes one clock runs fast by d ppm and the other slow by d ppm, and both are exactly right at each sync. After T seconds without a sync, the gap is 2 times d times T microseconds. At 50 ppm the largest gap, just before the next sync, is:

| Sync every | Largest gap |
|---|---|
| 1 second | 0.1 ms |
| 10 seconds | 1 ms |
| 1 minute | 6 ms |
| 10 minutes | 60 ms |
| 1 hour | 360 ms |
| 1 day | 8.6 s |

Now the order. Write 1 happens at real time t1 on the fast clock and gets timestamp t1 + e1. Write 2 happens at t2 on the slow clock and gets t2 + e2. Write 1 really came first, but the timestamps say the opposite whenever t2 + e2 is not larger than t1 + e1. That is when the true gap t2 - t1 is smaller than the clock gap e1 - e2. Writes 1 ms apart need a clock gap under 1 ms, which with 50 ppm each way means a sync every 10 seconds or less.

The model leaves out the error a sync itself leaves behind. Even right after a sync, two clocks can differ by up to about 1 ms on a quiet network [S64]. So with writes 1 ms apart, even a perfect sync schedule cannot guarantee the order. Chapter 10.6 deals with that.

</GoDeeper>

## A clock step makes time run backwards

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m10-wall-vs-monotonic" />

A job really takes 400 ms. While it runs, the wall clock is stepped back one second: by a time correction, by a person, or by a leap second. End minus start is now minus 600 ms. A monotonic clock only counts forward, so it still says 400 ms. Use it to time things.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: leap seconds, a real outage, and the code">

The earth's rotation is not perfectly steady, so a leap second is sometimes added to the world's clock time (UTC). For one second the wall clock repeats or stands still. A 2012 Linux kernel bug turned a leap second into a livelock, a state where the machine keeps running but makes no progress. Google-style smearing avoids this: it spreads the extra second over a day, so no clock ever repeats [S52]. Amazon Time Sync does the same on its NTP service, but not on its PTP hardware clock [S61]. The standards body that sets the length of the second (CGPM) decided to stop leap seconds by or before 2035 [S61].

<RealWorldEvent title="A leap second broke Cloudflare DNS" when="1 January 2017" summary="Cloudflare's DNS software timed things by subtracting two wall-clock readings. At the leap second one duration came out negative. The negative number reached a function that picks a random number up to a limit, called rand.Int63n, which panics on a negative limit. About 0.2% of DNS queries failed at the peak, until 06:45 UTC." lesson="Time can go backwards. Measure durations with a monotonic clock, never with the difference of two wall-clock readings." />

You can see the failure with fake clocks. `wall` plays `Date.now()` and `mono` plays `performance.now()`:

```js
// A job takes 400 ms. The wall clock is stepped back 1 s while it runs.
let realMs = 0;                       // real time passing, in ms
let stepMs = 0;                       // total step applied to the wall clock
const wall = () => 1_700_000_000_000 + realMs + stepMs;  // plays Date.now()
const mono = () => realMs;                                // plays performance.now()

const wallStart = wall();
const monoStart = mono();
realMs += 200;                        // 200 ms of work
stepMs -= 1000;                       // the wall clock is stepped back 1 s
realMs += 200;                        // 200 ms more work

console.log("wall clock says:", wall() - wallStart, "ms");
console.log("monotonic says: ", mono() - monoStart, "ms");
```

It prints `wall clock says: -600 ms` and `monotonic says:  400 ms`. In Node.js the real timer is `performance.now()`, which counts milliseconds from an arbitrary start and does not follow wall-clock steps. `process.hrtime.bigint()` does the same in nanoseconds.

## Choose the clock by the question

<MermaidDiagram title="Which clock for which job" chart={`flowchart TD
  Q["What is the time for?"] -->|"how long something took"| M["Monotonic clock"]
  Q -->|"a time to show or store"| U["Wall clock, stored in UTC"]
  Q -->|"which of two events on two machines came first"| N["Neither alone: read 10.3 to 10.6"]
`} legend="The question decides the clock. Ordering events across machines is a third question that no single clock answers." />

For how long, use the monotonic clock. For a time people read, use the wall clock and store it in UTC. For which event came first on two machines, neither is enough, because both can be wrong by milliseconds or more. The next chapters give up on real time to get an order you can trust.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: habits, and In AWS">

Four habits follow from this chapter. Compute timeouts, retries and durations from a monotonic clock. Store wall-clock times in UTC and in one unit, such as milliseconds since 1970. Never treat two servers' timestamps as exact: assume they differ by at least a few milliseconds. And watch each server's clock offset the way you watch its disk, so a clock that has lost sync shows up before it corrupts a log.

**In AWS.** The Amazon Time Sync Service gives EC2 instances a time source backed by satellite-connected and atomic reference clocks in each Region, and it smears leap seconds on its NTP service [S61]. Chapter 10.6 has its error bounds and the ClockBound library.

Sources: Kleppmann, Cambridge Distributed Systems notes [S52], for drift, slewing and the 2012 leap second bug. The NTP FAQ [S64], for accuracy on quiet and wide networks. Amazon Time Sync Service documentation [S61], for smearing and the end of leap seconds. Cloudflare's post on the 2017 leap second [S133], for the outage. The Souq servers are a fictional composite.
