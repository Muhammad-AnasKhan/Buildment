## Your gate answer: two writes 1 ms apart

This is the question that started the module. Two writes arrive 1 ms apart on two different servers. Each server stamps its write with its own clock. How do you decide which write came first, and what does your choice cost? In an interview this comes up whenever a design has two writers and a rule such as "the latest write wins". In a real team it comes up the first time a bug report says "my change was overwritten by an older one".

A weak answer says "use timestamps" and stops. A strong answer separates cases. First, ask whether the two writes are linked. If one write caused the other, through a message, through a client that carried a token from the first reply to the second request, or through a chain of those, then a logical or hybrid clock gives the right order with no waiting. The cost is a small stamp on every message, and the order is only as good as the links. If the two writes are not linked, for example one person on a laptop and then the same person on a phone, no message joins them.

Second, say what plain wall-clock stamps give you, with a number. With two clocks that were synced and that drift, the gap between them is a few milliseconds at best, so writes 1 ms apart can come out in the wrong order. Your part 1 numbers show how often. Third, name the honest options for unlinked writes: accept that the order is unknown and keep both versions, as a vector clock allows; bound the clock error and wait it out before saying "done", as TrueTime and commit wait do, at a cost of about 2 times the clock uncertainty on every write; or send every write through one node that hands out the order, which removes clock doubt at the price of a round trip, a bottleneck and a single point of failure.

End with the cost of the option you chose, in words and, if you can, in milliseconds, and say when you would switch to another option.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is saying a Lamport or vector clock gives the real order: they give an order that respects cause and effect, and they say nothing about real time. The second is saying a time sync fixes the problem: a sync leaves an error, and with writes 1 ms apart the error is the problem. The third is naming commit wait without its price: it makes every write slower by about 2 times epsilon, and it only works if the clocks have a known error bound.

A good cost line is short and has a unit. For a hybrid clock it can read: "eight extra bytes on each message, no waiting, and the order holds only for writes that are linked." For commit wait it can read: "about 2 times the clock uncertainty on every write, so 8 ms or more when the uncertainty is 4 ms." For a single ordering node it can read: "one extra round trip on every write, and no writes at all while that node is down." Compare your own line with these. If it says "some latency" or "a bit more complexity", it is not finished.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right choice.
