A Lamport number cannot tell "this came before that" from "these are unrelated". Souq's shopping cart needs exactly that difference. Hana adds milk on her phone while her sister adds tea on the laptop, and two servers store the two edits.

## Each node keeps a counter for every node

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m10-vector-build" />

A vector clock is a list with one counter per node, here [A, B, C]. An event adds 1 to the node's own slot. A receive takes the larger value in every slot first, then adds 1 to its own slot. After the two messages, C holds [2,2,1]. It knows of 2 events at A, 2 at B and 1 of its own.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the rules, and how this relates to Lamport">

The rules are the Lamport rules, repeated for every slot. A local event: add 1 to your own slot. A send: add 1 to your own slot, then attach the whole vector. A receive: for each slot take the larger of your value and the message's value, then add 1 to your own slot.

A Lamport clock is a vector squeezed into one number. The vector keeps what the single number loses: which node each event came from. Reading a vector tells you how many events at each node this event has "heard of", either by doing them itself or through a chain of messages.

Dynamo, Amazon's key-value store, used vector clocks to find versions of an item that had grown apart, and left it to the reader to reconcile them [S45]. Kleppmann's notes set vector clocks against Lamport clocks and show what the extra slots buy [S52].

The price is size. A vector has one slot for every node that can write, so it grows with the cluster, and it travels with every message and every stored version. Real systems keep the vector short by dropping the oldest entries once it gets long, which can hide some ordering.

</GoDeeper>

## Compare two vectors slot by slot

<MermaidDiagram title="Comparing two vectors" chart={`flowchart TD
  S["Two vectors, x and y"] --> Q1{"Every slot of x at most y?"}
  Q1 -->|"yes, and they differ"| B["x happened before y"]
  Q1 -->|"yes, and equal"| E["same event"]
  Q1 -->|"no"| Q2{"Every slot of y at most x?"}
  Q2 -->|"yes"| A["y happened before x"]
  Q2 -->|"no"| C["concurrent"]
`} legend="The test has three outcomes. Concurrent means each vector is bigger than the other in at least one slot." />

Check every slot. If every slot of x is at most the slot of y, and the vectors differ, x happened before y. If every slot of y is at most that of x, y happened before x. If each is bigger somewhere, they are concurrent. [2,0,0] and [0,0,1] are concurrent: the first leads in slot A, the second in slot C.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the test works in both directions">

If x is at most y in every slot, then y has heard of every event x had heard of, and it has done or heard of at least one more. A chain of messages must have carried that knowledge, so x happened before y. If x leads in one slot and y in another, each has heard of something the other has not. No chain connects them, so they are concurrent.

This is the gain over a Lamport clock. With Lamport numbers, "a happened before b" gives a smaller number, but a smaller number does not prove it. With vectors the comparison goes both ways: the vector of a is below the vector of b exactly when a happened before b [S52]. That is how a system can know that two writes were concurrent instead of guessing.

Missing slots count as zero. [1] and [1,0] are the same vector. That detail matters when a new node joins the cluster, and you will handle it in the code exercise.

</GoDeeper>

## Pick two events and compare

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m10-vector-compare" />

| Event | What happens | Vector |
|---|---|---|

| A2 | A sends to B | [2,0,0] |
| B1 | B, local event | [0,1,0] |
| B2 | B receives from A2 | [2,2,0] |
| C1 | C, local event | [0,0,1] |
| B3 | B sends to C | [2,3,0] |
| C2 | C receives from B3 | [2,3,2] |
| C3 | C sends to A | [2,3,3] |
| A3 | A receives from C3 | [3,3,3] |

Of the 36 pairs, 29 are ordered and 7 are concurrent: A1 and B1, A1 and C1, A2 and B1, A2 and C1, B1 and C1, B2 and C1, and B3 and C1. C1 is concurrent with many events because C did it before hearing from anyone. A3 is after every other event: its vector is bigger in every slot than all the others, because by then A has heard of everything.

Try one by hand. Is B2 [2,2,0] before C2 [2,3,2]? Every slot of B2 is at most the slot of C2, and they differ, so yes. B2 happened before C2, which makes sense: B3 came after B2 and C2 received B3.

## Neither vector is bigger: keep both

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m10-vector-concurrent" />

Hana adds milk through node A, and her sister adds tea through node C, at the same time. The vectors are [1,0,0] and [0,0,1]. Neither is bigger, so node B knows these are concurrent versions. It must not throw either one away. It keeps both and shows both to the next reader.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what to do with two versions">

Dynamo's answer was to keep both versions and return both on the next read, so the application can decide how to combine them [S45]. In the Dynamo paper's 24-hour measurement of the shopping cart, 99.94% of the time the cart had only one version. Concurrent versions exist, but they are rare.

There are three choices for two concurrent versions. First, keep both and merge them later, as in the next beat. Second, merge them by a rule built into the data type, which is the idea behind CRDTs in module 13. Third, keep only the one with the later timestamp. The third is the simplest and the most dangerous, because it quietly drops a write. The 2013 Cassandra test that lost about 28% of acknowledged writes used exactly this rule [S76].

A timestamp-based rule cannot tell concurrent from ordered. A vector clock can, so it can say "these two need a human or a merge rule" instead of silently choosing.

</GoDeeper>

## A merge replaces both versions

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m10-vector-merge" />

The phone reads both versions from B and merges them into one cart with milk and tea. It writes back with the slot-by-slot maximum of the two vectors, [1,0,1]. B adds 1 to its own slot and stores [1,1,1]. That vector is bigger than both old ones, so both can be dropped.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the merge, its risk, and what is missing">

The merged version carries a vector that is bigger than both inputs, so it replaces them: every slot is at least as big, and slot B is bigger. The next reader sees one version again. If a third writer had added coffee in the meantime through node A, its vector would be neither above nor below the merged one, and the cart would split again. That is how branches in Git work, too: two edits, then a merge.

Merging a cart by taking the union never loses an item that someone added. But an item that someone deleted can come back, because one side still has it [S45]. The merge rule is part of the design, and a cart that must never resurrect deleted items needs a different rule.

Vectors answer "which came first, or neither", but they give no help with "when". There is no time in [1,0,1], so you cannot ask for the cart as of noon yesterday, and the vector grows with every node that writes. Chapter 10.5 joins a physical clock with a counter to get both order and a time close to the wall clock.

Sources: DeCandia et al., "Dynamo: Amazon's Highly Available Key-value Store", SOSP 2007 [S45], for vector clocks, reconciliation on read, the 99.94% figure and resurfacing deletes. Kleppmann's notes [S52], for the comparison with Lamport clocks. Kingsbury (2013) [S76], for the lost writes. Hana, her sister and the cart are a course example.

</GoDeeper>
