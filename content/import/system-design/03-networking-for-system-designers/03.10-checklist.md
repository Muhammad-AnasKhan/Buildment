## Your gate answer: real-time for a delivery tracking screen

Real-time choices look simple in a diagram and become expensive at peak. In a design review, "we use WebSockets" is not an answer. A good answer says how the updates travel, why that way fits the screen, and what it costs in delay, requests per second and open connections, with the arithmetic shown. It also names one way the choice fails and what the design does about it.

The screen for your answer is a fictional food delivery app. After ordering, the customer sees a map with the courier's position, which the courier's phone reports to the server every 4 seconds. The customer never sends anything on this screen; they only watch. At the busiest hour, 30,000 orders are out for delivery at the same time, each watched by one customer. The product team says the courier's dot must never be more than about 5 seconds behind the courier's real position, and the screen must keep working when the customer's phone switches from Wi-Fi to mobile data.

Here is the difference between a weak line and a strong one, for a different screen: a weather app that refreshes the forecast for 50,000 users. Weak: "WebSockets, because they are real-time and scale well." Strong: "Polling every 10 minutes: 50,000 users make about 83 requests a second, the servers hold no open connections, and a new forecast appears within 10 minutes, which the product accepts." The strong line can be checked, argued with and improved. The weak one can only be believed.

You choose how the courier's position reaches the customer's phone. There is no single right answer. A mentor checks that your choice fits the screen and that your numbers follow from your assumptions, not that you picked the same method as someone else.

A good answer has four parts, each one or two lines. First, your choice: polling at some interval, long polling, server-sent events or WebSockets. Second, the delay: how far behind the dot can be with your choice, counting the courier's 4 second reporting interval. Third, the load at peak: requests per second and open connections for 30,000 watching customers, with the working shown. Fourth, one failure and its fix: for example, what happens when the phone changes network or a server is restarted, and how your design recovers.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is forgetting that the courier only reports every 4 seconds, so no delivery method can make the dot fresher than that; your delay budget is what is left of the 5 seconds. The second is counting requests but not connections, or connections but not requests: polling and pushing spend different things, and you must state both. The third is choosing a two-way connection for a screen that only listens, without saying what the second direction buys.

Before you submit, check your answer as a reviewer would. Recompute your requests per second from your interval. Make sure your worst-case delay, including the 4 seconds, stays within about 5 seconds. Then compare one number with your own measurements: challenge 2, part 3 showed how many requests and bytes each way used for five messages, and part 1 gave you a real round trip. If your design needs a reconnect after a network change, use your part 1 numbers to say what one new HTTPS connection costs from where you sit.

Put the link to your challenge 2 folder, or its part 3 `results.md`, in the link field. The mentor will read your design next to your measurements, because the two belong together: choosing a design, and knowing what it costs on a real network. If the mentor asks a follow-up question, answer it by editing and resubmitting.

You may write the design, or record yourself explaining it with a sketch, or both. The written answer is required; a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.
