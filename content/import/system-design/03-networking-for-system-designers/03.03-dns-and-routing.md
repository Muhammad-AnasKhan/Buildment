A shop moves to a new server at noon and updates its DNS record. At 12:40, some customers still reach the old, switched-off server. Nothing is broken: DNS is doing exactly what it promises. This chapter shows what that promise is.

## One question, a walk down the tree

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m3-dns-lookup" />

The phone asks its resolver one question: "where is shop.example.com?" The resolver's cache is empty, so it walks the tree. The root points it to the .com servers, they point it to the shop's own name server, and that one gives the address. The resolver keeps the answer, then replies. One question for the phone, three for the resolver.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: recursive and iterative, and what a cold lookup costs">

<GlossaryTerm term="DNS" definition="The Domain Name System: the internet's directory that turns names like shop.example.com into addresses machines can connect to." /> names form a tree. The root sits at the top, below it are the top-level domains such as .com, and below those each domain has its own <GlossaryTerm term="name server" definition="A server that holds the official records for a domain, for example the address of shop.example.com." />, run by the owner or a DNS provider.

RFC 1034 describes two ways a name server can answer. A **recursive** answer means the server does all the work for the client and returns the final answer. An **iterative** answer means it returns a referral, "I do not know, ask that server", and the client continues. In the picture, the resolver answers the phone recursively, while the root and the .com servers answer the resolver iteratively. The phone sends one question; the resolver does the walk.

A cold lookup, with nothing cached, costs one round trip from the resolver to each server on the walk, plus the phone's own round trip to the resolver. Most lookups are not cold. The referrals from the root and the .com servers are records too, so the resolver keeps them for their own TTL, and the next lookup for any .com name skips straight to the right name server. The common case is the next picture: the whole answer is already in the cache.

The resolver is usually run by your internet provider, by your company, or by a public DNS service. That means a company does not control how most of its users' lookups are cached. It only controls what its own name servers say, including how long the answer may be kept. That one number is the subject of the next three beats.

</GoDeeper>

## The answer is kept for its TTL

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m3-dns-cache" />

Sara asks the same resolver 60 seconds later. The answer is still inside its <GlossaryTerm term="TTL" definition="Time to live: how many seconds a stored copy of an answer may be used before the source must be asked again." /> of 300 seconds, so the resolver answers at once from its cache. It passes on the time left, 240 seconds. The shop's name server is not asked at all.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what the TTL promises, and what it costs">

RFC 1035 defines the TTL as the time a record "may be cached before the source of the information should again be consulted". A TTL of zero means the answer may be used only for the transaction in progress and must not be cached. The record's owner sets the TTL on their name server, and every cache on the way honours it.

When a resolver answers from its cache, it passes on the time left rather than the full TTL, so a copy further down expires at the same moment as the resolver's own copy. {/* source needed: resolvers return the remaining TTL from cache (RFC 1034 or RFC 1035 section) */} You will build exactly this behaviour in the code exercise in chapter 3.7.

The TTL is a trade-off with no free side.

- **A long TTL** (an hour, a day) means most lookups are answered from caches: fast for users and light on the name servers. But a change to the record takes up to that long to reach everyone.
- **A short TTL** (a minute) means changes spread quickly, but more lookups go all the way to the name server, and more users pay a cold lookup.

Load balancers are an example of a short TTL chosen on purpose. AWS Elastic Load Balancing gives its DNS records a TTL of 60 seconds, so that when the load balancer's own machines are replaced or added, clients move to the new addresses within about a minute.

A common plan follows from the next beat: before you move a service, lower the record's TTL well ahead of the move, by at least the old TTL, so the old long-lived copies have expired by the time you change the address. After the move, raise it again.

</GoDeeper>

## A change spreads only as caches expire

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m3-ttl-spread" />

Tap a TTL, then drag the rail to move time forward. Each dot is a resolver that cached the old address at some moment before the change, and it turns green only when its copy expires. With a 60 second TTL, everyone has moved within a minute. With a one-day TTL, some users reach the old server for up to a day.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the model behind the dots, and its limits">

The picture uses a simple model. Before the change, many resolvers asked for the record at random moments, so at the moment of the change each one holds a copy of a different age, anywhere from 0 to one TTL old. A copy that is a seconds old expires TTL minus a seconds later. So at time t after the change, the share of resolvers that have the new answer is t divided by the TTL, until it reaches all of them at exactly one TTL.

With a TTL of 3,600 seconds, about one in six resolvers has moved after 10 minutes, half after 30 minutes, and all after an hour. With a TTL of 60 seconds, all have moved after one minute.

This is why the TTL is the number to check before any planned move, and why a short TTL is part of a failover plan. If DNS is how you move users away from a broken region, the TTL is a lower bound on how fast that can happen.

The model is the best case. It assumes every cache honours the TTL exactly and that users do not keep a connection open to the old address. Apps that keep long-lived connections, such as the WebSockets in chapter 3.5, stay connected to the old server until that connection closes, whatever the TTL says. {/* source needed: some resolvers and clients keep records longer than the TTL */} Some caches may keep records longer than they should, so keep the old server running for a while after a move.

</GoDeeper>

## Anycast: one address, many places

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m3-anycast" />

With <GlossaryTerm term="anycast" definition="One network address announced from several places at once; internet routing delivers each packet to one of them." />, the same address is announced from Frankfurt and from Singapore. Internet routing carries each user to one site, usually a near one. When the Frankfurt site withdraws its route, routing moves European users to Singapore. No DNS answer changes, so no cache has to expire.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what anycast is good at, and how it fails">

RFC 4786 describes anycast as one service address announced from several locations, with routing choosing one. Because the choice is made by the network's routing, users reach a nearby site without any change to DNS, and a site that stops announcing its route stops receiving traffic.

It comes with a rule that matters for TCP and QUIC. All packets of one connection must keep reaching the same site, or the new site receives packets for a connection it never opened. The RFC therefore says routing must stay stable for longer than a transaction, and warns that splitting packets of one flow across sites can break TCP. Anycast fits short exchanges, such as a DNS question and answer, very well, and works for longer connections where routes are stable.

Meta's DNS servers used anycast, which is part of the story in the last beat. AWS Global Accelerator gives a service static addresses that are "anycast from the AWS edge network", so users enter AWS's network at a nearby edge location.

**Anycast or DNS?** Anycast moves users by changing routes, with no cache to wait for. DNS-based routing, the next beat, moves users by giving different answers, and each answer is cached for its TTL. DNS is easier to set up and lets you choose by measured latency or by health. Anycast needs someone who can announce routes from many places, so most teams get it through a provider's service, such as Global Accelerator.

</GoDeeper>

## DNS can answer by where you ask

<MermaidDiagram title="Location-aware DNS answers" chart={`flowchart TD
  E["Resolver in Europe asks for shop.example.com"] --> D{"DNS with latency routing"}
  A["Resolver in Asia asks for shop.example.com"] --> D
  D -->|"lowest latency from Europe"| F["Frankfurt servers: address 1"]
  D -->|"lowest latency from Asia"| S["Singapore servers: address 2"]
  D -.->|"failover: a health check fails"| B["Backup region: address 3"]
`} legend="Solid arrows: each resolver gets a different answer for the same name. Dashed arrow: the answer changes when a region fails its health check." />

Here the name server gives different answers to different askers. A resolver in Europe gets the Frankfurt address, one in Asia gets Singapore. If a region fails its health check, the answer switches to a backup. This is often called GeoDNS. Every answer is still cached for its TTL, so a switch is only as fast as the TTL allows.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: routing policies and In AWS">

DNS services offer routing policies that choose the answer per question. Amazon Route 53, for example, has latency routing, which answers with the region that has the lowest latency for the asker, and failover routing, which answers with a primary resource while it is healthy and a secondary one when it is not.

Two details shape a design. First, the name server sees the resolver that asks, not the phone behind it, so a user who uses a far-away public resolver can be sent to the wrong region. {/* source needed: DNS routing decides by the resolver's location; EDNS Client Subnet */} Second, the switch in a failover is a new DNS answer, so users keep the old answer until their cached copy expires. A failover plan that relies on DNS must state its TTL, and its recovery time can never be shorter than that.

**In AWS.** Route 53 provides DNS with latency and failover routing. AWS Global Accelerator gives a service static anycast addresses at the AWS edge. Elastic Load Balancing publishes its load balancers' addresses in DNS with a 60 second TTL. The separate AWS course builds on these.

</GoDeeper>

## When DNS breaks, everything breaks

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m3-dns-empty" />

On 19 October 2025, a race in AWS's own DNS automation left the record for DynamoDB's regional endpoint in us-east-1 with no addresses. DynamoDB itself was running, but nothing could find it by name. Services that depended on it failed too, and some took hours longer to recover than DNS did.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: two DNS outages and their lessons">

**AWS us-east-1, 19 to 20 October 2025.** AWS's post-event summary describes a race between two parts of DynamoDB's DNS automation: one applied an old plan, and the clean-up then deleted it, leaving the regional endpoint with no IP addresses. Every client that connected by name, including AWS's own services, could no longer reach DynamoDB in that region. Even after the record was fixed, EC2 instance launches and Network Load Balancer health checks kept failing for hours, because backlogs had built up and health checks removed capacity that was in fact fine. AWS's fixes included disabling the automation until it was made safe and adding limits on how fast health checks may remove capacity. The lesson for a designer: DNS is a dependency of everything that connects by name, and recovery can take longer than the fault that started it.

**Meta, 4 October 2021.** A command during maintenance took down the backbone between Meta's data centres. Meta's DNS servers are built to withdraw their anycast routes when they cannot reach the data centres, and they did, so the rest of the internet could no longer find Meta's DNS servers at all. Recovery was slow because the tools engineers would use to fix it depended on the same network. The lesson: a safety rule that is right for one server can take everything down when every server applies it at once, and you need a way in that does not depend on the system you are fixing.

Caching softens such failures for a while. A resolver that cached a good answer keeps using it until the TTL runs out, so a long TTL can hide a short DNS outage, and the same long TTL slows down a fix. The TTL trade-off appears again.

</GoDeeper>
