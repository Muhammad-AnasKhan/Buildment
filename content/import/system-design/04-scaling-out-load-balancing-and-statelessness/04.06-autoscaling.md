Exam results go online at 8 pm, and Daftar's traffic doubles in a minute. At 3 am it is a tenth of that. Paying for the 8 pm fleet all night is waste. An autoscaler changes the number of servers to follow the load. This chapter shows what it measures, and why it is always a little late.

## Scale on a signal, toward a target

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m4-scale-formula" />

Four servers report 90% CPU. The target is 60%. The autoscaler's rule asks how many servers would bring 90% down to 60%: 4 times 90 divided by 60 is 6. It adds two, and the fleet settles at 6 servers at about 60%. Measure, compute, adjust, then measure again.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the rule, the loop and the signal">

An <GlossaryTerm term="autoscaler" definition="A control loop that measures a signal from a group of servers, such as CPU or requests per server, and adds or removes servers to keep the signal near a target." /> is a loop. Kubernetes' Horizontal Pod Autoscaler is a well-documented example. Every 15 seconds by default it reads a metric and computes:

desired servers = ceil(current servers x current value / target value)

`ceil` rounds up to a whole server. With 4 servers at 90% CPU and a 60% target: ceil(4 x 90 / 60) = ceil(6) = 6. With 6 servers at 40%: ceil(6 x 40 / 60) = 4. The rule assumes the load spreads evenly, so twice the servers means half the CPU each. That is only true if the app is stateless and the balancer spreads well, which is what chapters 4.3 to 4.5 were for.

The signal matters as much as the rule. CPU is the default and works when every request costs CPU. It fails when the bottleneck is elsewhere: an app that mostly waits on the database can be slow at 20% CPU. Requests per second per server, the number of requests in flight (chapter 2.5's L) or the length of a work queue (module 15) are often better signals, because they measure the load itself.

The target is a choice about headroom. A 60% target keeps 40% of every server free for a sudden rise while new servers start. An 80% target is cheaper and leaves less room. The last beat of this chapter turns that into a number.

**In AWS.** An Auto Scaling group runs a set of identical EC2 instances and changes its size from a policy, such as keeping average CPU near a target.

## New servers are not ready at once

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m4-scale-lag" />

The spike lands at 0 seconds and the servers are overloaded. The autoscaler sees it at its next check, 15 seconds later, and starts three servers. They need 90 seconds to boot and warm up. Help arrives at 105 seconds. For almost two minutes, the old fleet carries the spike alone.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where the delay comes from">

The time from a spike to new capacity has three parts. The first is noticing: the autoscaler checks on a schedule, and metrics are often averaged over a short window, so a spike is seen up to one interval late. The Kubernetes default interval is 15 seconds.

The second is starting. A new virtual machine must boot its operating system and start the app. A container starts faster but may first need a machine to run on, and its image must be downloaded. The 90 seconds in the picture is a made-up round number; real times range widely with the image size, the machine and the app.

The third is warming up. A freshly started app often has cold caches, no open database connections and, for some runtimes, code not yet optimised. Its first requests are slow. Many systems wait for a warm-up period, or a readiness check (chapter 4.4), before giving a new server its full share.

There is one more trap in the picture. CPU cannot read above 100%. At the moment of the spike, the fleet needs 120% of its capacity, but it reports 100%, so the rule computes ceil(4 x 100 / 60) = 7, not the 8 that would bring it to 60%. A saturated signal under-counts the need, and the next check must add the rest. Signals that can exceed 100%, such as requests per second, avoid this.

## Set the delay, count the drops

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m4-spike-play" />

Daftar's traffic doubles at 8 pm. Drag the boot time, from instant to three minutes, and switch the target between 60% and 80%. Watch the extra traffic spill over while new servers start, and count the dropped requests. A shorter boot and a lower target both cut the drops. Notice which one costs money every hour.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the arithmetic of the gap">

The picture uses a simple model: Daftar runs at 300 requests a second, each server does 100 a second, and at 8 pm traffic jumps to 600. Requests above the fleet's capacity are dropped (a real fleet would queue them first, then time out). The autoscaler uses the rule from the first beat every 15 seconds.

With a 60% target, the fleet before the spike is ceil(300 / 60) = 5 servers, 500 a second of capacity. At 600, it drops 100 requests a second. At the 15-second check it reads 100% and computes ceil(5 x 100 / 60) = 9 servers. With a 90-second boot, they are ready at 105 seconds. Dropped: 100 a second for 105 seconds, about 10,500 requests.

With an 80% target, the fleet is 4 servers, 400 a second. The spike drops 200 a second. The first check asks for ceil(4 x 100 / 80) = 5, still too few, because CPU reads 100% when the real need is 150%. The shortfall only shows once the fifth server is running at 105 seconds; the next check then asks for 7, ready at 195 seconds. Dropped: 200 a second for 105 seconds plus 100 a second for 90 more, about 30,000 requests, three times as many.

With the 60% target but a 30-second boot, the new servers are ready at 45 seconds: about 4,500 dropped. Faster start-up helped as much as a lot of headroom, and it costs nothing per hour. That is why teams spend effort on small images and fast warm-up.

Lower targets buy safety with money every hour of every day. Faster starts buy it once, with engineering time. Most teams use some of both.

</GoDeeper>

## Scale in slowly, or flap

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m4-flapping" />

Load dips to 40% for half a minute. An autoscaler with no waiting window removes two servers at once. Then the load comes back, and it must add them again, with a 90-second boot. Removing capacity on a short dip leaves the fleet short when the load returns. Scale out fast; scale in slowly.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: stabilisation windows and cooldowns">

Changing the number of servers back and forth is called <GlossaryTerm term="flapping" definition="An autoscaler adding and removing servers again and again because the signal moves around its target, wasting start-up time and leaving the fleet short when load returns." />. It wastes start-up time, adds risk on every change, and, as the picture shows, can leave the fleet small just when load returns.

The fix is to make scaling in deliberately slower than scaling out. Kubernetes' autoscaler uses a stabilisation window for scale-down: it looks at the recommendations over a recent period and scales down only to the highest of them, so a short dip does not remove servers. Scaling out still happens at the next check.

A related idea is a <GlossaryTerm term="cooldown" definition="A pause after a scaling action before the autoscaler may act again, so the new servers have time to start and change the signal." />: after a scaling action, the autoscaler waits before acting again, so it does not react to a signal that the servers it just started have not had time to change. Without it, an autoscaler can see the same high CPU twice and start servers twice for one spike.

Scaling in has a second cost from chapter 4.3. Every server removed should be drained first: the balancer stops sending it new requests, it finishes the ones it has, then it stops. A server removed without draining drops the requests it was serving, and any state it held, such as sticky sessions, is lost.

## Headroom covers the gap

<MermaidDiagram title="What the target buys during the lag" chart={`flowchart LR
  T60["Target 60%"] -->|"100 / 60"| H60["Absorbs a rise to 1.67 x the load at once"]
  T80["Target 80%"] -->|"100 / 80"| H80["Absorbs a rise to 1.25 x the load at once"]
  H60 & H80 --> GAP["Anything bigger waits for new servers: check interval + boot + warm-up"]
  GAP --> PLAN["Known peaks: scale out ahead of time on a schedule"]
`} legend="The target decides how big a sudden rise the running servers can absorb on their own; anything bigger waits for the autoscaler's lag." />

A target of 60% means every server has room for its load to grow to 100 / 60, 1.67 times, before it is full. At 80%, only 1.25 times. Headroom carries the fleet through the autoscaler's lag. For peaks you can see coming, like 8 pm results, scale out on a schedule before they arrive.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: choosing a target and planning known peaks">

The headroom rule comes from the same arithmetic as N+1 in chapter 4.2. If servers run at a target utilisation u, the running fleet can take a sudden rise to 1 / u times the current load before it is full. At 60% that is 1.67 times; at 50%, 2 times; at 80%, 1.25 times. Remember the knee from chapter 2.5, though: "full" is 100%, and waits already grow badly from about 80%. So a 60% target absorbs a 1.33-times rise (to 80%) comfortably and a 1.67-times rise only just.

Pick the target from the rises you expect within one lag. If traffic can rise 50% within two minutes and your lag is two minutes, the target must leave room for 1.5 times the load, so u must be at most 1 / 1.5, about 67%, and lower if you want to stay under the knee.

Some peaks are known in advance: exam results at 8 pm, a sale at noon, a match on Saturday. For these, waiting for a signal is the wrong tool. Scale out on a schedule, ten or twenty minutes before, and let the autoscaler handle the rest. Some cloud autoscalers can also forecast daily patterns from history.

Autoscaling also has a floor and a ceiling worth setting by hand. The floor is N+1 for your quietest hour, so one failure at 3 am does not take Daftar down. The ceiling protects the database: twice as many app servers send twice as many queries, and the database may be the part that cannot scale out (modules 8 and 9). An autoscaler with no ceiling can turn a traffic spike into a database outage.
