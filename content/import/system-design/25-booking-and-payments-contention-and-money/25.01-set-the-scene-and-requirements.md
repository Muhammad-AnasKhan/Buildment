Tazkara is a concert ticket service (a fictional one). A big show goes on sale at 10:00, and fans have waited for weeks. This module runs the design loop from chapter 1.4 on Tazkara, from clarify to failure. This first chapter does the first two steps: say what Tazkara must do, then turn a ticket drop into numbers. Look at the pictures and ask yourself what you would try first.

## Ten o'clock: everyone presses buy

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m25-drop-opens" />

At 10:00 the sale opens. About 400,000 fans want one of 20,000 seats, and many want the same few. A seat can go to only one person, so most requests must be told no. They must be told quickly, because every other fan is waiting for an answer too.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: clarify first, the way chapter 1.4 does">

Before any numbers, ask what Tazkara must do and what it will not do. All of this is a course choice, because Tazkara is made up.

**Tazkara does.** It sells numbered seats for one stadium event of 20,000 seats. A fan picks a seat on a map and gets a hold for 10 minutes. A fan pays by card during the hold and gets a ticket. If the event is cancelled, every buyer gets a full refund. Tazkara keeps a 10% fee and owes the other 90% to the organizer.

**Tazkara does not, for now.** It has no resale, no changing prices, no seat swaps, no tax rules, no fraud scoring and only one currency. Tazkara does not run card processing either. A payment provider does that, and Tazkara talks to it over an API. Each missing feature is fair, and each would add work to every later step.

**Never.** Two rules are not negotiable. A seat is never sold twice. Money is never lost: every card charge has a ticket or a refund, and every ticket has a charge. Everything else can bend before these two do.

**Fast.** A hold request is answered in under 200 ms at the service, measured as p99 (chapter 2.4). A clear "taken" answer counts as an answer.

**Reliable.** During the drop, 99.9% of requests get an answer, a success or a clear refusal. A paid ticket is never lost.

**Honest about.** The seat map may be a few seconds out of date. When a fan clicks a seat that was just taken, Tazkara says so and does not hide it.

**Limits.** A team of five, one cloud account, one PostgreSQL database to start, and a payment provider.

The two "never" lines matter later. The first one decides how seats are held (chapter 25.2). The second one decides how money is recorded (chapters 25.3 and 25.4).

## Drag the crowd, find the pain

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m25-estimate-play" />

Drag the number of fans, then the number of seats. Requests climb with the crowd, but watch the fans per seat. At 20 fans per seat, 9 in 10 hold attempts must fail. Payments are only about 170 a second. There are few payments, but each one is money, and each must be exact.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the working, and what is an assumption">

Nobody gave Tazkara these numbers, so each one is written down as an assumption: 400,000 fans, 20,000 seats, half of the fans arriving in the first 10 seconds, 4 requests per fan in that time, one hold attempt per fan, and 2 minutes for a buyer to pay. The method is the one from chapter 2.3: start from people, divide by seconds, and keep the unit next to every number.

| Quantity | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Fans in the first 10 s | 400,000 x 50% | 200,000 |
| Fans a second at the start | 200,000 / 10 | 20,000 |
| Requests a second at peak | 20,000 x 4 | 80,000 |
| Fans per seat | 400,000 / 20,000 | 20 |
| Hold attempts in 10 s | 200,000 x 1 | 200,000 |
| Share that must fail | 1 - 20,000 / 200,000 | 90% |
| Payments a second | 20,000 seats / 120 s | about 167 |
| Provider calls a second | 167 x 2 (authorize, capture) | about 330 |
| Money moved in 2 minutes | 20,000 x 80 | 1,600,000 |

The ticket price of 80 is a course number.

Now ask which number drives the design. There are two, and they are different problems. The first is contention: 20 fans for each seat, 80,000 requests a second, and 9 in 10 of the holds refused. Many requests fight for few rows. One database can hold 20,000 seat rows easily, but it cannot let 200,000 requests all take a lock and wait. This is the problem of chapter 25.2.

The second is correctness of money. About 167 payments a second is small. A single ordinary service can handle that. But 1.6 million moves in two minutes, and a wrong total or a double charge is a real harm to real people. Speed is not the hard part here. Being exactly right is, and chapters 25.3 and 25.4 are about that.

These are averages and guesses. Say them out loud and invite correction. If 1,000,000 fans come for 20,000 seats, the share that must fail rises to 96% and the request rate to 200,000 a second. The design shape stays the same, but every protective step matters more.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say:** "Let me fix scope and numbers before I draw. One event, 20,000 numbered seats, a 10-minute hold, card payment through a provider, full refund on cancel. Two rules I will not break: never sell a seat twice, never lose money. I will assume 400,000 fans and half of them in the first 10 seconds. That is about 80,000 requests a second and 9 in 10 holds refused, but only about 170 payments a second. So there are two problems: contention on the seats, and exactness of the money."

**Expect:** "Why only 170 payments a second?" **Answer:** "Only seat holders pay, so at most 20,000 buyers, each taking about 2 minutes. 20,000 divided by 120 seconds is about 167. The number is small, but each payment must be exact."

</GoDeeper>
