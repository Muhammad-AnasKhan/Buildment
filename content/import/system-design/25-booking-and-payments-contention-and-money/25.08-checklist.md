## Your gate answer: Nuzul

This is the case study again on a different product. Nuzul (a made-up service) sells hotel rooms by the night in one city. A stay is a range of nights, for example three nights from Thursday. Here are the assumptions. There are 800 rooms across 60 hotels. A summer weekend sale opens, and 20,000 guests arrive in the first 5 minutes for 2,000 room-nights. A guest pays a 20% deposit now and the rest at the hotel. A guest can cancel for free until 48 hours before arrival and gets the deposit back. Nuzul keeps a 12% commission on the booked price. Card payments go through a provider. In an interview, this is the "design a hotel booking system" question, and it is the same loop you just ran.

A weak answer lists boxes: a search service, a database, a payment service, and stops. A strong answer starts from the numbers and lets them decide which boxes to draw. Compute the requests a second for the sale, the fans per room-night, and the share of attempts that must fail. Then ask what those numbers need. Tazkara had one seat as one row. Nuzul has a different shape: a stay of three nights needs three rows, one for each night, and all three must be free or none may be taken. Say how you hold a range, and in what order you touch the rows so two guests who want overlapping stays cannot block each other forever.

Then say what you do about a guest who is slow to pay, and whether a hold needs an expiry here. Next, say how money is recorded. A deposit is not the whole price, so the ledger entries differ from Tazkara's: say which accounts you use and what a free cancellation posts. Say what happens if a provider webhook is duplicated, late or missing.

Be explicit about one trade-off. Write it in the same shape each time: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would switch. A good cost line has a number or a name. For example: "an extra provider call for every booking, about 4 a second at the sale's peak," or "a guest may see a room disappear after choosing it." If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished.

A good order for 250 words is the order of the loop. Numbers come first, in two or three lines. Then the hold and the money, which are the sketch and the deep dive. Then the trade-off, and last the failure. If you run short of words, cut the sketch, not the numbers or the trade-off.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is copying Tazkara's design without checking the numbers, so a waiting room appears that nothing in the estimate asks for. The second is holding the nights one by one with separate commits, so a guest can end up holding two nights of three. The third is storing the deposit as a number on the booking row, with no ledger entry that explains it.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right design. Two students can choose differently and both pass, as long as each one starts from the numbers, ties the hold method to the shape of the inventory, records the money so that it can be proved, names the failure the choice answers, and says when they would change their mind. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens when a guest cancels at the same moment as the hotel closes the room, or how you would notice a missing webhook. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, about 250 words. A recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.

A checker polls payments pending for 5 minutes, and a nightly job reconciles with the provider. Trade-off: I authorize before capture. It buys that a guest who loses a room is never charged.
