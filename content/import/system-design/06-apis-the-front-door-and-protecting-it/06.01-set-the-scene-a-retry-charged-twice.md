## One tap, a timeout, two charges

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m6-double-charge" />

The app sends Pay. The provider charges the card, but its reply is slow. The app stops waiting after 10 seconds and asks Huda to try again. Her second tap is a brand new payment, so the provider charges her again. Nothing crashed. Every part did its job. The design simply had no way to tell a retry from a new payment.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the app could not know">

When a request times out, the client knows only one thing: no answer came in time. It does not know which of three things happened. The request may never have reached the server. It may have reached the server and failed. Or it may have reached the server, done its work, and the reply was slow or lost on the way back. Huda's first payment was the third kind: the money moved, and only the reply was late.

The app has two bad choices. If it never retries, a payment that really failed leaves Huda without her book, and she leaves. If it retries, a payment that really worked charges her twice. Retrying is still the right instinct. Networks drop packets, servers restart, and providers have slow minutes, so clients must retry. Marc Brooker's guide to timeouts and retries says it plainly: APIs with side effects are not safe to retry unless they provide <GlossaryTerm term="idempotency" definition="A property of an operation: doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once. Reading a book's page is idempotent. Charging a card is not, unless the API is built to make it so." /> [S89].

The fix is not a longer timeout. A 30-second timeout only moves the problem: some reply will always take 31 seconds. The fix is to make the retry safe. The app gives each payment a unique key before the first attempt and sends the same key with every retry. The server remembers keys it has seen, and answers a repeat with the saved result instead of charging again. Chapter 6.4 builds exactly that, and challenge 5 measures it: in your own run, a slow provider and a 250 ms timeout will charge some purchases four times without keys and exactly once with them.

The numbers in this story (10 seconds, 8 seconds, 12 seconds, $18) are made up for the example. Kitabi, Huda and the payment are a course story, not a real incident.

</GoDeeper>

## The front door, and what guards it

<MermaidDiagram title="Kitabi's front door" chart={`flowchart LR
  P["Huda's phone"] -->|"HTTPS: Pay $18, key k7"| G["Gateway: who are you? too many requests? (if so, 429)"]
  S["A script"] -->|"100 requests a second"| G
  G -->|"checked, routed"| A["Payments API: seen key k7 before?"]
  A -->|"charge once"| C["Card provider"]
`} legend="Every request enters through one door. The gateway checks who is calling and how often; the payments API makes a repeated payment harmless; the provider is charged once per key." />

Every request to Kitabi comes through one front door. A gateway checks who is calling and whether they are calling too often. Behind it, the payments API remembers keys, so a retry is answered from memory instead of charging again. This module builds each guard and shows what each one costs.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what this module covers">

An API is the contract between a client and a server: the addresses, the requests a client may send, and the replies it gets back. The double charge was a contract problem. The contract said "POST a payment and it happens", and said nothing about what happens when the same payment arrives twice.

Chapter 6.2 compares three styles for that contract: REST, GraphQL and gRPC, and when each one fits. Chapter 6.3 covers the details that decide whether clients can use an API safely for years: names for resources, pagination that does not skip or repeat rows, versions, and errors a client can act on. Chapter 6.4 makes writes safe to retry with idempotency keys, the fix for Huda's double charge.

The next three chapters protect the door. Chapter 6.5 compares five rate limiting algorithms under the same burst, because one client sending too much can slow the API for everyone. Chapter 6.6 decides what belongs in an API gateway and what does not. Chapter 6.7 covers authentication (who are you?) and authorization (may you do this?) when there are many servers and many services.

Then you practise. In 6.8 you write a token bucket and a sliding window counter against a fake clock. In challenge 5 (6.9) you build a rate limiter, share one limit across two servers with an atomic script in Valkey, and add idempotency keys to a payment endpoint, measuring double charges before and after.
