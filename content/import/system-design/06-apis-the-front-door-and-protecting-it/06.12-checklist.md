## Your gate answer: design a payments endpoint

Every earlier chapter in this module solved one piece of the payments problem. Your gate answer puts the pieces together for one endpoint. In a design review or an interview, this is the moment to show that you can see the whole contract, not only the happy path: what a client sends, what it gets back, what happens when it sends the same thing twice, and what happens when it sends too much.

The endpoint is `POST /payments` for a marketplace like Kitabi, a fictional app where students pay sellers for books. You may change the product if you prefer another one, such as paying for a bus ticket or topping up a phone. A mentor checks your reasoning and whether the parts fit together, not whether you chose the same numbers as anyone else.

A strong answer covers five things, a few lines each. First, the request and the success response: the fields, with types and units (amounts in whole cents, not decimals), and the status code. Second, idempotency: who makes the key and when, where the server stores it and for how long, how it is claimed so that two copies cannot both charge, what a retry gets back, and what happens when the key is reused with a different body. Third, rate limits: the key you count by, the algorithm, the numbers, and where the count lives when there are several servers. Fourth, errors: at least four failure cases, each with its status code and what the client should do next. Fifth, one failure you thought about and how the design handles it, such as a crash between charging the provider and saving the reply.

Keep the answer at the level of a design, not code. Name the store, the command or constraint that makes the claim atomic, and the numbers you chose, but do not paste your challenge code. A reviewer should be able to read your answer in two minutes and know exactly what a client must send, what it can rely on, and what the server must never do twice.

Watch for four common mistakes. The first is a key made on each retry instead of once per payment, which protects nothing. The second is "check for the key, then save it after the charge", which leaves the gap that charged purchases twice in your own challenge 5 run. The third is a rate limit kept in each server's memory, which multiplies with the number of servers. The fourth is answering 503 for "too many requests", which a client cannot tell apart from an outage; 429 with `Retry-After` is the right answer.

Before you submit, test your design the way a reviewer would. Walk through Huda's story from 6.1: the provider charges, the reply is late, the app times out and retries. Follow the retry through your design step by step and count the charges. Then imagine a script sending 1,000 payment requests a minute with one stolen account. Which part of your design stops it, and what does the script see? If either walk-through surprises you, fix the design before submitting, and say what you changed; that change is often the most interesting part of the answer.
