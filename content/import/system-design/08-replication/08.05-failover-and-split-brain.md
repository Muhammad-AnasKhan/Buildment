In chapter 8.2 you killed the leader and writes stopped. Here is how the system gets them back: it must notice, choose, promote and tell everyone. Each step can go wrong, and the worst failure happens when the old leader is not dead at all.

## Silence: dead or only slow?

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m8-detect" />

Followers listen for the leader's heartbeat, a small message sent every moment. When it stops, they start counting. After a timeout they decide the leader is dead. But silence has two causes: a crashed leader, or a slow network. From outside they look the same. A short timeout fails over too fast. A long one leaves writes stopped for longer.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how long should the timeout be?">

Detecting failure is a guess made from silence, and every system picks its own balance. Raft, a consensus algorithm you meet in module 12, uses randomised election timeouts, for example 150 to 300 ms. The Raft paper sets a rule: the time to broadcast a message must be much less than the election timeout, which must be much less than the time between failures. It notes that broadcast time may be 0.5 to 20 ms, so an election timeout between 10 ms and 500 ms is likely [S68]. Google Spanner's Paxos leaders hold leases of 10 seconds, and after the authors killed a whole zone, recovery took about 10 seconds [S60].

A timeout that is too short is dangerous in itself. A busy leader, a garbage-collection pause or a passing network problem looks like death, and the followers promote a new leader that nobody needed. Failover is not free: it loses writes, as the next picture shows, and it can create two leaders. More careful detectors reduce false alarms. The phi accrual detector turns the gap since the last heartbeat into a suspicion score and acts only above a threshold: the default is 8, and 12 is suggested on EC2 [S73]. SWIM asks other members to probe the silent node before declaring it dead, and marks it as suspected first [S72].

PostgreSQL does not detect failure at all. Its documentation says it provides no software to identify a failure on the primary, and points to separate tools, usually connected by a heartbeat [S38]. Chapter 12 shows why a group, not one follower, should decide.

</GoDeeper>

## Promotion can lose acknowledged writes

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m8-promote-loss" />

One follower is chosen and promoted. It can only have what it received. Here the old leader reached position 8, but the follower only 6. Writes 7 and 8 were acknowledged to clients and are now gone. The best candidate is the follower with the most log. It can still be behind.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the steps of a failover">

A failover has five steps, and each can fail. First, detect that the leader is gone. Second, choose the new leader: the follower with the highest applied position, because it has the most data. Third, promote it: in PostgreSQL you run `pg_ctl promote` or call `pg_promote()` [S38]. Fourth, point the clients and the other followers at the new leader, for example by moving an IP address, as the PostgreSQL documentation describes [S38]. Fifth, deal with the old leader.

The old leader cannot simply rejoin. After the failover there is one server in operation, a degenerate state in the documentation's words, and the former primary might stay down. To return to normal, a standby must be recreated on the former primary's machine or a new one, and the `pg_rewind` tool can speed this up on large clusters [S38]. The reason is the lost writes: the old leader holds entries the new leader never saw. Letting it follow the new leader would mean deleting those entries, so a human or a tool must decide what happens to them.

Synchronous replication changes the loss. If the promoted follower was the synchronous one (chapter 8.3), every acknowledged write is on it, and nothing acknowledged is lost. The PostgreSQL documentation states the limit: it cannot be certain that all standbys received all outstanding data at the time of the crash, so some transactions may be committed on the primary but missing on the standby, yet the application never got an acknowledgment for them [S38].

</GoDeeper>

## Cut the link: two leaders

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m8-split-brain" />

Tap Cut to cut the network between the leader and the others, but leave the leader running. The followers elect a new leader. Tap both leaders to write: both accept, and the two histories grow apart. Turn Fencing on and repeat: the old leader's writes are refused. Notice which writes remain.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why this is called split brain">

Two leaders accepting writes at once is called <GlossaryTerm term="split brain" definition="Two nodes both believe they are the leader and both accept writes, so the data splits into two histories that disagree." />. It is not a rare theory. It happens when a leader is cut off, or is merely paused for a long time and wakes up believing nothing changed. A lease, a lock that expires after a set time, does not fix it by itself: a client that holds a lease can pause past its expiry and still write afterwards [S70].

The damage is that the two histories cannot be merged by magic. Both leaders may have accepted a booking for the same seat or two different names for the same row. When the link heals, one history must win and the other must be thrown away or reconciled by hand. The writes on the losing side were acknowledged to users.

The PostgreSQL documentation names the required protection. If the old primary restarts after a failover, you must have a mechanism for telling it that it is no longer the primary, sometimes called STONITH, "shoot the other node in the head", which stops two systems from both believing they are the primary and leading to data loss [S38]. STONITH means cutting power or network to the old node before the new one starts. It works when you control the machines. When you cannot be sure the old node received the command, you need the next picture as well.

</GoDeeper>

## A number the database checks

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m8-fencing-token" />

Each new leader gets a bigger number, its epoch. Every write carries the epoch. The database remembers the highest epoch it has seen and refuses anything smaller. The old leader does not need to know it was replaced. It is stopped at the door. This number is called a fencing token.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: fencing tokens, terms and generation numbers">

Martin Kleppmann describes the pattern with a lock service: each time a client gets the lock, the service gives it a token that grows. The client sends the token with every write, and the storage server rejects a write whose token is lower than one it has already seen. In his example a client holding token 33 pauses, the lease expires, another client gets token 34 and writes, and then the first client wakes up and writes with 33, which the storage refuses [S70]. Google's Chubby calls the same idea a sequencer: a lock generation number that servers check [S71].

Raft builds the idea into the protocol. A term is a numbered period with at most one leader, and it acts as a logical clock: a node that sees a higher term steps down [S68]. Epoch, term and generation are different names for the same fence.

Two limits to remember. The check must happen at the place that stores the data, on every node. A leader that writes to a system that does not check numbers is not protected, however careful the election was. And the number must come from something that cannot give out the same value twice, which is why it usually comes from a consensus group (module 12), not from one machine's counter.

STONITH and fencing tokens work together. Cutting the old node's power is the quick action. The token is the guarantee that holds even when the quick action is late or fails.

</GoDeeper>

## A 43-second blip became a day

<MermaidDiagram title="GitHub, 21 October 2018" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["Network cut for 43 seconds between the East Coast hub and its main data centre"] --> B["The failover tool promotes databases in the West Coast data centre"]
  B --> C["The East Coast still holds a few seconds of writes that never reached the West"]
  C --> D["Both coasts have writes the other lacks"]
  D --> E["Service degraded for 24 hours 11 minutes while data was reconciled"]
`} legend="From the GitHub post-incident analysis. The partition lasted 43 seconds. The cleanup took a day." />

GitHub's failover tool saw the cut, formed a quorum without the East Coast, and promoted databases on the West Coast. The East had a few seconds of writes the West had never received. Going back would have lost them. Going on meant a day of repair. Failover is a data decision, not only a technical step.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what GitHub learned">

The cut came from maintenance on failing optical equipment at 22:52 UTC. Connectivity was back after 43 seconds. In that time the failover tool, Orchestrator, running with Raft consensus, had deselected its leader in the East Coast data centre. Tool nodes on the West Coast and in the East Coast public cloud formed a quorum and promoted primaries in the West Coast data centre. The tool did what it was configured to do. The application tier could not cope with its databases being on the other side of the country.

The East Coast clusters held a short period of writes that had not been replicated to the West. GitHub chose to fail forward: it accepted that the West was the new truth and rebuilt around it, because failing back would have made most database calls cross the country and left the site largely unusable. Restoring several terabytes from backups took hours. The service was degraded for 24 hours and 11 minutes in total, and some data had to be reconciled by analysing the binary logs [S134].

The follow-up work is a good checklist: stop the failover tool promoting across regional boundaries, test the assumptions with fault injection, and move toward serving from several data centres at once. For your own designs, ask three questions. Who decides to fail over, and what do they do when unsure? Where may the new leader live? What happens to the writes that exist only on the old side? Chapter 19 returns to these under recovery objectives.

</GoDeeper>
