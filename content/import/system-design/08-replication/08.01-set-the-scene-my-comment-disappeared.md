Layla uses a study forum (a fictional app). She writes a comment under a classmate's question and taps Post. The forum keeps its data in a database, and that database is not one machine. It is several copies of the same data.

Watch the next picture once, and ask yourself two things. Where did her comment go? And why did her second post work?

## It looked lost, so she posted twice

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m8-comment-twice" />

Layla posts, and the leader database saves her comment. She reloads, but a replica answers, and the leader has not copied the comment to it yet. The replica shows no comments. She thinks the post failed and taps Post again. Now the forum shows her comment twice. Nothing broke. One copy was simply behind.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the forum keeps copies at all">

A single database machine has three problems. It can fail, and then every page is empty. It can be too busy, because every page view is a read. And it can be far away: a student reading from a machine on another continent waits for every round trip. Keeping extra copies answers all three. A copy can take over if the main machine fails. Copies share the reads, so no single machine answers them all. And a copy can sit close to its readers.

The price is that copies must be kept in step, and a change cannot reach every copy at the same instant. This is called <GlossaryTerm term="replication" definition="Keeping a copy of the same data on more than one machine, and sending every change to each copy." />. The gap between the moment a change is saved on the main machine and the moment a copy has it is called replication lag. In Layla's story the lag is about three seconds so you can watch it. A real replica is often well under a second behind: the PostgreSQL documentation says typically under one second if the replica can keep up with the load [S38]. Under one second is still long enough to show a reloaded page the old data.

Layla, the forum and the timings are a course story. They are not from a real product.

</GoDeeper>

## One leader, several copies

<MermaidDiagram title="The forum's database" chart={`flowchart LR
  A["App server"] -->|"writes: every change goes here"| L["Leader"]
  L -->|"copies of every change, a little later"| R1["Replica 1"]
  L -->|"copies of every change, a little later"| R2["Replica 2"]
  A -->|"reads: any copy can answer"| R1
  A -->|"reads: any copy can answer"| R2
  A -->|"reads: or the leader"| L
`} legend="The app writes to the leader. The leader sends each change to the replicas. Reads can go to the replicas or to the leader. The copy arrows take time." />

All changes go to one machine, the leader. It sends each change to the replicas, so they can answer reads. Look at the two copy arrows: they are the slow part. This module follows that arrow. How should copies be sent? What happens to a reader when a copy is late? What happens to the writes when the leader dies?

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the road through this module">

Chapter 8.2 follows a write from the leader to its followers and shows what a replica can and cannot do. Chapter 8.3 asks whether the leader should wait for a copy before it says OK. Waiting is safer and slower. 8.4 returns to Layla's problem and names it: a user not seeing their own write, or seeing time go backwards. It also fixes both with a routing rule.

Chapter 8.5 asks what happens when the leader dies: how followers notice, how one is promoted, which writes are lost, and how two leaders can appear at once. Chapter 8.6 lets two regions both accept writes and shows what happens when they change the same record. Chapter 8.7 takes away the leader completely and writes to several nodes at once.

Then you practise. In 8.8 you write a read router in code, and in challenge 7 (8.9) you run a real PostgreSQL primary and replica in Docker, make the replica lag, reproduce Layla's bug, and fix it with log positions. A short quiz and a written plan for a social app close the module.

</GoDeeper>
