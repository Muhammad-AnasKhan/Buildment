The forum's leader has just saved a write. It can tell the client OK now, or wait until a follower also has a copy. This chapter puts numbers on that choice: how long the write takes, and what is lost when the leader fails.

## Asynchronous: OK before the copy

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m8-async-loss" />

Nour's order #8 is saved on the leader, and the client is told OK. The copy has not left yet. The leader crashes, and the follower, which has up to #7, is promoted. Nour asks for order #8 and it is gone. She was told it was saved. That is the price of an early OK.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: how much can an early OK lose?">

With <GlossaryTerm term="asynchronous replication" definition="The leader tells the client OK as soon as it has saved the write itself, and sends the copy to the followers afterwards." />, a crash can lose every write the leader acknowledged but had not yet sent. PostgreSQL's streaming replication works this way by default: the log records are shipped after the commit [S38]. The amount at risk is about the lag times the write rate. If the lag is 600 ms and the leader takes 500 writes a second, around 300 acknowledged writes could be missing from a follower (illustrative numbers, not a measurement).

The lost writes are not random. They are the newest ones, and often they belong to the busiest users. Users were told OK. Systems that must not break such a promise, like payments, use another setting.

A different problem hides here too. Even if the old leader's disk survived, its newest writes exist only there. If the old leader comes back later, the new leader has already moved on, and those writes conflict with the new history. Chapter 8.5 returns to this.

Why would anyone choose asynchronous replication, then? Because it is fast, and because the leader keeps working when a follower is slow or gone. A write costs only the leader's own work. For data where losing the last moment is acceptable, such as a view counter or a read receipt, it is the right default.

</GoDeeper>

## Synchronous: wait for the copy

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m8-sync-wait" />

Now the leader waits. It saves the write, sends the copy to the follower, and only when the follower confirms does it tell the client OK. The client waits about 60 ms, one round trip across the country. If the leader crashes now, the follower has the write. The price is time, on every single write.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the round trip, and what PostgreSQL promises">

The cost has a floor: one round trip between the leader and the follower. PostgreSQL's documentation says it directly: the minimum wait is the round-trip time between primary and standby [S38]. The napkin-math table gives the numbers: about 0.25 ms inside one region, 60 ms between North America East and West, 80 ms between EU West and North America East, and 160 ms between EU West and Singapore [S3]. A disk write with fsync is about 0.3 ms [S3]. In the same region, waiting costs about as much as the disk. Across continents, it costs a hundred times more.

With PostgreSQL's setting `synchronous_commit = on`, a commit waits until the commit record is written to the log on disk on both the primary and the standby. The documentation states that data can then be lost only if both machines fail at the same time [S38]. Two weaker settings exist: `remote_write` waits only until the standby has the data in its operating system, and `remote_apply` waits until the standby has also applied it, so a read there sees the write.

Waiting also holds locks longer. The documentation warns that locks are kept until the transfer is confirmed, so careless synchronous replication raises response times and contention [S38].

There is a second cost, on availability. If the one synchronous follower crashes, the leader waits for a confirmation that never comes. The documentation says such commits may never complete if a synchronous standby crashes [S38]. The leader is healthy, yet the app cannot write. The fix is on the next picture.

</GoDeeper>

## How many followers must confirm?

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m8-confirm-count" />

Drag the picture to choose how many followers must confirm before the leader says OK: none, one or two. Watch the write time and how many copies exist at the moment of OK. Then switch off the West follower and drag to two. Notice what happens to writes.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the model behind the picture">

The leader sits in one region with two followers: West, 60 ms round trip away, and EU, 80 ms away [S3]. The leader's own disk write is 0.3 ms [S3]. The model has three rules.

Write time is the leader's disk time plus the wait for the k fastest confirmations. With k = 0 it is 0.3 ms. With k = 1 it is 0.3 + 60 = 60.3 ms, the West follower being faster. With k = 2 it is 0.3 + 80 = 80.3 ms, because the leader must hear from both.

Copies at OK time: 1, 2 and 3. A write with 2 copies survives the loss of one machine. A write with 3 copies survives two. With k = 0 the only copy at OK time is on the leader, and the amount at risk is about the one-way delay times the write rate: 30 ms x 500 writes a second is 15 writes.

Availability: the leader needs k followers up. With West down and k = 1, the leader waits for EU, 80 ms, and carries on. With West down and k = 2, there are not enough followers, and writes stop. This is the sharp edge of strict synchronous replication: asking for more copies makes the writes depend on more machines.

The picture leaves out disk contention, queueing when many writes arrive at once, and network jitter. Use it for the shape, not for a price.

</GoDeeper>

## One waits, the rest do not

<MermaidDiagram title="One synchronous follower, two asynchronous" chart={`flowchart LR
  C["Client"] -->|"1. write"| L["Leader"]
  L -->|"2. copy, and WAIT for the answer"| S["Follower A: synchronous"]
  L -.->|"3. copy, no waiting"| B["Follower B: asynchronous"]
  L -.->|"3. copy, no waiting"| D["Follower C: asynchronous"]
  S -->|"confirmed"| L
  L -->|"4. OK"| C
`} legend="The leader waits for one follower only. If follower A stops answering, one of the others can become the synchronous one." />

Most teams do not wait for everyone. They pick one synchronous follower, and the rest stay asynchronous. Every acknowledged write then exists on two machines. If the synchronous follower fails, another takes its place, so writes keep flowing. You get a second copy before OK, without waiting for the slowest follower.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: priority and quorum settings in PostgreSQL">

PostgreSQL names the followers that may be synchronous in `synchronous_standby_names`. Writing `FIRST 1 (s1, s2, s3)` means: wait for one standby, chosen by priority, s1 first. If s1 disconnects, the next one in the list is promoted to synchronous at once [S38]. Writing `ANY 2 (s1, s2, s3)` means wait for any two of the three. This is a quorum rule: the fastest two answers are enough, so one slow follower does not slow the writes [S38].

The documentation recommends naming more candidates than you need, so that the number of synchronous standbys you asked for can always be met [S38]. If you cannot keep that many up, lower the number, or turn synchronous replication off for a while, and reload the settings.

Other databases offer the same idea under other names. Amazon DynamoDB global tables have two modes. In the default mode, called MREC, a change in one Region is copied asynchronously to the others, typically within a second or less, and the recovery point is "usually a few seconds" of data. In MRSC mode, a write is copied synchronously to at least one other Region before it returns, so the recovery point is zero, but write latency depends on the distance between Regions [S43].

The choice is not all or nothing. You can also choose per transaction. PostgreSQL lets one connection set `synchronous_commit` to `off` or `local` for a write that does not matter, and keep the strict setting for the one that does [S38].

</GoDeeper>

## Which writes deserve to wait?

<MermaidDiagram title="Choosing per kind of write" chart={`flowchart TD
  A["A write the client was told succeeded"] --> B{"Would losing it after OK hurt the user? A payment, a booking"}
  B -->|"no: a view count, a read receipt"| C["Asynchronous. Accept losing the last moments on a crash."]
  B -->|"yes"| D{"Can you afford the round trip? Same region: about 0.25 ms. Another continent: 60 to 160 ms."}
  D -->|"yes"| E["Synchronous to one follower, or any 2 of 3"]
  D -->|"no"| F["Synchronous to a near follower in another zone. Asynchronous to the far one."]
`} legend="The question is not sync or async for the whole database. It is what each kind of write deserves." />

The honest answer is per write. A like counter can lose its last second. A payment cannot. Put the near follower in the same region but another zone: it costs a fraction of a millisecond, and a failure of one machine or rack cannot take the write with it. Keep far copies asynchronous, and accept a small, known loss if a whole region disappears.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a number to take to a design review">

You can state the choice as two numbers: how long a write takes, and how many writes you may lose in a failure. For asynchronous replication, the second number is the lag times the write rate. For a quorum-synchronous setup, it is zero for a single failure, and the first number grows with the distance to the followers you wait for.

Say it like this in a review: "We wait for one follower in the same region, so every acknowledged write exists on two machines and costs about a millisecond more. The follower in the other region is asynchronous, so losing the whole region could lose a few seconds of writes. We accept that for comments and do not accept it for payments, which use the strict setting."

Two traps to avoid. Do not call a write durable because it reached the leader's disk: one disk is one failure. And do not assume synchronous means the follower can answer a read right away. Only the strictest PostgreSQL setting, `remote_apply`, waits until the follower has applied the change; with the others the follower has the data but may not show it yet [S38]. Chapter 8.4 starts from exactly that gap.

</GoDeeper>
