## Your gate answer: a replication plan for a social app

Replication is where a design meets the cost of copies, and where a short answer in an interview turns into a long, honest one. A weak plan says "we use a primary and two replicas". A strong plan says what each kind of write is promised, where each kind of read goes and why, and what happens on the worst day. It uses numbers, names its assumptions and says what it accepts to lose.

The app for your answer is a fictional social app for students called Rawi. People post short updates and photos, comment, like, edit their profile, send direct messages, and pay a small fee to pin an update. Users are in two regions, with most in one. The database is relational with one leader. You choose the size and the assumptions. A mentor checks your reasoning, not whether your numbers match anyone else's.

A good answer has four parts. First, the topology: how many followers, in which zones, and whether any is in the second region. Second, the write promises: for each kind of write (a like, a comment, a profile edit, a message, a paid pin), say whether the leader waits for a follower, which one, and what a crash can lose. Use the lag times the write rate to estimate the loss, and say what the wait costs in milliseconds. Third, the reads: say which reads may go to any replica, which must see the user's own writes and how you ensure it (the leader, a position token, or a short wait), and what you do when every replica is behind. Fourth, the worst day: how a dead leader is detected, which follower is promoted and why, what stops the old leader from writing, and which writes you accept losing.

Watch for four common mistakes. The first is to choose synchronous replication everywhere because it sounds safe: with a follower in the other region it adds a whole round trip to every like. The second is to route reads by a fixed time after a write, such as one minute, which fails the first time the lag is longer than that. The third is to forget the second device: a token kept in one browser does not protect a user who switches phones. The fourth is to describe failover as "a replica takes over" without saying who decides, what is lost and how the old leader is stopped.

Your plan does not need to be long, but each sentence must carry a decision. A sentence such as "we use replicas for scale" says nothing a reviewer can check. A sentence such as "profile pages read any replica; the page right after an edit reads a replica at the user's token" can be tested against your numbers.

Before you submit, test your plan the way a reviewer would. Take one write, such as a paid pin, and follow it from the user's tap to the copy. Take one read, such as the profile page right after an edit, and say which node answers it and what it shows. Then pull the plug on the leader and walk through the next minute. If any step is "it just works", add what makes it work.
