## Your gate answer: Dars

This is the case study again on a different product. Dars (a made-up service) is a lecture platform for a school network. Teachers upload recorded lectures. Students watch them on phones, and about four in ten are on weak mobile data of 1 Mbit/s or less. Here are the assumptions. There are 5 million views a day, and each view lasts about 20 minutes. There are 2,000 new lectures a day, each 45 minutes long and uploaded at 4 Mbit/s. Dars makes three versions at 0.3, 0.7 and 1.5 Mbit/s, and the average viewer receives 0.7 Mbit/s. Making all three versions costs 2 worker-minutes for each minute of video. Most views are of lectures from the last two weeks. In exam week, views are 10 times the average, and students watch old lectures too. In an interview, this is the "design a lecture platform" question, and it is the same loop you just ran.

A weak answer lists boxes from Lamha: a CDN, a queue, workers, sharded counters, and stops. A strong answer starts from the numbers and lets them decide which boxes to draw, and which Lamha boxes to leave out. Compute the views a second at the exam week peak, the viewers watching at once, and the video sent at that peak. Compute the storage for a year, and the transcoding machines kept busy. Then ask which number drives the design, as chapter 27.1 did. Is it bandwidth again? Does Dars need sharded counters, or is one row enough? Say so, and say when you would change your mind.

Then choose how uploads work for a teacher on a weak connection, and tie your chunk size to that. Say how the lecture becomes playable, and how a student on 1 Mbit/s keeps watching when the signal drops. Say where the skew breaks: what happens to the hit rate in exam week, when old lectures are asked for all at once? A design that is fine in a normal week can fail in the one week that matters most.

Be explicit about one trade-off. Write it in the same shape each time: the choice, why now, what it buys, what it costs, and when you would switch. A good cost line has a number or a name. For example: "about 24 TB pulled from storage once a term to warm the edges," or "a start-up that is 700 ms slower for the oldest lectures." If your cost line says "some complexity", it is not finished.

A good order for 250 words is the order of the loop. Numbers come first, in three or four lines. Then the upload and the delivery path, which are the sketch and the deep dive. Then the trade-off, and last the failure. If you run short of words, cut the sketch, not the numbers or the trade-off.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is copying Lamha's design without checking the numbers, so a fleet of thousands of transcode machines appears where the estimate asks for hundreds. The second is forgetting the player: a single version of each lecture that stalls for the four in ten students on weak data. The third is treating the hit rate as a constant. It depends on the skew, and exam week changes the skew.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right design. Two students can choose differently and both pass, as long as each one starts from the numbers, ties the delivery choice to the requirement, names the failure the choice answers, and says when they would change their mind. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens when the edge loses its cache, or how you would count views for a teacher's report. Answer them by editing and resubmitting. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required, about 250 words. A recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.
