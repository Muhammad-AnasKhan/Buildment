Lamha is a video platform (a fictional service). Teachers, shops and families upload videos, and other people press play on a phone, a laptop or a TV. This module runs the design loop from chapter 1.4 on Lamha, from clarify to failure. This first chapter does the first two steps: say what Lamha must do, then turn that into numbers.

## Play is many small downloads

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m27-press-play" />

A viewer taps a video. The phone asks Lamha for the watch page, which holds the title and a list of where the pieces of the video are. Then the phone starts to download short pieces, one after the other, and plays each as it arrives. Nothing downloads the whole file first.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: clarify first, the way chapter 1.4 does">

Before any numbers, ask what Lamha must do and what it will not do. Every line below is a course choice, because Lamha is made up.

**Lamha does.** A signed-in user uploads a video of up to 10 GB. Lamha turns it into versions that play on phones, laptops and TVs. Anyone can watch, and the video starts fast and keeps playing when the network gets worse. The watch page shows a title, a description, a thumbnail and a view count.

**Lamha does not, for now.** It has no live streaming, no copy protection (DRM), no comments, no ads, no recommendations and no search. Search is module 23. Each of these is a fair feature, and each would add work to every later step.

**Fast.** The video starts playing within 2 seconds for 95 of every 100 viewers (a p95 start-up time, chapter 2.4).

**Reliable.** Playback works 99.95% of the time, about 22 minutes of trouble a month. Once Lamha says "upload complete", the video is never lost. A new video can be watched, at least in low quality, within 10 minutes of the upload for a 5 minute video.

**Honest about.** The view count on the watch page may be up to a minute late and within 1% of the true number. A separate exact count, built later from a log, is used to pay creators.

**Limits.** A team of eight, a cloud account, and a budget. Video bandwidth is the largest bill.

The last line matters. When one cost dominates, the design is mostly about that cost.

## Drag the views, find the load

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m27-estimate-play" />

Drag the views a day, the minutes watched and the uploads a day. Views per second is only 11,600. Each view lasts minutes, so about 2 million people are watching at the same moment, and together they pull 4 Tbit/s. Sending video is the load. The ringed card drives the design.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the working, and what is an assumption">

Nobody gave Lamha these numbers, so each one is written down as an assumption: 1 billion views a day, 3 minutes watched per view, a busiest second that is 3 times the average, 500,000 uploads a day, a 5 minute video at 8 Mbit/s when it arrives, and an average of 2 Mbit/s sent to a viewer. The method is the one from chapter 2.3: start from people, divide by seconds, keep the unit next to every number.

| Quantity | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Views a second, average | 1,000,000,000 / 86,400 | about 11,600 |
| Viewers watching at once | 11,574 x 180 s (Little's law, chapter 2.5) | about 2.1 million |
| Video sent, average | 2.08 million x 2 Mbit/s | about 4.2 Tbit/s |
| Video sent, peak | 4.2 x 3 | about 12.5 Tbit/s |
| Video sent in a day | 1 billion x 45 MB | 45 PB |
| Uploads a second, average and peak | 500,000 / 86,400, then x 3 | about 5.8 and 17.4 |
| Upload bytes a day | 500,000 x 300 MB | 150 TB |
| Stored for one video | 300 MB source + 5 versions, about 439 MB | about 740 MB |
| Stored in a year | 500,000 x 365 x 740 MB | about 135 PB |
| Transcode work | 500,000 x 15 worker-minutes / 1,440 minutes | about 5,200 busy machines, 15,600 at peak |
| Metadata, 5 years | 912 million videos x 1 KB | about 0.9 TB |

The 45 MB is 2 Mbit/s for 180 seconds. The 439 MB comes from five versions of the video at 0.4, 0.8, 1.5, 3 and 6 Mbit/s, which together are 11.7 Mbit/s for 300 seconds. The 15 worker-minutes means that making all five versions takes 3 minutes of one machine for each minute of video. These are course numbers, not measurements.

Now ask which number drives the design. Suppose one sending machine can push 100 Gbit/s (a course number). Then 12.5 Tbit/s at peak needs 125 machines that do nothing but send video, and the cloud bill for that traffic would be the largest line. Storage at 135 PB a year is large, but it grows slowly and sits cold. Transcoding is a big, steady cost. The metadata, under 1 TB, is tiny. So Lamha is a bandwidth problem first, a storage problem second and a compute problem third. The view counter is small in bytes, but 34,700 writes a second at peak makes it a trap for a single row.

These are averages and guesses. Say them out loud and invite correction. If people watch 10 minutes, not 3, the bandwidth triples.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say:** "Let me fix scope and numbers first. I will support upload, processing into several qualities, adaptive playback, a view count and a watch page, and I will leave out live, DRM, comments and search. I will assume a billion views a day and 3 minutes per view. That is about 11,600 views a second and about 2 million people watching at once, so about 4 Tbit/s on average and 12 at peak. So this is a bandwidth problem. I will design delivery first, and treat processing and counting as separate paths."

**Expect:** "Why 3 minutes?" **Answer:** "It is an assumption. Watch time sets concurrent viewers and so the bandwidth. I would check it against real data, because it moves the answer more than any other number."
