Lina is on a train with weak mobile data. Her 300 MB lesson must reach Lamha, and then Lamha must turn it into versions that play on any screen. This is the first deep dive of the design loop. It follows one video from the phone to a playable state, and asks what happens when a connection drops, a worker crashes or too many videos arrive at once.

## A dropped connection resumes, not restarts

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m27-resume-upload" />

Lina's phone cuts the 300 MB file into chunks of 8 MB, 38 in all. Chunks 1 and 2 are saved. The connection drops during chunk 3. When the phone is back, it asks what the server has, and sends chunk 3 onward. The first two chunks are never sent again.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: chunks, checksums and the size of a chunk">

A chunked upload has three calls. Start: the API makes an upload session and returns a chunk size and an address for each chunk (chapter 27.2). Send: the app sends each chunk to its own address. Finish: the app lists the chunks it sent, and storage joins them into one file. Object stores offer this directly, often called a multipart upload. {/* source needed: S3 multipart upload (part size limits, maximum number of parts, how parts are joined) and the tus protocol's HEAD request for the current offset; the course uses the ideas, not the exact limits. */}

Resuming needs one more call: "which chunks do you have?" The server answers from its own record, never from the app's memory, because the phone may have restarted. Sending the same chunk twice is harmless, since a chunk has a number and a second copy replaces the first (this is the idempotence idea from chapter 6.4).

Each chunk carries a checksum, a short fingerprint of its bytes. The server compares it on arrival, so a chunk damaged on the way is refused and sent again. The finish call checks that the list is complete.

The chunk size is a trade-off. Large chunks mean few requests but a big loss on a drop: on weak data a 64 MB chunk may fail more often than it succeeds. Small chunks lose little but cost a request each. At 8 MB, a 300 MB file is 38 requests, and a drop wastes at most 8 MB. That 8 MB is a course number. A fast connection can send several chunks at once; a weak one sends one at a time.

An abandoned upload leaves chunks behind. A rule in storage deletes unfinished uploads after 7 days (a course choice), so they do not cost money forever.

</GoDeeper>

## An upload is a small state machine

<MermaidDiagram title="The life of one upload" chart={`stateDiagram-v2
  [*] --> Created
  Created --> Uploading: first chunk saved
  Uploading --> Uploaded: finish call, all chunks present
  Uploading --> Expired: no chunk for 7 days
  Uploaded --> Processing: job queued
  Processing --> Playable: first versions ready
  Processing --> Failed: not a video, or jobs keep failing
  Playable --> Ready: all versions ready
  Ready --> [*]
`} legend="An upload moves from created to ready. It can expire before it is finished, or fail during processing. Playable means viewers can already watch it." />

An upload is never just "done". It is created, uploading, uploaded, processing, playable and ready, and it can end as expired or failed. The status is stored in the database, so the app can ask for it at any time. Only a playable or ready video is shown to viewers.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the states are written down">

Each arrow is a write to the database, and each state answers a question someone will ask. The uploader's app asks "can I stop sending?" (uploaded). Support asks "why is this video missing?" (failed, with a reason such as "not a video file"). The cleanup rule asks "is anyone still sending?" (expired). A system that keeps these only in memory loses the answers the moment a server restarts.

The change from uploaded to processing needs care. The finish call must write the new status and put a job on the queue, and a crash between the two leaves a video stuck in "uploaded" with no job. Two fixes are common. The first is a sweeper that finds videos in "uploaded" for more than a few minutes and queues them again. The second is to write the job into the same database in the same transaction as the status change, and let a relay move it to the queue (the outbox pattern, chapter 14.4). Both rely on the job being safe to run twice, which the retry beat below shows.

Failed is not the end for the user. Lamha keeps the raw file and the reason, so a fixed worker can run the video again without a new upload.

</GoDeeper>

## Transcoding is a graph of small jobs

<MermaidDiagram title="The transcoding graph" chart={`flowchart TD
  P["Probe: is it a video?"] --> S["Split into 6 s chunks"]
  S --> A["Extract audio"]
  S --> T["Thumbnails"]
  S --> E1["Encode 240p: 50 chunk jobs"]
  S --> E2["Encode 720p: 50 chunk jobs"]
  S --> E3["Encode 1080p: 50 chunk jobs"]
  E1 --> K1["Package 240p"]
  E2 --> K2["Package 720p"]
  E3 --> K3["Package 1080p"]
  A --> M["Master playlist"]
  K1 --> M
  K2 --> M
  K3 --> M
`} legend="An arrow means waits for. Jobs with no arrow between them can run at the same time. The other two qualities, 360p and 480p, look like the three shown. Package joins the chunks of one quality and writes its playlist." />

One big encode of a 5 minute video takes about 15 minutes on one machine. So Lamha cuts the video into 6 second chunks and encodes every chunk in every quality at the same time. An arrow means "waits for". Jobs with no arrow between them run together, as many as there are free workers.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: counting the jobs">

A 5 minute video is 50 chunks of 6 seconds. With five qualities, that is 250 encode jobs. The estimate said the whole ladder costs 15 worker-minutes, which is 900 worker-seconds, so each encode job is about 3.6 seconds of one machine (a course number). Short jobs have a use. A worker that dies loses seconds, not minutes, and a retry is cheap.

The graph is a DAG, a directed acyclic graph: arrows have a direction, and no path leads back to its start. Two ideas make it work. Fan-out: the split step creates many independent jobs. Fan-in: the package step for 720p must wait until all 50 chunks of 720p are done. A small orchestrator keeps the graph in the database, records which jobs are done, and puts a job on the queue when everything it waits for is finished. Each job is a message on the queue from chapter 15.2.

The slowest path decides how long one video takes when machines are free: probe, split, the slowest chunk encode, package, master. With course numbers for each step, that is under a minute. When machines are busy, the wait comes from the line, not from the graph, which the next beats show.

Cutting a video into independent chunks only works if each cut falls on a keyframe, a frame that does not depend on earlier frames. A cut elsewhere would give a chunk that cannot be decoded alone. {/* source needed: keyframes (GOP boundaries) and why chunked encoding cuts there; the course states the idea only. */}

Existing tools do this work, so Lamha would not write an encoder. FFmpeg is a widely used open-source one. A managed service such as AWS Elemental MediaConvert is the alternative: no fleet to run, and a price per minute of video. Most teams start managed and move to their own fleet when the bill is large enough to justify a team. {/* source needed: FFmpeg licence and MediaConvert pricing model; check current docs. */}

</GoDeeper>

## A failed job reruns under the same name

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m27-job-retry" />

Worker A crashes in the middle of one encode job. The queue waits a while, then puts the job back in line. Worker B takes it and writes its result to the same file name. If A had finished after all, both would write the same file with the same bytes. A retry leaves one file, not two.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why a retry is safe">

Queues deliver at least once [S83]. When a worker takes a job, the queue hides it for a while. SQS calls this the visibility timeout, 30 seconds by default and up to 12 hours [S84]. If the worker does not finish and delete the job in time, the job reappears for another worker. So the same job can run twice, and Lamha must make that harmless.

The rule is to name the output from the job, not from the worker. The 720p encode of chunk 7 of video `v81` always writes to `v81/720p/chunk0007.ts`. A second run writes the same bytes to the same key. Writing to a temporary key first and then renaming (or using a "put if absent" write, chapter 7.5) avoids a half-written file that a reader might see.

Two more rules. A long job extends its timeout while it works, so it does not reappear while it is still running. And a job that fails again and again, perhaps on a damaged file, is moved to a dead letter queue after a fixed number of tries [S85], so it cannot block the line (chapter 15.5). The video is marked failed with the reason, and a person can look at it.

</GoDeeper>

## Drag the uploads, watch the backlog

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m27-backlog-play" />

Drag the upload rate for the busy hour and the number of machines. In the busy hour the line grows by uploads minus capacity. Afterwards it shrinks only by the spare capacity. Read the wait at the end of the busy hour. Spare machines cost money all day, so look for the fewest that keep the wait short.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: sizing the workers">

The model is the one from chapter 15.2, with Lamha's numbers. A video is one job of 15 worker-minutes, so a fleet of W machines finishes W / 900 videos a second. The estimate said 5,200 machines are busy on average and about 15,600 at peak (chapter 27.1). A fleet sized for the average falls behind at every peak, and a fleet sized for the peak sits mostly idle.

The line is allowed to grow if the wait stays inside the target. Lamha promised a new video is watchable within 10 minutes. The wait at the end of a busy hour is the backlog divided by the capacity, so you can read off the cost of each choice. A fleet of 12,000 machines, with the peak lasting an hour, leaves about 14,600 videos in the line and a wait of about 18 minutes. That misses the target, and the 15,600-machine fleet meets it.

Real fleets scale. They add machines when the line grows and remove them when it empties, as autoscaling does (chapter 4.6). That follows the backlog with a delay of minutes, so a short burst is still absorbed by the line. Lamha can also use cheaper machines that the cloud may take away at short notice, because a job that loses its machine simply runs again, as the last beat showed.

Where the line must not grow without limit, set a maximum. When it is reached, Lamha slows new uploads with a clear message ("try again in a few minutes") instead of hiding a wait of hours. That is backpressure (chapter 15.2).

</GoDeeper>

## Publish after the first versions, not the last

<MermaidDiagram title="Playable first, complete later" chart={`flowchart LR
  U["Upload complete"] --> L["Encode 240p and 360p"]
  L --> PL(["Status: playable"])
  U --> M["Encode 480p and 720p"]
  U --> H["Encode 1080p"]
  M --> R(["Status: ready"])
  H --> R
`} legend="The low qualities are cheap and finish first, so the video becomes playable early. The expensive qualities finish later and the status becomes ready." />

Viewers do not need 1080p to start watching. The low qualities are cheap and finish first, so Lamha marks the video playable after them. HD arrives later, and the player simply lists the qualities that exist. The uploader sees "watchable" in minutes, even when the line is long.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: an uncommon option, encode the top quality only when it is wanted">

Most uploaded videos are watched a handful of times. Encoding every one in 1080p costs the most worker time for the least benefit. A different order is to encode the cheap qualities for every video, and to encode the expensive ones only when a video has earned them, for example after 1,000 views. Until then, the player offers the best quality that exists.

What it saves is compute: if most of the 500,000 daily uploads never pass the threshold, the fleet can be much smaller than the 15,600 machines at peak. What it costs is a second, on-demand path: a job queued by the view counter, and a short period where a popular video is watched in lower quality than it could be. It also makes the view counter part of the processing pipeline. Many teams accept that cost only at large scale. {/* source needed: no public source is used for this option; it is a course design option, not a claim about any company. */}

Another, simpler cut is a shorter ladder. Dropping the 240p version saves 3.4% of the stored bytes (0.4 of 11.7 Mbit/s) but loses viewers on the weakest data, which are the viewers Lamha cares about most. Check the numbers before cutting a rung.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say:** "I upload in 8 MB chunks, direct to object storage, each with a checksum. If the connection drops, the app asks the server which chunks it has and sends the rest. The upload has a status in the database. On finish, a job goes on a queue, and the transcode is a graph: probe, split into 6 second chunks, encode each chunk in each quality in parallel, package each quality, then write a master playlist. Jobs write to deterministic keys, so a retry is harmless. I size workers from arrival rate times job time, about 5,000 busy on average and 15,000 at peak, and I mark the video playable after the cheap qualities so uploaders do not wait for HD."

**Expect:** "What if a worker dies halfway?" **Answer:** "The queue's timeout puts the job back in line. The next worker writes the same output key, so nothing is doubled. After a few failures the job goes to a dead letter queue and the video is marked failed with a reason."

</GoDeeper>
