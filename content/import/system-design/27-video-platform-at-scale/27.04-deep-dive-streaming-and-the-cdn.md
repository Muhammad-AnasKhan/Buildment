Lina's lesson is ready. Now Omar presses play on a bus, with a phone that gets one bar of signal in the tunnels. This is the second deep dive of the design loop. It follows the video out to the viewer, and asks what happens when the network gets worse, when a video is very popular, and when it is almost never watched.

## A video is a list of short pieces

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m27-segments" />

Omar's player asks for a playlist, a small text file that lists the qualities of the video and where each piece lives. Then it downloads piece 1, starts to play it, and asks for piece 2 while piece 1 plays. The video never downloads as one file. Every piece is an ordinary file that any web cache can hold.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: HLS and DASH, and why plain files">

Two formats describe this idea. HLS was made by Apple and is described in RFC 8216. DASH is an international standard (MPEG-DASH). Both use a master playlist that lists the qualities, a playlist for each quality that lists the pieces in order, and the pieces themselves, called segments. {/* source needed: RFC 8216 for HLS and ISO/IEC 23009-1 for DASH; check which container formats each requires today. */}

Because the segments are plain files, Lamha needs no special streaming server. Storage holds them, and the CDN caches them like any image. The pieces of a finished video never change, so each one can carry a very long `Cache-Control: max-age` and a name that includes the video and quality, for example `v81/720p/seg0007.ts` [S22]. A changed video gets new names, which is the "change the name" answer from chapter 5.7 [S28]. The playlist is different: if a video is taken down, the playlist must change quickly, so Lamha gives it a short lifetime, 60 seconds (a course choice).

The piece length is a trade-off. Short pieces start sooner and let the player change quality faster, but they mean more requests, more files and slightly worse compression. Long pieces are the opposite. At 6 seconds, a 5 minute video in five qualities is 250 files plus playlists (a course choice). {/* source needed: typical segment durations recommended for HLS and DASH; the course uses 6 seconds. */}

</GoDeeper>

## The player chooses the quality

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m27-abr-switch" />

Omar's bus goes through a tunnel and the network slows. Lamha's servers do not notice. The player does: piece 3 arrived slowly, so it asks for the 360p version of piece 4. The picture gets softer and the video does not stop. This is adaptive bitrate streaming, and the player is the one that adapts.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the client decides">

The server cannot see the last mile: Omar's signal, his battery, whether his screen is small. The player can measure it by timing each piece. If the server chose the quality, every viewer would need a private conversation with it, and the pieces would no longer be the same files for everyone. With the client choosing, each piece is a public file that the CDN caches once for all viewers of that quality.

The player keeps a buffer, the seconds of video already downloaded. A stall (also called a rebuffer) happens when the buffer reaches zero. Quality can change only at the start of a piece, because every piece begins at a keyframe and can be decoded alone (chapter 27.3). A short piece means a quick change.

Start-up needs care. The target is a first frame within 2 seconds at the 95th percentile. So the player often begins at a low or middle quality, whose first piece is small, and climbs when it has measured the connection. This is why a video may look soft in its first seconds.

</GoDeeper>

## The player's rule fits in a small tree

<MermaidDiagram title="A simple player rule" chart={`flowchart TD
  D["A piece finished downloading"] --> B{"Buffer under 5 s?"}
  B -->|"yes"| L["Go one quality down"]
  B -->|"no"| U{"Buffer over 15 s and speed 1.25 times the next quality?"}
  U -->|"yes"| H["Go one quality up"]
  U -->|"no"| S["Stay"]
`} legend="An example rule, not a standard. A low buffer means danger, so drop quality at once. Climb only when the buffer is healthy and the measured speed clearly allows it." />

The rule is small. If the buffer is low, go down. If the buffer is healthy and the measured speed is clearly higher than the next quality needs, go up. Otherwise stay. Real players are smarter, but the idea is this: fear a stall more than a soft picture.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the choices a player makes">

The numbers in the tree (5 seconds, 15 seconds, 1.25 times) are illustrative. Real players use different thresholds, and some use a model of the buffer or of the network instead of a rule. {/* source needed: published adaptive bitrate algorithms (buffer-based and throughput-based) and the open-source players that implement them; the course uses an invented rule. */}

Two failure modes show the trade-off. A player that switches too eagerly flaps between qualities, and the viewer sees the picture pulse. A player that is too slow to go down stalls, and the viewer sees a spinner. Stalls hurt viewers more than a soft picture, so rules are built to drop fast and climb slowly.

The ladder of qualities is part of the product. Five rungs from 0.4 to 6 Mbit/s mean each step is about double the one below, so a drop of one rung roughly halves the data needed. If the rungs were too close, the player would flap. If too far apart, a drop would be a big loss in quality.

</GoDeeper>

## A popular video costs one trip to storage

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m27-popular-edge" />

The first viewer in a region asks the edge for piece 1. The edge does not have it, fetches it from storage once, keeps a copy and passes it on. The second viewer gets the copy from the edge. A video watched a million times in that region costs about one trip to storage per piece, not a million.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the CDN tiers and what a miss costs">

This is the cache-aside pattern of chapter 20.4, with an edge in place of a cache node. A hit is served from a machine close to the viewer. A miss goes to a regional layer or the origin shield, and then to storage (chapter 5.7). The shield exists so that many edges asking for the same new piece cause one read from storage, not hundreds.

The copy at the edge lasts as long as `Cache-Control` says and as long as the edge keeps it. An edge has limited disk, so it drops the pieces that were used least recently. That brings the next question: how much does a cache of a given size catch? It depends on how views are spread over videos.

A brand new video that goes viral is the hard case. Thousands of viewers ask for piece 1 in the same second, before any edge has it. Without care, each of those requests becomes a miss. The shield and "collapsed forwarding", where the edge sends one request to storage and holds the others until it returns, turn a stampede into one read (chapter 5.6).

</GoDeeper>

## Drag the skew, watch the origin

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m27-skew-play" />

Drag the skew and the size of the edge cache. A steep skew means a few videos get nearly all the views, and a small cache is enough. A mild skew means views spread over many videos, and even a big cache misses often. The origin number is what Lamha pays for. The ring shows whether it stays under 1 Tbit/s.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the model, and what it leaves out">

The model is a Zipf distribution: the video ranked r gets views in proportion to 1 / r^s. The skew s is the one number that matters. The cache is assumed to hold the C most-watched videos, so the hit rate is the share of all views that belong to the top C. With N = 1 billion videos and a peak of 12.5 Tbit/s:

| Skew s | Cache of 100 thousand | 1 million | 10 million |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.9 | 31.7% hits, origin 8.5 Tbit/s | 43.4%, 7.1 | 58.1%, 5.2 |
| 1.0 | 56.8%, 5.4 | 67.6%, 4.1 | 78.4%, 2.7 |
| 1.1 | 79.6%, 2.6 | 86.6%, 1.7 | 92.1%, 1.0 |
| 1.2 | 92.4%, 0.95 | 95.7%, 0.54 | 97.8%, 0.27 |

Read down one column: a small change in the skew moves the origin load by a factor of ten. The 95% hit rate of chapter 27.2 needs a skew near 1.2, or a cache of 100 million videos at a skew of 1.1. That is why the number to measure first is the skew of Lamha's own views, from its own logs.

What the model leaves out: it treats a video as one unit when the cache holds pieces, so a half-watched video is only half cached. It assumes the cache holds exactly the most popular videos, which a real cache with an LRU rule only approximates. It uses one cache, when a real CDN has many edges, each seeing a slice of the audience and each with its own skew. And the exponent is invented: no public figure is used. {/* source needed: a published estimate of how video views are distributed over a catalogue; the course uses a Zipf shape and invented exponents. */}

</GoDeeper>

## A rarely watched video pays the full trip

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m27-long-tail" />

Nobody has watched this old lesson for months. Omar is in Singapore and the video is stored in EU West. The edge has no piece, so it asks storage across the world and waits. Every piece of the video is a miss. The long tail is slow, and it is also the part the CDN cannot save.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what a miss adds to start-up">

Two numbers from napkin-math [S3] show the size of the delay. A round trip from EU West to Singapore is about 160 ms, and reading a small object from blob storage takes about 80 ms. A miss at the edge adds about 160 + 80 = 240 ms to one piece, and more if the shield is also far away.

To start, the player needs the master playlist, the quality playlist and the first piece, one after another. If all three miss, start-up gains about 3 x 240 = 720 ms. Against a target of 2 seconds at the 95th percentile, that is more than a third of the budget. After the start, the player is ahead of the video by a few pieces, so later misses are hidden by the buffer. The cost of the long tail is paid mainly at the start.

Most of Lamha's videos are in the long tail, and each is watched rarely. But together they make a large share of the plays. At a skew of 1.0 down to 0.9, the videos outside a cache of 1 million take 32 to 57 of every 100 plays (see the table in the last beat). So the start-up target is decided as much by the misses as by the speed of the edge.

</GoDeeper>

## Treat hot, warm and cold videos differently

<MermaidDiagram title="Three kinds of video" chart={`flowchart TD
  V["A video, by plays a day"] --> H{"Over 1,000?"}
  H -->|"yes"| HOT["Hot: all qualities cached at the edges"]
  H -->|"no"| W{"Over 10?"}
  W -->|"yes"| WARM["Warm: shield and storage, cached when asked"]
  W -->|"no"| COLD["Cold: low storage tier, only the cheap qualities kept ready"]
`} legend="The thresholds are examples. A video moves between kinds as its plays change, so the system measures plays and moves the video." />

Hot videos are few and get most plays, so keep them everywhere. Warm videos are cached when someone asks, and the shield protects storage. Cold videos are many and almost never watched, so keep them on cheaper storage and accept a slower start. The kinds are not a label, they are a measurement that changes, so a cold video that goes viral warms up.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the levers and what each costs">

Four levers keep the tail cheap, and each has a price.

- **Origin shield.** One layer between all edges and storage collapses duplicate misses. It costs an extra hop on a miss.
- **Bigger or tiered edge caches.** More disk raises the hit rate, but the table showed that at a mild skew even ten times the cache buys little.
- **Cheaper storage for cold videos.** Storage classes for rarely read data cost less to keep and more to read, and may read slower. Check the current classes and prices of your provider. {/* source needed: current object storage classes, their per-GB storage and retrieval prices, and first-byte latency; the course gives no prices. */}
- **Fewer ready qualities for cold videos.** The idea from chapter 27.3: keep only the cheap qualities and encode the rest if the video heats up. It saves compute and storage and adds an on-demand path.

An uncommon alternative is to do nothing: accept the long tail's slower start and size the budget for it. For a platform whose value is the catalogue, many teams do exactly this for cold videos, because making every rarely watched video fast costs more than the lost viewers are worth. The honest question is how many viewers leave when start-up goes from 1 second to 2, and that comes from Lamha's own data.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="In an interview">

**Say:** "The video is stored as short segments in several qualities, with a playlist. The player downloads segments over plain HTTP and picks the quality for the next segment from its measured speed and its buffer, so the server stays simple and the segments are cacheable. A CDN serves nearly all plays. How well it works depends on the skew of views: if the top million videos hold about two thirds of the views, a modest cache catches two thirds and the origin sends the rest. I would measure the skew, then size the cache and the origin budget, and use a shield so a viral new video causes one read from storage."

**Expect:** "What about videos nobody watches?" **Answer:** "They always miss, so they pay the full trip to storage and start slower. I would keep them on cheaper storage, keep only the cheap qualities ready, and accept the slower start, because the cost of making them fast is high."

</GoDeeper>
