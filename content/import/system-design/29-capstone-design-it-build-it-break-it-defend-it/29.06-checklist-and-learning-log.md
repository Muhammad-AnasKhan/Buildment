## Learning log

1. **Your driving number:** What was the number that made your system hard? Did it match the one in your design document before you built anything? If it moved, say what moved it.
2. **Your surprise:** Which result in this capstone surprised you most: a table, a chart or a test that failed? What did you believe before, and what do you believe now?
3. **Your weakest claim:** Which sentence in your defence would you least like a mentor to question? What would you measure to make it stronger, and how long would that take?
4. **Your trade-off:** Name one thing your design gives up to meet its cost target. Who would notice, and when?
5. **Your next module:** If a company asked you to build a real version of your system next month, what is the first thing you would add that the harness does not model, such as real disks, real networks, login or deployment? Why that one first?

## Your gate answer: compare with Daftar

In chapter 1.1 you met Daftar, an app where students share class notes (a fictional app). It ran on one rented server, and you were asked what you would change first. In chapter 1.7 your learning log asked you to sketch how Daftar could serve 10,000 students who open the app in the same evening, and said you would answer the same question again in the capstone. This is that moment.

First, find your old answers. Open your learning log from chapter 1.7, question 1, and your answer in 1.1. Read them again before you do anything else, and do not change them. The point is to see your old self clearly, and the old answer is evidence of where you started.

Then, without looking at the old sketch again, draw Daftar a second time for the same evening, on paper or in any drawing tool. This time, start the way you started your capstone, with numbers. Chapter 1.1 gave you what you need: each student with the app open sends one request every 20 seconds, which is 0.05 requests a second, and one server needs 10 ms for a request, so it can finish at most 100 requests a second. With 10,000 students that is 10,000 times 0.05, which is 500 requests a second, five times what one server can do at full speed. You also learned in module 2 that a server near full speed makes everyone wait, so you would plan for each server to be well below full. Show that sum, choose how many servers and what else you need, and give the cost, the p99 you would aim for and the availability you would promise.

Then draw the failure. Choose one thing that can go wrong on that evening: a server that dies, a database that slows down, a link that breaks. Write what a student sees, what your design does, and how many seconds until it is normal again. If you want to test your answer, you can build a small version in your harness as a fourth scenario, but this is optional.

Now compare the two sketches. A good comparison does not say "the second one is better", because a mentor cannot check that. It names six specific differences, one for each of these questions, and says what the first sketch did about each:

| Question | What to compare |
|---|---|
| Numbers | Did the first sketch have a rate, a cost per request or a budget? Did it say how much traffic one server can take? |
| Single points of failure | Which box, if it died, would stop everything? Did the first sketch know? |
| Where state lives | Which data must survive a restart, and did the first sketch say where it lives? |
| Failure | What does a student see when something breaks, and how long does it last? |
| Evidence | How would you know the design works? Did the first sketch say how to measure it? |
| Cost | What does it cost per hour, and what would you give up to halve that? |

Be honest about two things. First, name something the first sketch got right. Many first sketches already have a second server or a cache in the right place, and a good comparison says so. Second, name something your capstone taught you that is still missing from the new sketch, because it will be. The goal is not a perfect answer. It is that you can see the distance between where you started and where you are, and say it in words.

Here is a warning about a common mistake, and a reason for it. Learners often feel that they must show how much they know. Do not pad the comparison with the list of things you built. A mentor has read your capstone and does not need it again. Spend your words on how your thinking changed: what you ask first, what you refuse to believe without a number, and what you check before you say a design works.

Write about 150 words in the answer box, and put the link to your capstone folder in the link field. You may add a photo of both sketches if you like, but the written answer is required. If a mentor has follow-up questions, answer them by editing and resubmitting.
