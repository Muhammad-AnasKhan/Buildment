For twenty-eight modules you built parts: a rate limiter, a hash ring, a Raft node, a saga. Now you build one whole system, from a blank page to a defence, and you show a mentor that it meets numbers you can measure. This chapter is short on purpose. Pick your system, then read how the work is judged, so you know the finish line before you start.

Pick the one whose hard part you most want to understand. Each system has one number that makes it hard, and you will find it in your estimates. The first fold below gives the traffic, the targets and the promises of all three. Read it before you choose.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the three systems in full">

All three systems are fictional composites. The traffic and the targets are made up for this course, with round numbers so the arithmetic is easy. Every system has the same three targets: a p99 latency, an availability and a cost per hour. Every system also has promises: things it must never get wrong, however busy or broken it is. A system that is fast but breaks a promise fails.

**A. Hayat Links.** Hayat is a made-up aid charity. It prints short links on posters and sends them in messages. On a normal day, 400 requests a second read links. When a post about a campaign goes viral, reads jump to 8,000 a second for ten seconds, and 7 in 10 of them ask for the same one link. The system stores links and answers each read with the link's address. It builds on module 20 (the URL shortener, chapters 20.3 and 20.4), on module 5 (cache patterns, stampedes and hot keys, chapters 5.3 and 5.6) and on chapter 9.4 (hot partitions). The hard part is one key: 70% of 8,000 is 5,600 reads a second for the same link. If one read costs the store 0.5 ms of work, that single link needs 2.8 seconds of store work every second. The promise: a link that the system acknowledged always returns the same address, and never an error saying it does not exist.

**B. Majlis.** Majlis is a made-up app for a student community with 5,000 students. Ten accounts are very popular, such as societies and well-known students, and 8 in 10 students follow each of them. Students also follow about 30 ordinary accounts. The app gets 20 new posts a second, 1 in 5 from a popular account, and 400 timeline reads a second. It builds on module 21 (the news feed, chapters 21.3 to 21.5), chapter 9.4 (skew) and chapter 15.4 (keys and order). The hard part is the shape of the followers: one post from a popular account must reach about 4,000 timelines, and four such posts a second means about 16,000 timeline writes a second. The promises: a timeline lists posts newest first, only from accounts the student follows, and a new post shows up within 2 seconds.

**C. Souq.** Souq is a made-up marketplace with 2,000 shoppers in two regions, called eu and na, 80 ms apart by round trip. Every second, about 200 cart adds, 200 cart reads and 30 purchases arrive. There are 20 items with 150 units each, and half of all purchases ask for just two items, so those two sell out in about ten seconds. It builds on module 13 (the cart that must always accept, chapters 13.1, 13.6 and 13.7), on module 19 (active-active and global consistency, chapters 19.4 and 19.6) and on chapter 25.2 (holds and stock). The hard part is that two promises pull in opposite directions when the link between the regions is cut. The promises: an acknowledged cart add is never lost, and no unit of stock is sold twice.

| | A. Hayat Links | B. Majlis | C. Souq |
|---|---|---|---|
| Latency target (p99) | 10 ms for link reads | 10 ms for timeline reads | 30 ms for cart reads and adds |
| Availability target | 99.9% of requests answered | 99.9% | 99.9% |
| Budget per hour (illustrative prices) | 0.40 | 0.50 | 0.60 |
| Busiest moment | 8,000 reads a second, 10 seconds | 20 posts and 400 reads a second | link cut between regions |
| Promise 1 | an acknowledged link keeps its address | newest first, followed accounts only | no acknowledged cart add is lost |
| Promise 2 | no false "not found" | a new post appears within 2 seconds | stock is never sold twice |
| Fault to expect in 29.4 | a slow or dead box at the peak | a dead box that holds popular timelines | the link between regions is cut |

You may propose a different system if your mentor agrees before you start. It must build on one of the case studies in modules 20 to 27, have the same three targets with numbers, and have at least one promise that you can check with a test. The harness in chapter 29.3 only runs these three, so a different system also means writing its traffic and its checks.

The estimates in your design document become the numbers you check in the build. The build becomes the thing you break. The measurements and the chart become the story you tell in your defence. In the last chapter you draw your first sketch for Daftar from module 1 again, and see how far you have come.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the design document template">

Chapter 29.2 is where you write the design document and the sandbox design. This is the template. Copy it into `challenges/challenge-18-capstone/DESIGN.md` in your repository. It is about three to five pages. A mentor reads it in ten minutes, so write numbers, not adjectives.

```text
# DESIGN.md: <name of your system>

## 1. What it must do
Five or fewer functional requirements, as things a user does. One thing out of scope.
The three targets from the scenario (p99, availability, cost per hour) and the promises.
Constraints: what else limits you (the box sizes and prices are fixed).

## 2. Estimates
Assumptions, each labelled as a guess or as given. Requests per second at a normal
moment and at the peak, by kind (read, write). Work per request in ms.
Worker time needed at the peak = requests per second x ms per request / 1000.
Mark the driving number in bold. Sensitivity: double your least sure assumption
and say what changes.

## 3. High-level design
One picture. Every box, its size, and the cost per hour as a sum.
The read path and the write path, two or three sentences each.

## 4. Two deep dives
For each: the choice, the number that makes it right now, what it buys, what it
costs, the alternative, and the number at which you would switch.

## 5. Data and consistency choices
For each kind of data: where it lives, who may write it, what a reader may see,
and why that is enough for the promises.

## 6. Failure modes
A table: what fails, what the user sees, how the system notices, how it recovers.
Include the fault you will test in 29.4, with your prediction in numbers.

## 7. SLOs and how you measure them
The three targets as SLIs with a window. The promise checks. The burn rate
at which you would page someone.

## 8. What I would change at ten times the traffic
The first thing that breaks, with a number, and the change you would make.
```

Two parts of this template are where weak documents lose marks. Part 2 must show working, with a unit on every line, the way you did in module 2. Part 4 must name an alternative you did not choose and the number at which you would choose it. "I used a cache because it is faster" is a claim. "A cache hit costs 0.01 ms and a store read costs 0.5 ms, so at 5,600 reads a second for one key a cache saves 2.7 seconds of store work every second" is a reason.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the rubric a mentor uses">

A mentor scores five things from 0 to 3. A 0 means missing. The other levels are below. To pass, every criterion needs at least a 2. A 3 is for work that goes further than the task asked. The rubric is the course's own.

| Criterion | 1: basic | 2: solid | 3: strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Requirements and numbers | Lists features, few numbers, units missing | Targets and promises stated; rates, work and cost estimated with units; the driving number marked | Also shows how sensitive the estimate is to one assumption, and sizes the boxes from it |
| Design choices | Boxes drawn with no reasons | Each major choice has an alternative and a reason with a number; consistency stated for each kind of data | Also says at which number each choice would flip, and what it costs |
| Correctness under failure | Tests pass only on the happy path | The promises are checked by tests and by the harness; zero wrong answers in the normal run and in the fault run | Also breaks a mechanism on purpose, shows the check failing, and explains the promise it protects |
| Evidence | Numbers pasted with no method | Normal-run table with three seeds meets all targets; the experiment has a written prediction, a chart and a result; the claims file matches the measurements | Also explains the gap between prediction and result, and tests a second fault or ten times the load |
| Communication | Reads the document aloud, or runs over time | Five parts in five minutes; names the driving number and one trade-off with a number; answers probes with numbers | No notes; explains a failure trade-off with a number; says what changes at ten times the traffic |

Each deliverable feeds one or two criteria. The design document and the sandbox design feed the first two. The core and its tests feed the third. The measurements and the experiment feed the fourth. The recording feeds the fifth, and it is also where the mentor checks that you can explain what you built.

| Deliverable | Where | Feeds |
|---|---|---|
| Design document | 29.2 | Requirements and numbers, design choices |
| Sandbox design that meets the targets | 29.2 | Design choices, evidence |
| Core with tests, green in CI | 29.3 | Correctness under failure, evidence |
| Failure experiment with a chart | 29.4 | Correctness under failure, evidence |
| Recorded defence and three probes | 29.5 | Communication, design choices |

A mentor reviews a capstone in under 45 minutes: about 5 minutes for the recording, 10 for the design document, 10 for the repository and the CI tick, 5 for the chart and the results, and 15 for one to three follow-up probes that you answer in writing or by voice.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: milestones and the defence format">

The capstone takes about 14 hours in all. The minutes below come from the course plan. Most learners work in several sittings, and every chapter ends on a point where you can stop and push your work.

| Milestone | Chapter | Minutes | Done when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pick your system | 29.1 | 30 | Your mentor knows which one |
| Design document and sandbox design | 29.2 | 180 | `DESIGN.md` is pushed, and the sandbox design meets the targets |
| Build the core | 29.3 | 360 | `npm test` is green on your laptop and in CI, and the normal-run table meets every target |
| Break it on purpose | 29.4 | 150 | The prediction was committed first; the chart and the result are in `results.md` |
| Record your defence | 29.5 | 90 | A recording link and three probe answers |
| Checklist and learning log | 29.6 | 30 | Everything submitted, and your comparison with Daftar written |

The defence is five minutes, in five parts. In the first 45 seconds you say what the system must do and the driving number. In the next minute you show one picture of the design and give three choices with a reason each. In the next minute and a quarter you show the evidence that it meets the targets. In the next minute and a quarter you show the chart of the failure experiment: what you predicted, what happened and why. In the last 45 seconds you say what breaks first at ten times the traffic, with a number, and what you would change. Then you answer three probes: one about scale, one about a failure and one about a trade-off. Chapter 29.5 gives a bank of probes and a way to answer them.

</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Sources">

The scenarios, targets, prices and rubric are the course's own and are fictional. The service times used later in the harness are round numbers from the napkin-math table: a query to a cache or database about 500 microseconds, an SSD write with fsync about 300 microseconds, a round trip inside a region about 250 microseconds and between EU West and North America East about 80 ms [S3]. That table says some of its numbers are two to three times off, so treat them as arithmetic aids, not benchmarks. The words SLI, SLO and error budget follow the Google SRE book [S95, S96]. The always-writable cart comes from the Dynamo paper [S45], and the fan-out trade-off in a timeline is taught as a trade-off, not as the design of any one company [S103].

</GoDeeper>
