## Your gate answer: a cache plan for a product page

A cache plan is the part of a design that most often goes wrong quietly. Nobody notices a missing cache in a design review; they notice a stale price at checkout, or a database that falls over when a popular copy expires. A good plan answers four questions for each piece of data, with a number and a reason for each answer.

The page for your answer is a product page in an online shop of your choice, like Matjar's. It shows the product's name, photos and description, its price, a stock hint ("only 3 left"), the average rating with a review count, and the shopper's own basket count in the header. You choose the shop's size and traffic. There is no single right plan. A mentor checks your reasoning and your numbers, not whether you made the same choices as someone else.

Answer four questions for each part of the page. **What** do you cache: the whole page, parts of it, or the result of an expensive query? **Where** does each copy live: the browser, a CDN edge, the app's memory, a shared cache such as Valkey? **How long** may each copy live, and what does a wrong copy cost the shop? **How** is each copy invalidated when the data changes: a TTL alone, delete-on-write, versioned names or keys, or a purge?

Then add two things that separate a strong plan from a list. First, your plan for the moment the busiest copy expires during a sale, using one of the fixes from chapter 5.6, with the herd you would get without it. Second, one number from your own challenge 4 results that supports a choice, such as the stale reads you measured for a TTL, or the queries per expiry you measured with and without a lock.

Watch for four common mistakes. The first is putting personal data in a shared layer: the basket count must never be kept by a CDN and shown to the next shopper, so it needs `private` or must be fetched separately. The second is caching the stock count the same way as the description: a stock hint can be a few seconds old, but the decision to sell the last item is made against the database. The third is a TTL with no reason: "5 minutes" means nothing unless you say what a 5-minute-old price costs. The fourth is forgetting the copies you cannot reach: a purge clears CDN edges, but a browser keeps its copy until its `max-age` ends.

Before you submit, check your plan the way a reviewer would. Pick the part of the page with the longest lifetime and ask what happens if that data changes one second after a copy was stored. If the answer is "shoppers see the wrong thing for the whole lifetime", either that is acceptable and you say why, or you need delete-on-write, a version or a purge. Then estimate one load number: with your traffic and your hit rate, how many queries a second reach the database? Compare it with what you measured in challenge 4.

Add the link to your challenge 4 folder in the link field. Your mentor will open your results next to your plan, because the two belong together: a plan says what a cache should do, and your measurements show what one actually did. If the mentor asks a follow-up question, answer it by editing and resubmitting.
