## Your gate answer: stock for an inventory service

Every store that sells something faces the same problem as Ticketline's last ticket: a count that two buyers change at the same time. In an interview this appears as "design the inventory service" or "how do you stop overselling". In a real team it appears the day a sale makes the count matter. A weak answer says "use a transaction" or "use Redis" and stops. A strong answer names the store, names the exact way a stock change is made safe, says what that costs, and says what to measure to know it works.

The service for your answer is a small shop's stock service. It tracks 20,000 products. A sale lowers a product's stock, a delivery raises it, and stock must never go below 0. On a sale day, 300 orders a minute arrive, and a few products are far more popular than the rest. You choose the details and state your assumptions. There is no single right answer. A mentor checks that your reasoning holds together and that your numbers are in the right range.

A good answer has five parts, and each takes a sentence or two. First, the store family and why: which question the service asks most, and how that points to your store. Second, the way a stock change is made safe: one statement, a row lock, a version check with retries, or Serializable with retries. Say why you chose it over the others. Third, the cost of your choice: waiting, repeated work, or a limit on sales per second for one product, with a number if you can estimate it. Fourth, what happens when it goes wrong: a buyer gives up after the retry limit, a row is hot, a transaction is held open too long. Fifth, one thing you would measure to know it works, such as the lock wait, the retries per sale, or the oversold count, which must stay at 0.

Use your own numbers where you can. If your challenge 6 run showed a one-statement update selling several times faster than a lock, you may use it, but say it came from a laptop. If the work between the read and the write is a payment call, say how long you assume it takes, because that single number decides between a lock and a version check.

Watch for four common mistakes. The first is choosing a store because it is popular, without saying which question it answers. The second is saying "use a transaction" without an isolation level or a fix. A transaction at the default level still loses updates when the app computes the new value. The third is forgetting the hot row. If one product takes most of the traffic, no choice removes the one-row limit. Say what you would do, such as splitting the stock across several rows. The fourth is an unbounded retry loop, which turns a busy product into a pile of wasted work. Name your retry limit.

The mentor reads for three things. Does the choice follow from how the data is read? Does the safe-change method match the work between the read and the write? And does the answer admit a cost? An answer that says its choice has no downside is the one a mentor questions first. A short, honest answer with one number beats a long one with none.
