## Your gate answer: a checkout saga fails

This is the question that started the module. A shop's checkout runs a saga with four steps: reserve stock, charge the card, book a courier and send a receipt. The first three have an undo step: release, refund and cancel. The receipt has none. Walk through two failures in writing. In the first, the courier booking fails because no courier is free. In the second, the worker crashes right after the card was charged and before the saga recorded that step as done. In an interview, this is the question "what happens when something fails in the middle?", and in a real team it is the first question of every incident review.

A weak answer says "we use a saga, so it rolls back". A strong answer is specific about order and state. For the first failure, list which actions ran and which undo steps run, and put them in order. The refund runs before the release, because the undo steps go newest first. The receipt never runs, because the saga stopped before it. Say what the customer sees: probably an "order could not be placed" message and, on a bank statement, a charge and then a refund. Do not call this a rollback. The charge happened, and a second action cancelled it.

For the second failure, say where the saga's state lives. The progress must be in a table, so a restarted worker can find the unfinished saga. The row still says "charge is next", so the worker runs the charge again. Explain why that is safe: the charge carries the order id as its idempotency key, so the payment service refuses a second charge and returns the first result. Then explain the other half, the message side. If the step was started by a message from a broker, the message may arrive twice, so the handler records the message id in the same transaction as its work. Use the words at-least-once and idempotent, and show where each applies.

Next, name what could still go wrong, with one cost. Other users can see the stock as held while the saga runs, because a saga has no isolation. A refund can fail, and then the saga is stuck and needs a person. The receipt can fail after everything else worked, and then it is retried and not undone. Pick at least one of these and say how you would detect it, for example an alert on sagas that stay in the compensating state for longer than a few minutes.

A good answer is short and has numbers from your own work. For the audit, you can say: "In my run B the worker crashed 19 times and the audit still found 0 unfinished sagas and 0 orders holding money or stock." If your answer says "some orders might be inconsistent", it is not finished.

Watch for three common mistakes. The first is saying the undo steps run in the order the actions ran: they run in reverse. The second is saying two-phase commit would fix everything: it would keep the stock and payments in step, but it would block both services when its coordinator is down, which is why most teams choose a saga across services. The third is saying "our broker is exactly-once, so no consumer check is needed": exactly-once effects come from the consumer, not from the broker.

A mentor reads for method, not for one right design. Two students can pick orchestration or choreography and both pass, as long as each one names the order of the undo steps, says where the state lives, and says what stops the repeated step from doing harm. A mentor may ask one or two follow-up questions, such as what happens if the refund fails, or what the customer sees while the saga runs. Answer them by editing and resubmitting.

Use the numbers you measured in challenge 13 where they help. You may write your answer, record it, or both. The written answer is required; a recording is optional and should show only your work, not your surroundings.
