Layla's order must change two databases: Stock and Payments. You want both to commit, or both to abort, never one of each. Two-phase commit, or 2PC, is the classic way to get that.

## Ask everyone first, then decide

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m14-2pc-happy" />

A coordinator runs the show. In phase one it asks each database: can you commit? Each one saves its work, locks its rows and votes. In phase two the coordinator reads the votes. If all said yes, it sends commit. If any said no, it sends abort. Everyone ends with the same answer.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: the two phases, step by step">

**Phase one, prepare.** The coordinator sends `prepare` to every participant, the name for each database in the transaction. A participant does the work of the transaction, writes it to its own durable log, and writes a record that says "prepared". Only then does it answer yes. If it cannot do the work, for example because a constraint fails, it answers no and rolls back at once.

**Phase two, decide.** The coordinator counts the votes. All yes means commit. One no, or one participant that never answered, means abort. The coordinator writes the decision to its own log first, because after that write the decision is final even if the coordinator crashes. Then it sends the decision to every participant, and each one applies it and releases its locks.

Why two phases? One phase would be a coordinator saying "commit" to everyone. If Payments then hit a full disk, Stock would already have committed and the two would disagree. The first phase finds out whether everyone can commit before anyone does it. This is the protocol that Gray and Lamport analyse [S77].

You do not often write a coordinator yourself. A database may offer the participant side. PostgreSQL has `PREPARE TRANSACTION`, which saves a transaction in the prepared state, and `COMMIT PREPARED` or `ROLLBACK PREPARED` to finish it later, even from another session. Its documentation says the feature is meant for an external transaction manager and not for applications.

</GoDeeper>

## A yes is a promise you keep

<MermaidDiagram title="What a participant may do, state by state" chart={`flowchart LR
  W["Working: can still say no"] -->|"votes yes"| P["Prepared: locks held, must wait"]
  W -->|"votes no, or times out"| A["Aborted"]
  P -->|"coordinator says commit"| C["Committed"]
  P -->|"coordinator says abort"| A
`} legend="From Prepared, only the coordinator's decision moves a participant. It cannot choose by itself." />

Before it votes, a database can still say no and leave alone. After it votes yes, it has promised to commit if told to. It keeps its locks and waits for the decision. It may not commit or abort on its own, because it does not know what the others voted.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why a prepared participant cannot decide alone">

Put yourself in Stock's place. You voted yes and you are prepared. The coordinator goes silent. You have two choices, and each can be wrong.

If you commit, the coordinator may have heard a no from Payments and decided to abort. Then Payments rolls back and you have committed: the two databases now disagree. If you abort, the coordinator may have heard two yes votes, logged commit, and told Payments to commit before it crashed. Then Payments committed and you aborted. Both are the half-done order you wanted to avoid.

You could ask Payments what it knows. That helps only if Payments already heard the decision. If both of you are prepared and neither has heard, neither can know. This state is called <GlossaryTerm term="in doubt" definition="A prepared participant that has not yet heard the coordinator's decision. It cannot commit or abort alone, so it waits with its locks held." />. The only safe move is to wait.

This is the blocking problem. Gray and Lamport state it plainly: two-phase commit blocks if the coordinator fails [S77].

</GoDeeper>

## Crash the coordinator at each step

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m14-2pc-crash" />

Drag the crash point along the five steps, and drag how long the coordinator stays down. Before the votes are in, both databases can still abort alone. After the votes, they are stuck with their locks until it returns. Watch the count of orders waiting behind them grow with the downtime.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what each crash point leaves behind">

There are five places where the coordinator can stop. The outcome depends on what the participants know at that moment.

| Crash point | Stock DB | Payments DB | Blocked? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before prepare is sent | Working | Working | No. Both can abort alone. |
| After votes, before the decision is logged | Prepared | Prepared | Yes, both. The new coordinator has no record and must abort or ask. |
| After the decision is logged, before it is sent | Prepared | Prepared | Yes, both, until the coordinator restarts and reads its log. |
| After commit reached Stock only | Committed | Prepared | Payments only. It can ask Stock and learn "commit", if Stock is up. |
| After both were told | Committed | Committed | No. |

Look at rows two and three. In both, the participants are in the same state. They cannot tell the two rows apart, and neither can anyone outside. What differs is what the coordinator's disk holds. In row three, a restart finds "commit" in the log and finishes the job. In row two there is no decision in the log, so the usual rule is to abort. Either way the participants wait for as long as the coordinator is down.

In the picture, the number waiting is the downtime times an illustrative 2 orders per second that need the same rows. Ten seconds down is 20 orders, ten minutes is 1,200. These are course numbers. Real piles depend on how many orders touch the same rows.

</GoDeeper>

## Locked rows queue up behind the wait

<InteractiveCanvas slug="sysdesign-m14-2pc-stuck" />

While Stock DB and Payments DB wait, they hold their locks. Any other order that needs the same rows must queue behind them. At 2 orders a second, 15 seconds of downtime is 30 orders waiting. The longer the coordinator is gone, the bigger the pile.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: a lock held for a whole outage">

In chapter 7.5 a row lock held for 200 ms limited one event to 5 sales a second. A lock is held for as long as the transaction needs it. In 2PC the transaction cannot finish until the decision arrives, so the hold time includes a network round trip in the good case, and the coordinator's whole downtime in the bad case.

PostgreSQL's documentation warns about this. A prepared transaction keeps whatever locks it held. It is unwise to leave one prepared for a long time: it can stop `VACUUM` from reclaiming space and, in extreme cases, the database may have to shut down to avoid transaction ID wraparound. The documentation also suggests keeping `max_prepared_transactions` at zero if you have no transaction manager to close prepared transactions promptly.

So the real cost of 2PC is not the extra messages. It is that a failure in one component, the coordinator, freezes work in components that were healthy.

</GoDeeper>

## Three ways to survive a lost coordinator

<MermaidDiagram title="Ways out when the coordinator is gone" chart={`flowchart TD
  S["Coordinator crashed after the votes"] --> A["Wait for it to restart and read its log"]
  S --> B["Ask the other participants what they know"]
  S --> C["Make the coordinator a group: Paxos Commit"]
  A --> A1["Always works, but blocked until it returns"]
  B --> B1["Works only if someone already heard the decision"]
  C --> C1["Progress with F+1 of 2F+1 coordinators"]
`} legend="Only the third option removes the single point of failure, and it costs more machines and messages." />

You can wait for a restart, ask the other participants, or replace the single coordinator with a group. Paxos Commit does the last one: with 2F+1 coordinators it keeps going while F+1 of them are alive. The cost is more machines and more messages for every commit.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: replicated coordinators in practice">

Gray and Lamport show that 2PC is a special case of a protocol they call Paxos Commit [S77]. Instead of one coordinator that holds the decision, the decision is stored by a group of acceptors using the consensus idea from module 12. With 2F+1 coordinators, the protocol makes progress as long as F+1 are up. One crash no longer freezes the participants.

Some distributed databases use this shape. A transaction that spans several data partitions runs 2PC, and the coordinator role is itself held by a replicated group, so losing one machine does not leave the participants in doubt. Google's Spanner is a well-known example. {/* source needed: Spanner runs two-phase commit across Paxos groups, with a replicated participant leader as coordinator (Spanner paper, OSDI 2012, section 4.2.1, not yet read for this module) */}

For a team with its own services, that is a lot of machinery. If you cannot run your own consensus group, the safer move is to avoid cross-service 2PC and use a saga, which never holds locks across services.

</GoDeeper>

## Why microservice teams rarely choose it

<MermaidDiagram title="Choosing between a transaction, 2PC and a saga" chart={`flowchart TD
  Q1{"One database holds all the data?"} -->|"yes"| T["Use one ordinary transaction (7.4)"]
  Q1 -->|"no"| Q2{"Every participant supports prepare, and can you accept locks across services?"}
  Q2 -->|"yes"| P["Two-phase commit, with a coordinator you can restart"]
  Q2 -->|"no"| S["Use a saga (14.3)"]
`} legend="Start with the cheapest tool. Most cross-service workflows end at the saga." />

2PC needs every participant to support the prepare step, and it holds locks across a network. The more services and the longer the wait, the worse the pile. That is why many teams choose a saga for work that crosses services. Chapter 14.3 shows how.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what is lost when you skip 2PC">

2PC gives something sagas do not: isolation. While a 2PC transaction is prepared, other transactions cannot see its half-done state, because the locks hide it. A saga commits each step at once, so other users can see the middle (14.3). You trade isolation for availability. When one service is down, a saga's other steps can still commit, and your system keeps working in part. That trade is the same one module 11 describes for consistency: strong guarantees cost availability.

For a bank moving money between two accounts that live in one database, the ordinary transaction of 7.4 is still the right tool, and 2PC is never needed. For two services owned by different teams, a saga with a clear undo for each step is usually easier to run.

</GoDeeper>
