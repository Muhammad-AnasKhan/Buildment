## Two ways to get it wrong

Reem and Sami are junior developers on the same remote team, working for an NGO.

Reem gets an error when she runs the project. She does not want to look weak, so she tries alone for the whole day. In the evening she asks the team. A teammate answers in five minutes: one setting was missing. Reem lost a day.

Sami gets a different error. After two minutes he writes in the team chat: "Hi, are you free? Something is broken." His teammate is six hours behind and asleep. When she wakes up, she has to ask him what is broken. Sami waits half a day for an answer that a quick search would have given him.

Both made the same mistake from opposite sides. Reem asked too late. Sami asked too early and too vaguely.

## Step 1: try for a set time

Before you start on a problem, choose a time-box. For a normal problem, 30 to 60 minutes is a common choice. Make it shorter when someone is waiting on your work or a deadline is close.

During the time-box:

- **Read the whole error message.** The most useful line is often at the bottom or the top, not in the middle.
- **Change one thing at a time,** so you know which change made the difference.
- **Explain the problem out loud or in writing.** Programmers call this "rubber duck debugging", from the book *The Pragmatic Programmer*: you explain your code, line by line, to a toy duck. Often you find the answer while you explain.

## Step 2: search well

A good search often solves the problem before you need to ask anyone.

- **Copy the exact error text.** Put the main part in quotes so the search looks for those exact words.
- **Remove what is only about your computer,** such as your file paths, your username and line numbers.
- **Add the tool name and version.**
- **Check the date and the source.** Prefer official docs, the tool's own issue tracker on GitHub and recent answers on Stack Overflow.

AI assistants can also explain an error. Treat their answers like a tip from a stranger: test them, and check them against the docs, because they can be wrong while sounding sure.

## Step 3: ask a clear question

When your time-box ends and search has not helped, ask. In a remote team, write your question so that someone can answer it **in one reply**, even if they read it hours later. Do not send "Are you free?" and wait. Send the full question at once.

Use this template:

```text
Goal: what I am trying to do
What I tried: the steps, and what happened after each one
Exact error: copied and pasted, not retyped
What I expected: and what happened instead
```

Here is Reem's question, written the next time she got stuck:

> **Goal:** Run the NGO app locally so I can test the new donation form.
>
> **What I tried:** Ran `pip install -r requirements.txt`, then restarted the terminal. Searched the error; the answers say to install the package, which I did.
>
> **Exact error:** `ModuleNotFoundError: No module named 'requests'`
>
> **What I expected:** The app to start on port 8000. Instead it stops at once with this error.
>
> Python 3.12 on Windows. Any idea what I am missing?

Her teammate can see that Reem has already tried the obvious fix. That points straight to the likely cause: the package may be installed for a different copy of Python than the one running the app.

<Callout type="warning" title="Never paste secrets">
Before you share code or logs, remove passwords, API keys and any client or user data. Replace them with something like `API_KEY=xxxx`.
</Callout>

When you get an answer, say thank you, say what worked, and add it to your notes. If you solved it yourself, post the fix too. The next person with the same problem will find it.
