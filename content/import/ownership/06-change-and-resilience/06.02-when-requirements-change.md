## A late request

Khaled is a freelance web developer. He is building a booking website for a small dental clinic. The price and the deadline were agreed four weeks ago. One week before launch, the clinic manager, Helen, writes:

> "Our patients keep asking for reminders. Can you add SMS reminders before each appointment? Should be quick!"

Khaled wants to keep Helen happy. He also knows that "should be quick" is often not true. SMS reminders need a paid SMS service, a way to store phone numbers safely, and testing.

## Step 1: Understand the reason

A change usually has a reason behind it. Ask about it before you plan anything. Good questions are short and calm:

- "What problem are the patients having?"
- "Does this need to be ready for launch, or can it come soon after?"
- "Who asked for this, and who will decide?"

Khaled asks. Helen explains that many patients forget their appointments, so the clinic loses money on empty slots. Now Khaled understands the real goal: fewer missed appointments. SMS is one way to reach it, but not the only way. An email reminder, for example, is much faster to build.

On a team, the steps are the same. If your lead changes a task in the middle of a sprint (a short, planned block of work, often two weeks), ask what changed and what matters most now.

## Step 2: Check the impact

Every change costs something. When the **scope** (the full list of agreed work) grows, something else usually moves: the deadline, the price, or another feature. Before you say yes, check four things:

| Question | Khaled's answer |
|---|---|
| How much time does it add? | About four extra days, with testing |
| Does it cost money? | Yes, the SMS service charges per message |
| Does it affect other work? | Launch moves, or another feature waits |
| Does it add risk? | Phone numbers are private data to protect |

Write rough numbers, not perfect ones. A range like "three to five days" is honest and useful.

<Callout type="warning" title="Watch out for scope creep">
**Scope creep** means many small changes that add up until the project is much bigger than the price and deadline that were agreed. Each "quick" change looks harmless. Together they can make a project fail. Checking the impact of every change is how you stop it.
</Callout>

## Step 3: Offer options and agree a new plan

Do not say only yes or no. Give the client a choice, with the cost of each option. Then confirm the decision in writing, so nobody remembers it differently later.

> Hi Helen, thank you for explaining. Fewer missed appointments is a great goal. Here are two options:
>
> 1. Launch on the planned date with email reminders. No extra cost.
> 2. Add SMS reminders. This adds about four days, so launch moves to the 18th. The extra work costs 120 dollars, plus the SMS service fees.
>
> Which do you prefer? Once you decide, I will update the plan and send it to you.

Helen chooses option 1 now and SMS reminders next month. The launch stays on time, and Khaled has a paid follow-up project.

## Your feelings about the change

A change can feel unfair, especially when work you were proud of will not be used. That feeling is normal. It helps to wait a little before you reply, so the reply is about the project and not about the frustration. The work is also rarely wasted: you learned the problem better, and parts of the code or design can often be reused.
