## Friday morning

Ola is a backend developer at a small online learning startup. Her team works from Gaza, Amman and Toronto. On Thursday evening she released a change to the login page.

On Friday morning she checks the error logs and feels a rush of worry. Since her change, the "reset your password" emails have not been sent. About 40 students asked for a reset overnight and got nothing.

She can see the cause. It is a one-line fix. Her team lead, Marco, is in Toronto, seven hours behind, and still asleep. Nobody else has noticed yet.

## Why the quiet fix is tempting, and costly

Fixing a mistake and saying nothing feels safer. You avoid an uncomfortable message, and maybe nobody will ever know. But a quiet fix has hidden costs:

- **The people affected still need help.** The 40 students do not know they can try again.
- **Others decide without the facts.** Support may tell students to check their spam folder, which wastes everyone's time.
- **Mistakes usually come out later.** Then the question is no longer "What went wrong?" but "Why did she not tell us?" That is much harder to recover from.
- **The cause stays in place.** If the release process let this through, the same thing can happen to someone else next month.

A mistake you report early is usually treated as normal work. A mistake that others discover becomes a question about trust. Many tech teams hold "blameless" reviews after problems: they look for what in the process allowed the mistake, not for a person to punish. Your honest report is what makes that kind of review possible.

## The four parts of a mistake report

A good report answers four questions, in this order.

| Part | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| **What happened** | What went wrong, and when? |
| **Impact** | Who or what was affected, and how much? |
| **Fix** | What have you done, or what are you doing now? |
| **What changes** | What will stop it happening again? |

Here is the message Ola posts in the team channel at 08:45:

> Hi Marco and support team. I made a mistake in Thursday's release. My change to the login page stopped password reset emails from sending, from 19:00 Thursday to 08:30 today, Gaza time.
>
> **Impact:** about 40 students asked for a reset and got no email. I have the list of accounts.
>
> **Fix:** I released a fix at 08:30 and tested a reset myself. It works now. Support, could you email those students to ask them to try again? I can draft the note.
>
> **What changes:** I did not test the reset flow before releasing. From now on I will test it before any release that touches login, and I would like to add an automatic test for it this week. Sorry for the extra work this morning.

Look at what the message does:

- **It starts with the mistake**, in one sentence. No long story first.
- **It gives facts**: times and numbers, not "some users had issues".
- **It says "I"**, not "a bug happened" or "the code broke".
- **It apologizes once, briefly.** A long apology makes the message about your feelings instead of the fix.
- **It offers help** with the next step.

## Common traps

| Trap | Sounds like | Better |
|---|---|---|
| Hiding behind vague words | "Some emails were not sent" | "My change stopped the emails" |
| Blaming others | "Testing should have caught it" | Report your part first |
| Too much self-blame | "I am useless, I ruin everything" | "I made a mistake. Here is the fix" |
| Waiting for the full fix | Silence until Monday | Report now, update later |

The last trap matters most. You do not need a fix before you report. "I found a problem caused by my change. I am working on it. Next update at 11:00." is a good first message.

<Callout type="tip" title="When the cause is outside your control">
Sometimes a long power cut or a lost connection makes you miss a deadline. That is not a mistake in your work, but the impact on others is real. Use the same shape: what happened, the impact, what you are doing now, and what changes, such as a backup plan. You do not need to over-explain or apologize for your situation.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Malak, a freelance designer, sent a client the final logo files on Monday. On Wednesday she notices that one of the files still shows the client's old company name. The client has not replied yet. Which first message is best?"
options:
  - "Hi Sam, I think a few of the files may have small issues. Please wait for an update"
  - "Hi Sam, I am so sorry. I feel terrible about this. It was a very busy week, but that is no excuse"
  - "Hi Sam, the PNG logo I sent Monday has the old name. Please do not use it. New file by 3 pm"
  - "Hi Sam, attached is an updated set of files with some small improvements. Use these"
correct: 2
explanations:
  - "This is too vague. Sam does not know which file is wrong, so he may already be using it."
  - "This is all feelings and no facts. Sam still does not know what is wrong or what to do."
  - "Right. It names the file and the problem, tells Sam what to do now, and says when the fix will come."
  - "This hides the mistake. If Sam already shared the old file, he does not know he must replace it."
```
