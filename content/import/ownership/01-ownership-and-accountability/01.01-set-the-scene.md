## Two developers, one kind of task

Heba and Basel are junior developers. They work remotely for the same small startup. Their team lead, Diana, lives in Lisbon, two hours behind Gaza. On Monday, each of them gets a similar ticket: fix a bug in the app's sign-up form.

Heba fixes the bug. The automatic tests pass, so she closes the ticket and moves on to the next one.

Basel fixes the bug too. Then he opens the sign-up page on his phone, the way most of the app's users do, and signs up as a test user. The form works now. But the welcome email takes ten minutes to arrive, and many users give up before then. That problem is not in his ticket. He sends Diana two lines: what he saw, and an offer to look into it tomorrow if she wants.

Both of them did their task. Only one of them checked the result.

## Why this matters for remote work

When you work remotely, your manager cannot see you at your desk. They see what you deliver and what you tell them. Over time they learn who they need to check on, and who they can trust to finish things properly and to speak up when something is wrong.

That trust is what ownership builds. It is often the reason one junior gets bigger projects, better clients or a renewed contract, while another person with the same technical skill does not.

Ownership is not about working longer hours or doing everyone else's job. It is a set of small habits:

- Knowing why your work matters and who it is for.
- Checking that it really works, not only that the task is closed.
- Telling people early when something goes wrong, including your own mistakes.
- Finishing what you start, and saying so clearly when you cannot.

Like any skill, these habits can be learned and practiced. This course gives you the practice.
