## The task was done. The problem was not.

Majd is a freelance developer. An NGO that runs after-school classes asks him to add a phone number field to its volunteer sign-up form. He adds the field, checks that it saves, and sends the coordinator, Ruba, a short message: "Done."

Two weeks later Ruba writes back. She wanted to send volunteers a text message (SMS) before each class. But the numbers are saved in different formats, some with the country code and some without, and the SMS tool rejects half of them. Many volunteers got no reminder, and two classes started without enough helpers.

Majd did exactly what he was asked. So was his work finished?

## Task, output, outcome

Every piece of work has three levels. Most people only look at the first two.

| Level | Question | Majd's example |
|---|---|---|
| **Task** | What was I asked to do? | Add a phone number field |
| **Output** | What did I produce? | A field that saves numbers |
| **Outcome** | What changed for the user? | Volunteers get reminders |

A task-only mindset stops at the output. Ownership means you also care about the outcome. You do not have to build everything in the outcome row yourself. But you want to know what it is, and you want to know if your work helps it happen.

## Three habits of owning the outcome

**1. Ask why before you start.** Find out what problem the task solves and for whom. One short question is often enough: "What will you use this for?" or "How will we know it worked?" If you cannot reach the person, write down your best guess and send it with your work so they can correct you. Module 3 goes deeper into unclear tasks.

**2. Check it where it will be used.** Passing tests on your laptop is a good start, not the finish. Try the work the way the real user will: on a phone, on a slow connection, with realistic data, and in the next tool in the chain. Majd could have exported five test numbers into the SMS tool in ten minutes.

**3. Close the loop.** To close the loop means to go back and confirm the result, so nothing is left open. When you deliver, say what you checked and how the other person can check it. A few days later, ask if it is working: "Did the reminders go out on Monday?" This short follow-up catches problems while they are still small.

## What ownership is not

Owning the outcome does not mean doing everything alone, working all night, or quietly redoing other people's work. If you find a problem outside your task, tell the right person clearly. You do not have to fix it yourself. Module 2 covers how to bring a suggestion along with the problem.

It also does not mean promising things you cannot control. If power cuts mean you can only test for two hours a day, saying so early is part of owning the result.

<Callout type="warning" title="Watch out for 'it works on my machine'">
Developers joke about this sentence because it is so common: the code works on the developer's computer but fails for real users. When you hear yourself say it, take it as a sign to check the work in the place where it will really be used.
</Callout>

## Common questions

**Q: What if I cannot find out what the outcome is?**
A: Write your best guess in your delivery message, for example "I am assuming this is for the SMS reminders. Tell me if not." This lets the other person correct you early.

**Q: Is it my job to check things outside my task?**
A: It is your job to check that your work does what it is for. If you see a problem beyond that, tell the right person. You do not need to fix it yourself.
