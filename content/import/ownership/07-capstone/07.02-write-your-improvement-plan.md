## The five parts

A good improvement plan fits on one page. It answers five questions.

| Part | What it answers |
|---|---|
| 1. Goals | What two or three things will I improve, and what does done look like by when? |
| 2. Habits | What small action will I repeat each day or week for each goal? |
| 3. Obstacles | What is likely to get in my way, and what will I do then? |
| 4. Review dates | When will I look at my progress, and what will I check? |
| 5. Check-in person | Who will ask me how it is going, and how? |

## 1. Goals: two or three, with a date

In module 3 you learned to ask what success looks like. Use that here. A goal is clear when someone else could look at your work on a date and say "yes, this is done".

- "Get better at communication" is not clear.
- "Send each client a three-line update every Monday and Thursday, without being asked, by 15 December" is clear.

Keep it to two or three goals for six to eight weeks. More than that, and each one gets too little of your time.

## 2. Habits: small enough for a bad day

Each goal gets one habit. The goal is what you want. The habit is what you do.

Four rules make a habit realistic when power and internet are not reliable:

- **Tie it to an event, not only a clock.** "When the power comes on in the evening" or "after I charge my phone" works better than "at 9:00" if your power schedule changes.
- **Give it a minimum version.** The normal version might be 30 minutes of practice. The minimum version might be 10 minutes reading saved notes, or planning the next step on paper. On a bad day, the minimum still counts.
- **Count days per week, not a perfect streak.** "4 days out of 7" survives a lost evening. "Every single day" breaks the first time something goes wrong.
- **Prepare offline.** When you have internet, save the documentation pages, tutorials and practice files you need. Keep a paper notebook. Then the habit does not depend on a connection.

## 3. Obstacles: name them before they come

You already know many of the things that will get in your way. Write each one as an **if-then plan**: "If this happens, then I will do that." The psychologist Peter Gollwitzer studied plans like this and called them implementation intentions. His research found that deciding in advance what to do in a hard moment makes people more likely to follow through.

Common obstacles to plan for:

- No power or no internet at the time of your habit
- Urgent client or team work
- Exams or a busy week at your job
- Low energy, or a hard week at home
- Missing several days and feeling it is too late

The last one ends more plans than any other. Module 6 showed that a setback is normal and that recovery starts with a small next step. So plan for it now: "If I miss a whole week, then I restart the next day with the minimum version. I do not try to catch up."

## 4. Review dates: look back on purpose

Choose a review every two weeks, plus a final review at the end. Put them in your phone calendar with an alarm. It works offline.

At each review, check four things:

1. How many days did I do each habit?
2. What progress did I make on each goal?
3. What got in the way?
4. What one change will I make?

A review is not a test you pass or fail. Like module 4, it is a short check that the work is still moving toward done. If a habit is not working, change the habit. Do not drop the goal.

## 5. Who will check in

A plan that nobody else knows about is easy to forget. Choose one person: your mentor, a study partner, a colleague or a friend. Agree how they will hear from you. A short message after each review date is enough.

```text
Progress check, 29 November
Habits: portfolio 8 of 10 weekdays, client updates 4 of 4.
Win: my projects page is live.
Got in the way: no power for three evenings.
Change: I plan the next task on paper the night before.
```

## A complete example

Rania is a frontend developer in her final year at university. She freelances for two clients, a small online shop in Berlin and a local NGO. Here is her plan.

### Plan: 1 November to 27 December

**Goal 1: Finish my portfolio site.** It is online with three projects, each with a short description of the problem and my part, by 13 December.
Habit: on weekdays, when the power comes on, I do one task from my task list for 45 minutes. Minimum: 10 minutes planning the next task on paper.

**Goal 2: Update clients before they ask.** Both clients get a three-line update (done, next, blocked) every Monday and Thursday, without asking.
Habit: on Sunday and Wednesday evenings, I draft the updates in my phone notes. I send them the next morning.

**Goal 3: Learn basic TypeScript.** By 27 December, I convert one small project from JavaScript to TypeScript.
Habit: 20 minutes, 4 days a week, using documentation I saved offline. Minimum: read one saved page.

**Obstacles and plans:**

- If the power is off in the evening, then I plan on paper and do the screen work the next time the power is on.
- If the internet is down on an update day, then I send it with mobile data. If I have none, I send it as soon as I connect and say why it is late.
- If exams take my time in December, then all three habits drop to their minimum version for those two weeks.
- If I miss a whole week, then I restart the next day with the minimum version and do not try to catch up.

**Review dates:** 15 November, 29 November, 13 December and a final review on 27 December. I check habit days, progress on each goal, what got in the way and one change.

**Check-in:** I send my mentor, Waleed, a progress check message after each review date. My study partner Rasha and I also ask each other every Sunday: "Did you do your habits this week?"

```quiz
  - "If the power is off, I will try harder to find some free time later in the same week"
  - "If the power is off, I will write my queries on paper and run them when it returns"
  - "If the power is off for a long time, I will pause the plan until the schedule is more stable"
  - "If the power is off, I will make up for it with a double session on the next evening"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "This is too vague. It does not say what she will do or when, so it is easy to forget."
  - "Right. It names the obstacle and a specific action she can take without power, and it keeps her practicing."
  - "Power cuts are a normal part of her weeks. Pausing until they stop may mean the plan never restarts."
  - "Double sessions make the habit bigger, and the next evening may have no power either. Missed days pile up."
```

## Before you submit

Read your plan as if you were your mentor. Then check each part.

| Part | Ask yourself |
|---|---|
| Goals | Are there two or three? Could someone else tell when each one is done? |
| Habits | Does each habit have a minimum version I could do with no power or internet? |
| Obstacles | Did I name the problems I already know will come, with an if-then plan? |
| Review dates | Are they real dates in my calendar, with what I will check? |
| Check-in | Did I name a person and how they will hear from me? |

You do not need to finish in one sitting. If the power goes or your connection drops, write on paper or in your phone notes, then type the plan when you can.

<Callout type="info" title="What your mentor looks for">
Your mentor checks that the plan is clear and realistic: goals someone can check, small habits, honest obstacles with plans, and real dates. Expect questions, not a score.
</Callout>
