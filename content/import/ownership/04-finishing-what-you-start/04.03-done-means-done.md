## One word, two meanings

Yasmin is a developer on a remote team for a small startup. Her team lead, Daniel, works five hours behind her. In her end-of-day update she writes:

> Password reset bug: done.

The next morning, a customer emails Daniel to complain that password reset is still broken. Daniel had already told the customer it was fixed. He checks and finds that Yasmin's fix is on her own branch. It has not been reviewed, merged or released. She tested it on her laptop, but not on a phone, where most customers reset their passwords.

Yasmin did real work. But Daniel now looks unreliable to the customer, and Yasmin looks unreliable to Daniel.

## A simple definition of done

Many software teams, especially teams that use the Scrum method, write a shared definition of done. You do not need a big process to use the idea. For most work, four questions are enough:

1. **Works:** does it do what was asked, in the place where people will use it?
2. **Tested:** did you check it, including the unusual cases, not only the happy path?
3. **Documented:** can someone else understand and use it without asking you?
4. **Handed off:** does the person who needs it have it, and know it is ready?

The "happy path" is the normal case where the user does everything as expected. Real users do not always stay on it.

## For code and for non-code work

The four questions work for any task. Only the details change.

| Part | Code example | Non-code example (a donor report) |
|---|---|---|
| Works | The fix works on the live site or test server, not just your laptop | It answers the questions the donor asked |
| Tested | Normal and odd cases checked, on phone and desktop; tests pass | Numbers checked against the source; proofread once |
| Documented | Clear pull request description; README updated if needed | Sources listed; file has a clear name and date |
| Handed off | Reviewed and merged, or the client is told how to use it | Sent to the right person, who confirms they got it |

Not every task needs the same amount of each part. A one-line typo fix needs a quick check and a one-line message. A new payment feature needs much more. The questions stay the same; the effort grows with the risk.

## Agree it before you start

The best time to agree what done means is at the start, while you are clarifying the task, as you practiced in module 3. Ask a simple question:

> Before I start: when this is done, should it be live for users, or ready for your review?

If your team already has a definition of done, use theirs. If it does not, your four questions are a good start, and you can share them.

## Finishing the last ten percent

Testing, documentation and handoff are the parts people leave for "later", and later often never comes. Two habits help:

- **Put them in your breakdown from the start.** When you split a task, as in chapter 4.2, add steps such as "test on a phone", "write the README section" and "send the handoff message". Then they are real steps, not extras.
- **Say exactly what is left.** If you are not done, do not say "done" or "almost done". Say what is finished and what remains.

A good handoff message is short and complete:

> Hi Daniel, the password reset fix is merged and live. I tested it on Android, iPhone and desktop, including an expired link. The PR description explains the cause. Nothing is left on my side. Could you let the customer know?

## Common questions

**Q: What if the client says it is done but I know it is not tested well?**
A: Tell them what you checked and what you did not, in one or two sentences. Then let them decide. Hiding the gap is the problem, not the gap itself.

**Q: Does "handed off" mean my job is over?**
A: It means the work is in the right hands. Stay available for questions, and follow up if you do not hear back in a day or two.
