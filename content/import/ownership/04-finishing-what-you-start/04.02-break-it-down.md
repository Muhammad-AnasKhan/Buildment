## The task that never starts

Omar works part time for an NGO that runs youth workshops. His manager, Nadia, asks him to "build the volunteer sign-up feature" for the NGO's website. She thinks it will take about a week.

On Monday, Omar opens the project, looks at the code for twenty minutes and closes it again. On Tuesday he does the same. He is not lazy. The task is simply too big to hold in his head. Every time he looks at it, he sees ten problems at once and does not know which one to touch first.

## What makes a good step

"Build the sign-up feature" is not a step. It is a project. A good step has four things:

1. **It starts with a verb.** "Write", "send", "test", "fix", "ask". You know what to physically do.
2. **It has a visible finish.** Anyone could look and say "yes, that is done" or "no, not yet".
3. **It fits in a day or less.** Smaller is better. Many people aim for one to three hours, which also fits the hours when power is usually on.
4. **It is yours to do.** If a step depends on someone else, the step is "ask" or "send", not "wait".

| Vague | Clear step |
|---|---|
| Work on the form | Build the form with name, email and phone fields, no styling yet |
| Fix the emails | Send one test confirmation email to my own address |
| Do the database part | Add a volunteers table with the four agreed fields |
| Look into security | Read the framework's page on form validation for 45 minutes |

The writer David Allen, in his book *Getting Things Done*, calls this the **next action**: the very next physical thing you can do to move a project forward. If you cannot picture yourself doing it, it is not small enough yet.

## How to break a task down

You can do this on paper or in any notes app. It usually takes ten to twenty minutes.

1. **Write the end result in one sentence.** "Volunteers can sign up on the website and get a confirmation email."
2. **List the big pieces.** Form, database, confirmation email, admin list, testing, instructions for Nadia.
3. **Split any piece that is bigger than a day.** Keep splitting until every step passes the four checks above.
4. **Put them in order.** Make the first step tiny, so you can start today.
5. **Turn unknowns into time-boxed steps.** A time box is a fixed amount of time you give a task, then you stop and decide what to do next. "Read how the email service works for one hour" is a step. "Figure out emails" is not.

Here is part of Omar's list:

```text
Goal: volunteers can sign up and get a confirmation email.
1. List form fields, send to Nadia to check (30 min)
2. Build the form, no styling (2 h)
3. Save sign-ups to a volunteers table (2 h)
4. Read the email service docs, 1 hour time box
5. Send a confirmation email after sign-up (2 h)
...
```

## It works for non-code tasks too

Amal is writing a six-month report for an NGO's donor. "Write the donor report" feels heavy. Broken down, it becomes: collect attendance numbers from the three centers, write the summary paragraph, add two photos with permission, ask her manager to check the numbers, and so on. Each one fits in an afternoon.

<Callout type="tip" title="Leave a note before you stop">
Before you stop for the day, or when the power is about to go, write one line: "Next: test the email with a wrong address." When you come back, you start in seconds instead of spending half an hour remembering where you were.
</Callout>

## Common questions

**Q: Is breaking things down a waste of time when I could just start coding?**
A: For a one-hour task, yes, just start. For anything longer than a day, ten minutes of breakdown usually saves hours of confusion.

**Q: What if my list changes while I work?**
A: It will, and that is fine. Add, remove and reorder steps as you learn. The list is a tool, not a promise.
