<BigWordAlert term="Gap" plainEnglish="The difference between how things are now and how they could or should be." whyItMatters="You cannot take initiative on a problem you have not noticed. Spotting gaps is the first step." />

## Why gaps stay hidden

Tariq joined a small startup as a junior back-end developer. The team works across three time zones. In his first week, he notices something strange: every Monday morning, the weekly sales report for the client fails to send. Someone on the team re-runs it by hand, and nobody talks about it.

When Tariq asks, a teammate shrugs: "It has always been like that. It takes five minutes."

This is how most gaps survive. They are not hidden. Everyone can see them. People just stop noticing, because they got used to the workaround. A **workaround** is a way of getting around a problem without fixing it.

New people often see gaps more clearly than old team members, because nothing is normal to them yet. That is one of the most useful things you bring when you join a team.

## Where to look

Gaps usually show up as small, repeated signals. Here are the most common ones.

| Signal | Example |
|---|---|
| The same question again and again | New students ask how to fix the same install error |
| Manual work that repeats | Someone copies data between two sheets every Friday |
| Something that broke more than once | The demo server runs out of space every month |
| A task nobody owns | Shared inbox emails wait days, because each person thinks someone else replies |
| A handoff where things get lost | Bugs reported by the client in WhatsApp never reach the task board |
| An outdated document | The README describes a setup the team stopped using last year |

A good habit: when you feel a small annoyance, ask "Is this the first time, or does it keep happening?" A one-time problem is bad luck. A repeated problem is a gap.

## Check if it matters

Not every gap deserves your time. Some are small. Some are already being fixed. Some are not gaps at all: the team chose that way on purpose. Before you raise anything, run three quick checks.

1. **Who does it affect, and how often?** One person once a year is small. Every new student every week is not.
2. **What does it cost?** Count it if you can: hours lost, money, missed deadlines, unhappy clients or users who give up.
3. **Is there a reason it is this way?** Ask before you assume. Maybe a client asked for it, or a fix was tried and failed, or someone is already working on it.

The third check matters most when you are new. The writer G. K. Chesterton made this point with a story about a fence across a road: before you remove a fence, find out why someone built it. A short question such as "Is there a reason we do it this way?" saves you from proposing a fix that breaks something you could not see.

<Callout type="tip" title="Write it down with numbers">
"The report fails sometimes" is easy to ignore. "The Monday report failed 4 out of the last 5 weeks, and each time someone spent 20 minutes fixing it" is hard to ignore. Numbers turn an opinion into a fact.
</Callout>

Back to Tariq. He checks the logs and finds the report failed on 9 of the last 10 Mondays. Each time, a teammate in another time zone fixed it, and twice the client noticed the delay. That is no longer "five minutes". It is a real gap, with a real cost.

  - "Mixing an unrelated fix into your pull request hides it from review, and someone else may own that code or already be fixing it."

  - "Signup failing affects real users and the client's business. Noticing it and staying silent is not ownership."
  - "Raising it is good, but a loud public post before you have checked the facts can cause panic and embarrass the people who own that code."
