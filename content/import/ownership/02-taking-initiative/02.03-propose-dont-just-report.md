## Two messages about the same problem

Hana is a junior front-end developer on a remote team. She has noticed that bug reports from the client arrive in three places: email, WhatsApp and the task board. Some reports get lost. Last week the client was upset because a bug they reported twice was never fixed.

Before you see what she writes, think about this.

Here are two messages Hana could send to her team lead, Daniel.

**Message A: a report**

> Hi Daniel, client bug reports are a mess. Things keep getting lost.

**Message B: a proposal**

> Hi Daniel, client bug reports come in through email, WhatsApp and the board. In the last month, 3 of 14 reports got lost, including the one the client complained about on Monday.
>
> Suggestion: we ask the client to send all bugs through one short form that creates a card on the board. I can set up the form in about two hours and test it with one bug.
>
> Could you approve this? And will you tell the client, or should I draft the message for you?

Message A is true, but it hands Daniel a new job: find out what is wrong, how bad it is, and what to do. He is busy, so it may wait for days. Message B does most of that work for him. He only has to decide.

## The five parts of a proposal

A good proposal is short. It answers five questions.

| Part | Question it answers | In Hana's message |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem** | What did you see? | Reports come in through three places |
| **Impact** | Why does it matter? | 3 of 14 lost, one angry client |
| **Proposed step** | What do you suggest first? | One form that creates a board card |
| **Effort** | What will it take? | About two hours of Hana's time |
| **Ask** | What do you need from them? | Approval, and who tells the client |

Notice that Hana proposes a **first step**, not a whole new system. Small steps are easier to say yes to, cheaper to test and easier to undo if they do not work. If the form helps, she can suggest the next step later.

<Callout type="tip" title="Bring options when you are unsure">
If you see two good fixes, name both in one line each and say which one you would choose and why. You still make the decision easy, without pretending you are certain.
</Callout>

## Act on your own, or ask first?

Initiative works best **within your role**: inside the work and decisions your team expects you to own. Some things you can simply do and then tell people. Others need a yes first.

| You can usually act, then tell | Ask first |
|---|---|
| Fixing a typo or broken link in your team's docs | Changing how the whole team works |
| Adding a missing note to your own task | Anything that costs money |
| Writing a short guide for a step you just learned | Anything a client or user will see |
| Cleaning up your own code before review | Changing someone else's code or decision |
| Answering a question in your area | Anything hard to undo, or that uses access or data |

A simple test: **if this goes wrong, who is affected?** If only you, and it is easy to undo, act and tell. If other people, money, clients or data are involved, ask first. Asking first is not weakness. It is how you take initiative without breaking trust.

Some people worry that asking slows things down. In practice, a short proposal often gets a yes within a day. Acting without asking, and then having to undo it, costs far more time and trust.

## Common questions

**Q: What if my proposal is rejected?**
A: That is normal and useful. Ask what the reason is, so you learn what the team cares about. A clear proposal that gets a no still shows you think about the team, not only your tasks.

**Q: I am new. Who am I to suggest changes?**
A: New people often see gaps best. Start with a question ("Is there a reason we do it this way?"), keep the first step small, and offer to do the work. That respects the team and still adds value.

**Q: What if nobody answers my message?**
A: Wait a reasonable time, then follow up once, briefly. If it is urgent, say why in the first line. If it is not, bring it to your next private call with your lead, or to the next team meeting.
