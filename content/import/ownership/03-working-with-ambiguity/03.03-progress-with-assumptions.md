## Waiting for Toronto

Jana is a freelance developer. Her client, Michael, runs a small language school in Toronto, seven hours behind her. He wants "a sign-up page for new students" within five working days.

On Monday at 10:00 her time, Jana sends him four clear questions, each with her best guess. It is 3:00 in the morning in Toronto. The earliest reply will come late in her evening, or tomorrow if he is busy. Some answers may take two days.

Five days is not much. Jana has a choice to make today.

## Silent guesses and stated assumptions

Everyone makes assumptions. The difference is whether other people can see them.

| Silent guess | Stated assumption |
|---|---|
| Lives only in your head | Written down and shared |
| The client finds out at the end | The client can correct it today |
| A wrong guess wastes days | A wrong guess costs a small change |
| Feels safe, but hides risk | Feels a little exposed, but builds trust |

A stated assumption is a kind of promise: "This is what I am working from. Tell me if it is wrong." It turns a hidden risk into a visible one.

## Step 1: write your assumptions down

Make a short list. For each assumption, note why you think it is true and what would change if it is wrong. Here is part of Jana's list:

| Assumption | Why I think so | If it is wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Students choose one course per sign-up | The paper form has one course box | Small change to the form |
| Payment stays in person, not online | No payment was mentioned | Big change: about two extra days |
| The page is in English only | The current site is English only | Medium change: add a language later |
| Michael gets an email for each sign-up | That is how the paper forms reach him now | Small change to the settings |

The last column is the most useful. It shows which assumptions are **risky**: the ones that would cost a lot if they are wrong. Those are the ones to confirm first.

## Step 2: share them, with a date

Send the list to the client in a short message. Say clearly what you will do unless you hear otherwise, and by when. Point to the riskiest assumption so it does not get lost.

> Hi Michael, while I wait for your answers, I am starting the sign-up page with these assumptions:
> 1. Students choose one course per sign-up.
> 2. Payment stays in person, so no online payment for now.
> 3. English only for the first version.
> 4. You get an email for each new sign-up.
>
> If any of these is wrong, please tell me by Wednesday and I will adjust. Number 2 matters most: online payment would add about two days.

This message does two things. It keeps the work moving, and it gives Michael a clear, easy way to stay in control.

## Step 3: keep moving on the safe parts

While you wait, work on what stays true **whatever** the answers are. Jana can build the page layout, the basic form fields and the mobile view today. All of that is needed in every version.

Leave the risky parts for later. Jana should not build an online payment system until Michael confirms he wants one. A good rule: do the work that is **easy to change** first, and wait on the work that is hard to undo.

## When the answers arrive

Update your list, adjust the work and tell the client what changed. If an assumption was wrong, that is not a failure. It is the system working: you found out early, and you lost a few hours, not a week.

<Callout type="warning" title="When not to assume">
Some guesses are too costly to make. Do not act on an assumption when a wrong guess could lose money, delete data, expose private information, break a law or a contract, or hurt someone's reputation. In those cases, wait for a clear answer. If the client is silent and the deadline is close, ask your team lead or another person who can decide.
</Callout>

## Common questions

**Q: What if the client never answers my assumptions?**
A: Follow up once, briefly, before the date you gave. If there is still no reply, go ahead with the low-risk assumptions and say so in your next update. For risky ones, see the warning above.

**Q: Is it not safer to just wait?**
A: Waiting feels safe, but it has a cost too: lost days and a client who sees no progress. Stated assumptions let you move without hiding risk.
