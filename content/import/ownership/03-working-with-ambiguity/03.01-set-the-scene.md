## A message on Sunday morning

Huda is a freelance web developer. She builds websites for small businesses. On Sunday morning she gets a message from a new client, Emre, who runs a small travel agency in Istanbul:

> Hi Huda, our booking process is a mess. Can you fix it? We would like something better before the summer. Thanks, Emre

Huda reads it three times. She wants the work, and she wants to do it well. But she does not know what to build.

- What is "a mess"? Lost bookings, angry customers, or too much work for the staff?
- "Fix it" for whom: the customers who book trips, or the staff who handle the bookings?
- What does "better" mean? Faster, easier, or fewer mistakes?
- When is "before the summer"? May or late June?

None of this is in the message. That is not because Emre is careless. It is because he is busy, and he knows his problem much better than he knows the solution.

## Two common reactions

When a task is unclear, most people do one of two things.

**They guess and start building.** Huda could build a new online booking form, because that is what she imagines. Two weeks later, Emre might say: "Nice, but the real problem was that my staff keep double-booking hotel rooms." The work is wasted, and the trust is damaged.

**They wait for perfect instructions.** Huda could reply with twenty questions and do nothing until all of them are answered. Emre is busy, so the answers come slowly. A week passes with no progress, and Emre starts to wonder if he chose the right person.

Both reactions feel safe. Neither one owns the result.

## Why unclear tasks are so common

Unclear assignments are normal, not a sign of a bad client or a bad manager. Here are some reasons:

- **People know the problem, not the solution.** Emre feels the pain every day, but he does not know what a developer could build.
- **People do not know what they have not thought about.** They may never have thought about questions like "Who will use this on a phone?"
- **Remote work hides details.** In an office you could walk over and ask. With a client three time zones away, one short message is often all you get.
- **Busy people write short messages.** A few words feel clear to the writer, because the writer already knows the context.

## Owning an unclear task

In module 1 you learned to own the outcome, not just the task. With an unclear task, owning the outcome means **making it clear**. That is part of your job, not only the client's job.

The good news: you do not need perfect information to start. You need the right few questions, a written list of what you are assuming, and the courage to keep moving.

<Callout type="info" title="This is a skill, not a talent">
Some people seem naturally good at unclear tasks. Usually they just follow a simple routine: ask, write down guesses, share them and start. You will practice that routine in this module.
</Callout>
