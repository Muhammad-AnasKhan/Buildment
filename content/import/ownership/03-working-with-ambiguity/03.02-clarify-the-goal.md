## A week on the wrong dashboard

Fadi is a junior developer at a small startup. His manager, Elena, works from Berlin. On Monday she writes:

> Can you build a dashboard for the sales team? Something simple.

Fadi is excited. He spends the week building charts for everything he can find: sales per month, sales per country, the top products, a map of customers. On Friday he shows Elena.

She is polite, but disappointed. "This is impressive," she says. "But the team only needed one thing: a list of customers who have not paid yet, so they can call them."

Fadi worked hard for a week. Most of it was not needed. One good question on Monday would have saved it.

## Three questions that clarify most tasks

You do not need a long list. Most unclear assignments become clear with three questions.

**1. What does success look like?** Ask how everyone will know the work worked. A good answer is something you can see or count: "Staff stop double-booking rooms", "We get more sign-ups from the form", "The report is ready before the board meeting on the 15th."

**2. Who is it for?** Ask who will use the result, and who decides if it is good. These are not always the same person. Emre may decide, but his staff and his customers will use the booking system every day.

**3. What is out of scope?** Ask what you should **not** do this time. This protects you and the client. It stops the work from growing quietly, and it makes "finished" easy to agree on.

Behind all three is one more question: **why?** Why does the client want this now? When you understand the reason, you can sometimes suggest a simpler way to reach the same goal. That is ownership in action.

## Turn vague words into questions

Some words sound clear but mean different things to different people. When you see one, turn it into a question.

| Vague word | A question that makes it clear |
|---|---|
| "better" | Better for whom, and how will we know it improved? |
| "simple" | What is the one thing it must do well? |
| "soon" | Which date would cause a real problem if we missed it? |
| "fix it" | What happens now, and what should happen instead? |
| "modern" | Can you show me one example you like? |

## Ask well, especially across time zones

How you ask matters as much as what you ask. With a remote client, each round of questions can cost a full day. So:

- **Ask once, in one message.** Number your questions so they are easy to answer.
- **Keep it short.** Three to five questions. Ask only the ones whose answer would change your work.
- **Add your best guess.** Then the client can just reply "yes" or correct one line.
- **Say what you will do meanwhile.** It shows you are not waiting with nothing to do.

Here is how Fadi could have written to Elena on Monday:

> Hi Elena, before I start the sales dashboard, three quick questions so I build the right thing:
> 1. What should the team be able to do after looking at it? My guess: see which customers to follow up with this week.
> 2. Who will use it most? My guess: the four sales reps, on their laptops.
> 3. Is anything out of scope for now? My guess: no charts of past years and no export to Excel.
>
> If my guesses are right, a short "yes" is enough. Meanwhile I will connect to the sales data today.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not ask what you can find yourself">
Before you ask, check what you already have: earlier messages, the shared folder, the current website or app. Asking a client something that is in their own document looks careless. Asking something only they know looks professional.
</Callout>

## Play it back

When the answers arrive, write the goal back in two or three lines and ask for a quick yes. This is called **playing it back**: you repeat what you understood, so mistakes show up now instead of in two weeks.

> So the goal is a list of customers with unpaid invoices, for the sales reps. Done means they can see it each morning and sort it by amount. Not included this time: charts and exports. Is that right?

## Common questions

**Q: Will asking questions make me look like I do not know my job?**
A: Not when the questions are few, specific and include your guess. Clients worry more about people who never ask and then deliver the wrong thing.

**Q: What if the client says "you decide"?**
A: Then decide, write down what you decided and why, and share it. You will learn how to do that in the next chapter.
