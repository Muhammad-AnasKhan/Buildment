## Module 1 checklist

- [ ] I can name the four kinds of things an AI tool does not know: context, private data, local and recent facts, and what nobody wrote down
- [ ] I add the facts only I know before I ask, and I check what the AI could never know with people and official pages
- [ ] I let AI draft, search, explain and compare, and I keep the checking, the decision and the responsibility
- [ ] I can say what studies found about AI and learning, with their limits, and I ask for hints when I want to learn a skill
- [ ] I can explain every part of my project out loud, or I have marked the parts I cannot explain yet
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 1.2 to 1.5, and I submitted my task split below

## Learning log

1. **Your path:** Which of the three fellows from chapter 1.1 are you closest to today: Khaled, Rana or Yara? What is one thing you will do differently this week?
2. **Your missing facts:** Think of the last question you asked an AI tool. Which fact that only you knew did you leave out?
3. **Your calls:** Name one decision from the last month that you let a tool make for you. Should it have stayed with you?
4. **Your learning:** When did an AI answer last make you feel you had learned something? How could you test, without the AI, whether you really had?

## Split one task

This task brings the module together on one piece of real work. You take a task and split it three ways. First, what AI can help with: the drafting, searching, explaining and comparing. Second, what the AI does not know about this task, under the four kinds from chapter 1.2. Third, the calls that stay yours, with how you will check each one. The point is not to use as little AI as possible, or as much. The point is to see the line clearly before you start, so the fast parts go to the tool and the thinking parts stay with you.

Choose a task you will really do in the next few weeks, from your study, a project or paid work. A good task is concrete and has a person it is for: "a weekly homework page for my students' parents", not "learn web development". If you prefer not to use your own work, use the provided case below. Both are marked the same way.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional person. Reem, the teacher from chapters 1.2 and 1.3, wants a simple web page where her 32 students' parents can see each week's homework. Under AI help, she lists four things. The AI can draft the layout of the page and compare two free ways to publish it. It can draft the message that tells parents where the page is, and explain how to update it each week in a few minutes.

Under what the AI does not know, Reem writes one fact or more for each kind. Context: most parents will open the page on a phone, and term starts in three weeks. Private data: students' names and marks must never appear on the page, and she will not paste them into a chat. Local and recent: the school changed its rules this year on what teachers may post online, so she must check them with the head teacher. Nobody wrote it down: she asked two parents, who said many families share one phone, so the page must load fast on weak data.

Reem's calls are the strongest part. She will choose how to publish the page after testing both ways on an old phone. She will agree with the head teacher what may appear on it. She will check each week's homework against her own lesson plan before it goes up. She also writes one line about who answers: "If a parent sees the wrong homework, I fix it the same day." Every call has a check next to it, and a person's name.

A weak answer to the same task would read: "AI can help with everything. AI does not know my class. I will check the results." It may be true, but it names no drafts, no missing facts and no checks. It would not change a single thing Reem does, and a mentor cannot tell from it where the line between the tool and the person sits.

If you prefer not to use your own work, use this provided case. Majd, from chapter 1.2, has been asked by a small NGO that runs free coding workshops for teenagers to build a donation tracker. Here is what he knows. About 40 regular donors give each month: some in dollars online, some in shekels in cash at the office. Today the office manager writes every donation in a paper notebook. The NGO wants to record each donation, see the monthly total, and send each donor a thank-you message. The budget is $250 with nothing to pay each month. Two volunteers will use it on one shared laptop at the office. The deadline is in six weeks. Split Majd's task three ways, as Reem did.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer with the table below. Two careful people can split the same task differently, and that is fine. What matters is that every item is specific to the task, and that every call you keep has a check.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI help | Three or more specific drafts, searches, explanations or comparisons | One or two specific items | No specific item, or "AI can do everything" |
| What the AI does not know | A specific fact for each of the four kinds | Specific facts for two or three of the four kinds | Specific facts for one kind or none |
| Calls that stay yours | Three or more calls, each with how it will be checked | One or two calls with a check | No call with a check, or the AI makes the main decision |
| Who answers | Both: one sentence on who answers, and no private data given to the AI | One of the two | Neither |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet", at least three parts are "Met", and your reply to the mentor's follow-up question answers it with evidence.

After you submit, the mentor replies with one or two short questions about your split. For example, they may ask which missing fact came from a person rather than a page, or how exactly you will check one call. A good reply points to evidence: the person you asked, the page you read, or the test you will run. You can dictate your answer with your phone's microphone, and you may add a voice note link as well.
