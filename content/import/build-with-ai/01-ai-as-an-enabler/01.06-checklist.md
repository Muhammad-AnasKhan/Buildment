## Module 1 checklist

- [ ] I can name the four kinds of things an AI tool does not know about a task: context, private data, local and recent facts, and what nobody wrote down
- [ ] I add the facts only I know before I ask, and I check what the AI could never know with people and official pages
- [ ] I let AI draft, search, explain and compare, and I keep the checking, the decision and the responsibility
- [ ] I can say what studies found about AI and learning, with their limits, and I ask for hints when I want to learn a skill
- [ ] I can explain every part of my project out loud, or I have marked the parts I cannot explain yet
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 1.2 to 1.5, and I submitted my task split below

## Learning log

1. **Your path:** Which of the three fellows from chapter 1.1 are you closest to today: Khaled, Rana or Yara? What is one thing you will do differently this week?
2. **Your missing facts:** Think of the last question you asked an AI tool. Which fact that only you knew did you leave out?
3. **Your calls:** Name one decision from the last month that you let a tool make for you. Should it have stayed with you?
4. **Your learning:** When did an AI answer last make you feel you had learned something? How could you test, without the AI, whether you really had?

## Split one task

This task brings the module together on one piece of real work. You take a task and split it three ways. First, what AI can help with: the drafting, searching, explaining and comparing. Second, what the AI does not know about this task, under the four kinds from chapter 1.2. Third, the calls that stay yours, with how you will check each one. The point is not to use as little AI as possible, or as much. The point is to see the line clearly before you start, so the fast parts go to the tool and the thinking parts stay with you.

Choose a task you will really do in the next few weeks, from your study, a project or paid work. A good task is concrete and has a person it is for: "build a sign-up form for the club's summer camp", not "learn web development". If you prefer not to use your own work, use the provided case below. Both are marked the same way.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional person. Dina, from chapter 1.3, must build a sign-up form for the sports club's summer camp, which has 60 places. Under AI help, she lists four things. The AI can draft the questions on the form and compare two free form tools. It can draft the message that confirms a place, and explain how to close the form after 60 sign-ups. Under what the AI does not know, she writes one fact or more for each kind. Context: most parents will fill it in on a phone, and the deadline is in three weeks. Private data: children's names and ages will be collected, so she will not paste any answers into a chat. Local and recent: the camp fee was raised this year to 120 shekels. Nobody wrote it down: she asked the coaches, who said brothers and sisters must be placed in the same group.

Dina's calls are the strongest part. She will decide which form tool to use after testing both on her own phone. She will decide with the club manager who can see the children's details. She will check the confirmation message against the real camp dates before it goes out. She also writes one line about who answers. It says: "If a family is wrongly told they have a place, I answer the call and fix it." Every call has a check next to it, and a person's name.

Compare a weak answer to the same task: "AI can help with everything. AI does not know my club. I will check the results." It is not wrong, but it shows nothing. It names no drafts, no missing facts and no checks, so it would not change a single thing Dina does. A mentor cannot tell from it whether the line between the tool and the person is clear.

If you prefer not to use your own work, use this provided case. Majd, from chapter 1.2, has been asked by a small NGO that runs free coding workshops for teenagers to build a donation tracker. Here is what he knows. About 40 regular donors give each month: some in dollars online, some in shekels in cash at the office. Today the office manager writes every donation in a paper notebook. The NGO wants to record each donation, see the monthly total, and send each donor a thank-you message. The budget is $250 with nothing to pay each month. Two volunteers will use it on one shared laptop at the office. The deadline is in six weeks. Split Majd's task three ways, as Dina did.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer with the table below. Two careful people can split the same task differently, and that is fine. What matters is that every item is specific to the task, and that every call you keep has a check.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI help | Three or more specific drafts, searches, explanations or comparisons | One or two, or only general ones such as "help with the code" | None, or "AI can do everything" |
| What the AI does not know | At least one specific fact under each of the four kinds | Facts under two or three kinds | No specific facts |
| Calls that stay yours | Three or more calls, each with how you will check it | Calls listed without checks | No calls, or the AI makes the main decision |
| Who answers | One sentence naming who answers if it goes wrong, and private data kept out of the AI | One of the two | Neither |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "Which of your missing facts did you get from a person rather than a page?" or "How exactly will you check that call before it reaches anyone?" You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
