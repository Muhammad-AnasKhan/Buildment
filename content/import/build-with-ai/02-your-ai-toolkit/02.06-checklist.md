## Module 2 checklist

- [ ] I check what I paste with the stranger test, and I never paste a password, a key or someone else's data without permission
- [ ] I found the training setting in each AI tool I use, and I wrote down the date I checked it
- [ ] I check AI output before I use it: I run code on a copy, check commands in the official docs and open links
- [ ] I choose a tool by its job, with two options for each, and check that it opens here, at my age, on my device
- [ ] I keep my work in files I own, with a second copy, saved prompts and a fallback for every job
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 2.2 to 2.4, and finished the quiz in 2.5

## Learning log

1. **Your last paste:** Think of the last thing you pasted into an AI tool. If nothing comes to mind, use Mahmoud's customer list. Would it pass the stranger test? What would you change before pasting it now?
2. **One tool for everything:** Which job do you most often give to a tool that was not built for it? Which kind of tool fits that job better?
3. **If a tool closed tomorrow:** Where does your study work live today: in chats, in files, or both? What exactly would you lose?
4. **The check you leave out:** Of the three output checks (run it, read the official docs, open the link), which one do you leave out most often, and why?

## Your toolkit page

This task turns the whole module into one page you will keep using. It lists the six jobs from chapter 2.3, the tools you will use for each, and the date you checked them. It also says where your work is saved, and the rules you follow before you paste. You will come back to it at the start of each term and change the dates. Later modules assume you have it, because modules 3 to 6 ask you to pick a tool for a job without explaining the choice again.

The checks must be your own, done on the day you write the page. Open each tool's own pages: its supported places, its terms for age, its plans or help page for free access, and its settings for the training setting. Do not copy the facts from this module. They were checked on 2026-10-24, and some may already be out of date when you read this. A page with today's date and your own answers is worth more than a long page copied from a video.

Here is what a strong page looks like, told about a fictional person. Aseel is a freelance translator who is learning web development in the evenings. For "learn from my sources", she lists a source notebook opened in her phone's browser, and a chat assistant with the text pasted in. Next to each, she writes three short answers: opens here, allowed at her age, works in her phone's browser. She adds the date and what the free tier includes, from the tool's own plans page. For "run a lab", she writes that her laptop is too weak for Docker Desktop, so she uses a computer in the browser and checks its usage page weekly. Her fallback is the provided lab log in module 4.

Aseel's page then says where her work lives. Her notes are plain Markdown files on her phone, uploaded every Sunday to a private GitHub repository, with a page called "prompts that worked". Her privacy rules are three lines in her own words. "I never paste passwords, keys or client files. Before I paste anything else, I ask if a stranger could read it. I found the training setting in both chat assistants I use and switched it off on 2026-10-20." Every line can be checked, and every line has a date or a reason.

Compare a weak page for the same person: "Tools: one chat assistant for everything. Notes: in my chats. Privacy: I am careful." It names one tool for six jobs, so the first change to that tool stops all her work. It keeps her notes in the place Ahmad lost his. And "I am careful" is a feeling, not a rule. Nobody, including Aseel, could check whether she followed it.

If you prefer not to use your own situation, use this provided case. Hiba is 19 and in her second year of an IT degree at a university. In the evenings she shares a phone with her sister, and her phone data runs low by the end of each month. Two afternoons a week she can use the university's computer lab, where students cannot install programs but can use a browser. This term she is studying computer networks from her lecturer's PDF slides, and she will start daily labs next month. She also helps her uncle with the accounts sheet of his small shop, which lists his suppliers and what each one is owed. She has no notes system yet, and no GitHub account. Write Hiba's toolkit page, doing the tool checks yourself today.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your page with the table below. Two careful fellows can choose different tools for the same job, and both can be right. What matters is that each choice comes from the job and from your own check, with a date.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jobs and options | All six jobs, each with two options (two tools, or a tool and a way to do it by hand) | Four or five jobs, or only one option for some jobs | Fewer than four jobs, or one tool for everything |
| Checked and dated | Every tool has the three answers, its free access from its own page, and a date | Dates are there, but some answers are missing or came from a video or a friend | No dates |
| Where work is saved | A place you own, a second copy somewhere else, and where saved prompts live | One place you own, with no second copy | Chat history only, or not stated |
| Privacy rules | Three rules in your own words, and the training setting found for each AI tool, with a date | Rules are there, but no training setting was checked | No rules, or only "I am careful" |

**Pass rule:** your page passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "Which of your tools would you lose first if its free tier changed, and what would you do that day?" or "How would you check that the setting you switched off is still off next term?" You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
