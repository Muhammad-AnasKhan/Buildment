This is the gate for module 4. It includes running your lab day 1, so plan about 50 minutes. Tick each box only when it is true. Your answers save as you type.

## Module 4 checklist

- [ ] I can plan a lab around one question, in four parts, that fits about 30 to 45 minutes
- [ ] I give an AI chat my goal, time, level, tools and what to break, then cut its plan to my time
- [ ] I check every command in the official docs, and every package on the official package site, before I use it
- [ ] I write my prediction before I break one part on purpose, and I explain what I saw
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 4.2 to 4.5, and finished the quiz in 4.6
- [ ] I submitted my lab day 1, or my review of the provided log, below

## Learning log

1. **Your size:** Think of your last lab or study evening. If you have not run a lab yet, use Yara's first try at lab day 2. Was it one question? What would have made it small enough to finish?
2. **Your AI plan:** When an AI chat gives you a lab plan, which part will you check first, and where will you check it?
3. **Your guess:** In your lab, or in Shahd's day 3 below, what was the prediction, and what would have surprised you?
4. **Your check:** Which of the four questions before a push are you most likely to forget on a tired evening? How will you remember it?

## Your lab day 1

This task starts the lab log you will keep until the capstone. The capstone in module 6 asks for at least ten lab days over at least two weeks. This is day 1. It does not need to be impressive. It needs to be real, small, honest and safe to make public.

Choose one question from your own study or project. If you have no project yet, pick a topic you are studying and something you can run: a small web server, a database table, a page that saves a form. Plan the lab with an AI chat, or by hand from the five facts in chapter 4.3. Run it, break one part on purpose with a written prediction, write the entry in five parts, check it, and push it to a GitHub repository. If you have no strong laptop, use GitHub Codespaces in the browser.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional fellow. Raghad studies software at a university and has 30 minutes in the library for each lab. Her project is a small notice board for her student society. Her lab day 1 question was: "Can I run the notice board with its database, and add one notice?" A chat assistant drafted a 60-minute plan. She cut it to study 5, build 15, break 5 and write 5: 30 minutes. She checked the plan's two commands in Docker's docs. The plan also suggested a package for showing dates. She found it on the official package site: real, but with no release in six years. She left it out.

Her break was to stop only the database. She predicted that the page would load and that adding a notice would fail. Adding a notice did fail, but the page with the list of notices failed too. Her entry explains why her guess was half wrong: her app has no cache, so every page reads the database. Before her push, git status showed a .env file. She listed it in .gitignore and checked again. Her commit message reads "Day 1: run the notice board, stop the database, every page fails".

Look at the shape of Raghad's answer. One question, sized to her real time. A plan she cut and checked, with the place of each check. A prediction written first, and a wrong half she explains instead of hiding. A log that is safe to make public. Her mentor will probably ask about the wrong half first, because it shows what she now understands that she did not before.

This gate gives you less help than the earlier ones, on purpose: you now have the habits. There is no weak answer to compare with this time, so test your own answer against the shape of Raghad's. For each part, ask whether a mentor could see the evidence in what you wrote. Raghad's cut shows that she knows her real time. Her two checked commands show that she did not trust the plan only because it looked complete. Her wrong half shows that she looked at the result instead of defending her guess, and that is the part a mentor learns the most from.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer with one of the two tables below, depending on the route you chose. Your own lab can be about any topic, and it can go wrong in any way. What counts is whether the habits are there.

**Route 1: your own lab day 1.**

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small enough to finish | One question and all four parts, in 45 minutes or less | All four parts, but no single question, or over 45 minutes | Fewer than three of the four parts |
| Plan checked | What was cut, and where every command and package was checked | At least one check named, but not every one | No check named |
| Break with a prediction | All three: prediction written first, the result, and the reason | Two of those three | Nothing broken on purpose |
| Entry and safety | All five entry parts, a message that says what changed, nothing secret or personal | Four of the five parts, or a vague message, and nothing secret or personal | A password, key or someone's personal data in the log |

**Pass rule for route 1:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met", one of them "Entry and safety". Your reply to the mentor's follow-up question must also answer it with evidence.

**Route 2: Shahd's provided log.**

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problems found | All four problems, days 2 to 5, found | Three of the four found | Two or fewer found |
| Habit named | The habit named for every problem you found | The habit named for all but one | The habit named for fewer |
| Fix works | Every fix would work, and day 4 starts by changing the password | Three of four fixes work, and day 4 starts by changing the password | The day 4 fix does not change the password |
| Day 3 rewritten | All five parts, with a prediction written as if before the break | Four of the five parts, or no prediction | Not rewritten |

**Pass rule for route 2:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met". Your reply to the mentor's follow-up question must also answer it with evidence. The password on day 4 is a must: an answer that does not change it cannot pass.

After you submit, the mentor replies with one or two short questions. For example: "What did you predict, and what would have surprised you?" or "Where did you check that command?" A good reply points to something you can show: a line in your entry, a page you checked, a result you saw. You can dictate your answer with your phone's microphone, and you may add a voice note link as well.
