## Module 4 checklist

- [ ] I can plan a lab around one question, in four parts, that fits about 30 to 45 minutes
- [ ] I give an AI chat my goal, time, level, tools and what to break, then cut its plan to my time
- [ ] I check every command against the official docs, and every package on the official package site, before I run or install it
- [ ] I write my prediction before I break one part on purpose, and I explain what I saw
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 4.2 to 4.5, and finished the quiz in 4.6
- [ ] I submitted my lab day 1, or my review of the provided log, below

## Learning log

1. **Your size:** Think of your last lab or study evening. If you have not run a lab yet, use Yara's lab day 2. Was it one question? What would have made it small enough to finish?
2. **Your AI plan:** When an AI chat gives you a lab plan, which part will you check first, and where will you check it?
3. **Your guess:** In your lab, or in Shahd's day 3 below, what was the prediction, and what would have surprised you?
4. **Your check:** Which of the four questions before a push are you most likely to forget on a tired evening? How will you remember it?

## Your lab day 1

This task starts the lab log you will keep until the capstone. The capstone in module 6 asks for at least ten lab days over at least two weeks. This is day 1. It does not need to be impressive. It needs to be real, small, honest and safe to make public.

Choose one question from your own study or project. If you have no project yet, pick a topic you are studying and something you can run: a small web server, a database table, a page that saves a form. Plan the lab with an AI chat, or by hand from the five facts in chapter 4.3. Run it, break one part on purpose with a written prediction, write the entry in five parts, check it, and push it to a GitHub repository. If you have no strong laptop, use GitHub Codespaces in the browser.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional fellow. Raghad studies software at a university and has 30 minutes in the library for each lab. Her project is a small notice board for her student society. Her lab day 1 question was: "Can I run the notice board with its database, and add one notice?" A chat assistant drafted a 60-minute plan. She cut it to study 5, build 15, break 5 and write 5: 30 minutes. She checked the plan's two commands in Docker's docs. The plan also suggested a package for showing dates. She found it on the official package site: real, but with no release in six years. She left it out.

Her break was to stop only the database. She predicted that the page would load and that adding a notice would fail. Adding a notice did fail, but the page with the list of notices failed too. Her entry explains why her guess was half wrong: her app has no cache, so every page reads the database. Before her push, git status showed a .env file. She listed it in .gitignore and checked again. Her commit message reads "Day 1: run the notice board, stop the database, every page fails".

Look at the shape of Raghad's answer. One question, sized to her real time. A plan she cut and checked, with the place of each check. A prediction written first, and a wrong half she explains instead of hiding. A log that is safe to make public. The wrong half is not a weakness. It is the most useful line in her entry.

Compare it with a weak answer: "I did a Docker lab today. It worked and I learned a lot. AI helped me. Here is my link." It has no question and no time. Nothing was broken, so nothing was predicted or explained. "AI helped me" does not say how, or what was checked. A mentor cannot tell what this fellow can now explain, and neither can the fellow.

If you only have a phone, or you prefer not to use your own work, review this provided log instead. Shahd volunteers at a sports club with two football pitches. She is learning how a booking app works, so that she can later build one for the club. Her app runs in two containers, an app and a database. Here are her first five lab days, as she pushed them to a public repository.

| Day | Time | Commit message | What the entry says |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 40 minutes | Day 1: run the booking app and its database | All five parts. Broke: asked for a pitch that does not exist. AI line: who drafted the plan, and what she checked. |

Day 1 is a good entry, so use it as a model. For each of days 2 to 5, name the problem, the habit from this module it breaks, and the fix. Then rewrite day 3 as a full entry in five parts, with a prediction Shahd could have written first. Finally, say what Shahd must do first about day 4, and why deleting the file is not enough.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer with the table below. Your lab can be about any topic, and it can go wrong in any way. What counts is whether the habits are there. For Shahd's log, read each row as: did you find the problem in her log for this part, name the habit, and give a fix that would work?

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small enough to finish | One question, four parts, about 30 to 45 minutes | Four parts, but no single question, or far over the time | No question and no plan |
| Plan checked | Says what was cut and where each command and package was checked | Some checks, without saying where | An AI plan run or installed as given |
| Break with a prediction | Prediction written first, one part changed, result and reason | A result with no prediction, or no reason | Nothing broken on purpose |
| Entry and safety | Five parts, a clear commit message, nothing secret or personal | One part missing, or a vague message | A password, key or someone's personal data in the log |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet", at least three parts are "Met", and "Entry and safety" is one of them.

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "What did you predict, and what would have surprised you?" or "Where did you check that command?" You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
