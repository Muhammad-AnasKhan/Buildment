## A weekend, according to the chat

In her first week, before she had built anything, Yara asked a chat assistant, such as Gemini or ChatGPT, a simple question: "How long does it take to build a link shortener?" The answer came back in a few seconds. "With modern AI tools, a basic link shortener can be built in a weekend."

The answer was about one thing only: how long it takes to make the code run. A first version that turns a long link into a short one may well run after a weekend, as Khaled's store did in chapter 5.1. The answer said nothing about how long it takes to understand the code well enough to explain it, change it and fix it when it breaks. The chat could not know that Yara wanted a project she could defend in an interview. She had not told it.

So that same week, Yara did what this chapter teaches. She stopped asking how fast the project could be built. She started counting how many hours she really had, and what they had to cover.

## Hours, not feelings

A plan starts with one multiplication: hours a week times weeks. Yara has about 8 hours a week for her project, on top of her course time. This course counts 4 weeks in a month, to keep the arithmetic simple. So one month gives her 8 times 4, which is 32 hours. Six months give her 32 times 6, which is 192 hours.

Those 192 hours are the whole budget. Everything Qasir needs has to fit inside them: reading, watching, labs, building, testing, fixing, notes and write-ups. A weekend of focused work might be 16 hours. That is enough to make something run, and far too little to learn 12 areas.

## Yara's guess: learning takes most of the time

Yara listed the 12 areas Qasir needs in chapter 5.1. For each one, her plan makes two guesses. Learning an area well will take her about 8 hours: studying it from several angles, running small labs, breaking things on purpose and writing notes. Building that area into Qasir with AI help will take her about 4 hours. These are her own planning numbers, not a measured rule. Your areas will need more or less.

Why does she give learning twice the time? Yara expects AI to shorten building more than learning. That is why her plan gives building 4 hours an area and learning 8. Her reasoning is simple. A coding assistant can type a database function in seconds, but she still has to understand that function. A chat assistant can explain caching clearly. You met the problem with that in chapter 3.2: a clear explanation feels like knowing, but you only know it when you can recall it and use it.

This is her assumption, and she treats it as one. Her hours log will test it, as you will see later in this chapter.

## Worked example: does six months fit?

Here is Yara's arithmetic, step by step, so you can repeat it with your own numbers.

First, the learning. Twelve areas at 8 hours each is 96 hours. Second, the building. Twelve areas at 4 hours each is 48 hours. Together, the areas need 144 hours.

Third, compare with the budget. Six months give her 192 hours, which is 48 hours more than the areas need. Those 48 hours are not free time. Yara splits them. She gives 19 hours to spaced review of earlier areas and to reading open-source code, which is learning too. She gives the other 29 hours to joining the parts together, writing up her progress, and the weeks that go wrong because of exams, work or a power cut. A plan with no spare hours breaks the first time a week goes badly.

Now add it up. Learning is 96 plus 19, which is 115 hours. Building and the rest is 48 plus 29, which is 77 hours. And 115 plus 77 is 192, the whole budget. So Yara plans to spend about 60% of her hours learning, because 115 is about 60% of 192.

This is where "about six months" comes from in this course. It is the course sponsor's planning rule, not a research finding: no study we know of measures how long a strong project takes. The rule exists because the arithmetic of learning comes out in months for most fellows. If your own numbers give a very different answer, such as four months or nine, show your mentor the arithmetic and plan with it.

## Feeling faster is not being faster

Plans fail in a second way, even with good arithmetic. People guess their own speed badly, and AI tools can make the guess worse. In one careful study, experienced developers felt faster with AI but were slower. Later tools may be better. The lesson is to measure, not to trust the feeling.

That is why Yara's guess about AI needs a test. She measures with one line after every study session: the time she spent learning and the time she spent building. It takes ten seconds. From week 5, when her lab log starts, that line goes at the end of every lab log entry, the log you met in module 4. The diagram shows what she does with those lines.

```mermaid
flowchart TD
  A["Guess: 8 hours learn, 4 build"] --> B["Plan the months"]
  B --> C["Log real hours after each lab"]
  C --> D{"Four weeks later: guess held?"}
  D -->|"yes"| B
  D -->|"no, twice"| E["More months, or fewer areas"]
  E --> B
```

Notice where the arrows go back. The plan is never final: each month it is checked against the log, and the guess at the top is the thing being tested. In this example, her first area, the API, took her 10 hours of learning instead of 8. One area is not a pattern, so she does not change the plan yet. If the next areas also run over, she will choose between two honest fixes: more months, or fewer areas. Both are better than pretending.

<Callout type="info" title="Your numbers, not Yara's">
Yara's 8 learning hours and 4 building hours are her guesses for her areas. Make your own guesses, write them in your plan, then let your hours log correct them after a few weeks.
</Callout>

## A habit takes time too

A plan of 8 hours a week only works if the hours really happen, week after week, around study, work and family. That is easier when the hours become a habit: the same short lab at the same time of day. A daily habit can take weeks to months to feel automatic, and people differ a lot. So expect the first weeks of daily labs to feel like effort. That feeling is normal, and it does not mean the plan is wrong.

## Where it goes wrong

The first mistake is the chat's mistake: counting only building time. Any plan that says "two hours an area, because AI writes the code" has quietly dropped the learning. Ask of every plan, including one an AI writes for you: where are the learning hours?

The second mistake is planning with your best week. If you wrote 8 hours a week because you managed 8 hours once, the plan will fall behind in the first month. Plan with an ordinary week, and keep spare hours, as Yara's 29 hours for joining, writing and bad weeks do.

The third mistake is trusting the feeling of speed over the log. If your log and your feeling disagree, believe the log, and change the plan.
