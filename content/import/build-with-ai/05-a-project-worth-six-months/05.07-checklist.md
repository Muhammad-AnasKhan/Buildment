## Learning log

1. **Your hours:** Before this module, how long did you think your project would take? What does the arithmetic of hours a week times weeks say now?
2. **Your speed:** Do you feel faster when you use AI tools? How will you measure it over the next four weeks instead of trusting the feeling?
3. **Your first check:** When an AI suggests a book, a design or a block of code, what is the first thing you now check, and how?
4. **Your explain-back:** Is there a part of something you built that you kept without being able to explain it? If nothing comes to mind, use Khaled's prices. What would you do with it now?

## Your month 1 plan

This task turns the module into the first month of a real plan. A six-month plan is only useful if its first month is concrete enough to start on Saturday. So you will write that month week by week, with the hours behind it, the resources for its first new area, and one open-source project you will study. The point is not a perfect plan. Plans change, and yours will. The point is a plan whose numbers add up and whose every resource you have checked.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about Yara. Her project sentence comes first: Qasir turns long links into short ones and counts clicks, for small shops and NGOs that share links in group chats. She has 8 hours a week, so six months give her 192 hours. Her 12 areas need about 8 hours of learning and 4 of building each, so 96 hours of learning and 48 of building, with 48 hours left for review, write-ups and bad weeks. Learning is two thirds of the work on her areas, and she says why: the AI can draft the code, but she has to understand it.

Her month 1 has a theme, "a link that works", and four weeks. Week 1 is the API, her first new area. Week 2 builds the API into Qasir with AI help and reviews what week 1 taught her. Week 3 is the database, her second new area. Week 4 joins the database to the API, reviews both, and ends with a short write-up. No week has two new areas.

For week 1, an AI suggested five resources. Yara opened the two videos and the two articles. One article link opened a page about something else, so she dropped it. The book she found in a library catalogue with the same title, author and year. Each line in her table says how she checked it. Her open-source project is shortlane, made up for this course: MIT licence, a commit three weeks ago, nine contributors. She will read its README, run it in GitHub Codespaces, and then read its API folder.

Look at the shape of her answer. Every number can be checked with a sum. Every week has one job. Every resource has a "how". The project has a licence and two health signs, and she knows where she will start reading.

Compare it with a weak answer to the same task: "Month 1: learn the backend. Resources: some good videos the AI gave me. Open source: I will look on GitHub." It sounds like a plan, but there are no hours to check, no weeks, no way to tell whether the videos exist, and no project, so no licence. A mentor cannot help with it, because there is nothing specific to discuss.

If you prefer not to use your own project, use this provided case. Rasha volunteers at an NGO office. She wants to build a volunteer sign-up app where volunteers choose shifts and get a reminder. She has 6 hours a week. Her app has 10 areas: a web form, a database, user accounts, shifts with a calendar view, email reminders, tests, containers, logs, deploy, and privacy for volunteers' personal data. Her own planning numbers say each area needs about 7 hours of learning and 3 of building.

A chat assistant wrote Rasha a draft month 1. Week 1: the web form, the database and user accounts. Week 2: shifts. Week 3: reminders. Week 4: deploy. For week 1, the AI suggested two videos, one book and one article. Rasha opened the first video: 14 minutes, posted last year. She has not opened the second video. She found no library or bookshop listing for the book. The article is on the official documentation site of her web framework, and it was updated this year.

Rasha also found two open-source projects. Project S is a volunteer shift app with 1,500 stars, no licence file, and a last commit five years ago. Project T is an event sign-up app under GPL version 3, with a commit last month, 12 contributors, and issues answered within a week. Use these facts to write her month 1.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer with the table below. Two careful people can plan the same month differently. If your weeks are not the ones your mentor would choose, that is fine, as long as the numbers add up and every resource has a check.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hours and learning share | Hours a week, the six-month total and the learning share, with the sum shown | A total or a share, but no sum | No hours |
| Four weeks | At most one new area a week, with weeks to build and review | Four weeks, but one week has two new areas | No weeks, only a list of topics |
| Checked resources | Each first-week resource says how it was checked; unchecked or fake ones are out | Some resources say how they were checked | No checks, or a fake resource kept |
| Open-source project | Named, licence stated, two health signs, where reading starts | Named with its licence, but no health signs | No project, or an unlicensed one chosen to reuse |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "If exams take one week of this month, which week moves, and what moves with it?" or "What does this project's licence let you do with its code?" You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
