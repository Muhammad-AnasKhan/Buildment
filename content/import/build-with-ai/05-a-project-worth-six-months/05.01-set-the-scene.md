## One question in an interview

You met Khaled in module 1. He is quick, curious and not afraid of new tools. One weekend he asked an AI coding assistant for an online store, with product pages, a cart, discount codes and a page for the owner, and by Sunday night it ran. In chapter 1.1 you saw the first crack: sorted by price, a product at 100 shekels came before one at 20, because every price was stored as text. Khaled fixed the order with one more instruction to the assistant. In chapter 1.5 a mentor asked him why prices were text in the first place, and he did not know. He meant to find out. He never did.

Three months later, Khaled had a job interview at a small software company that builds websites for shops. The interviewer was a senior developer. She asked him to share his screen and open the store's repository. She scrolled through the files for a minute, and then she stopped at the part that saves products.

"Your prices are stored as text," she said. "Why did you choose that?"

"The assistant set it up that way," Khaled said. "There was a problem with sorting, but I fixed it."

"How did you fix it?" she asked. Khaled opened the sorting code. The assistant had added a line that turns each price back into a number just before sorting. He could see that it worked. He could not say why the prices were not numbers in the first place, or where else the same problem might hide.

Then she asked a third question. "In some languages, adding two prices stored as text joins them instead of adding them. A price of 10 plus a price of 5 becomes 105. How does your cart avoid that? And if you changed prices to numbers, what would the change cost you?" Khaled tried to answer, but he was guessing, and they both knew it. The interview ended a few minutes later.

A week later the company sent a short message. It said the store worked and looked good, but that they "could not see how he thinks about his own code". They chose someone else.

## What the interviewer was really asking

The interviewer did not ask how many pages the store had. She did not ask how fast he built it. She asked about one choice, and then she asked what that choice gave and what it cost. That is a question about a trade-off.

Storing prices as text is not always wrong. Text is easy to show on a page, and it can keep a currency sign next to the number. But text sorts badly and adds badly. Storing prices as numbers sorts and adds correctly. Many shops store a price as a whole number in the smallest unit of money, such as agorot or cents, so rounding never adds or loses a little money. Each option gains something and gives something up. A developer who understands the store can say which one they chose, and why it fits this shop.

Khaled could not, because he never made the choice. The AI made it for him, and nobody weighed it. This is the most important point of the case. A trade-off is always made. If you do not make it, a tool makes it for you by default, and you cannot explain a decision you never saw.

It is also worth saying what Khaled did well. He finished something. He used a tool that saved him days of typing. He put his work where others could see it. None of that was a mistake. The mistake was keeping parts of a project he could not explain, and then presenting the whole project as his own understanding.

## Why this matters for your career

In module 1 you saw that building something fast is no longer rare. An AI coding assistant can help almost anyone produce a working app in a weekend. That means a working app, on its own, tells an interviewer very little. Hundreds of people can show one. What is still rare is a person who can open their own project and explain every part of it: why it is built this way, what breaks when one part stops, and what they would change with more time.

Mentors, clients and interviewers test this in the same way the interviewer tested Khaled. They pick one part and ask "why?". Then they ask "what if?". A fellow who built less, but understands all of it, answers both questions calmly. This is why module 1 introduced the explain-back rule: you keep a part of your project only if you can explain it without notes. This module turns that rule into a plan you can follow for months.

## Yara's slower path

Yara, the fellow you have followed since module 1, is building Qasir, a link shortener for small shops and NGOs that share links in group chats. Qasir turns a long link into a short one and counts how many people click it. On its own, that sounds like a weekend project too. An AI assistant could produce a first version in a few hours.

Yara chose a different plan. She listed the areas Qasir needs: an API, a database, short codes, caching, containers, tests, rate limits, click counts, a simple web page, logs, deploy, and safety against harmful links. That is 12 areas. She has about 8 hours a week for her project, on top of her course time. Her plan gives Qasir about six months, so she can learn each area well before she builds it into the project.

Be clear about where "six months" comes from. It is this course's planning rule, set by the course sponsor. No study shows that a strong project takes six months. The rule exists because learning takes time, and a plan that leaves no time for learning ends like Khaled's interview. In chapter 5.2 you will check the rule against Yara's own numbers, and see why a much shorter plan cannot fit.

## What a slower plan looks like

A six-month plan is not six months of waiting. Yara still uses AI tools every week. She asks a chat assistant what to read and watch, and to explain ideas she finds hard. She uses it to draft code, which she then reads line by line. She runs small daily labs, as in module 4. The difference is in what she keeps. Every part that goes into Qasir is a part she can explain, and every big choice is one she made on purpose.

The module follows her through four habits. First, she works out how many hours her plan really has, and how many of them go to learning. Second, she plans the weeks one new area at a time, and checks that every resource an AI suggests really exists. Third, she slows down at big decisions, compares two options with her own facts, and refuses to keep code she cannot explain. Fourth, she studies open-source projects to learn how others solved the same problems, and reuses code only when the licence allows it.

If Khaled had followed even the third habit, his interview would have gone differently. He would have read the line that stored prices as text. He would have asked why. He might have kept text, or changed it to numbers, but either way he would have had an answer.

## The case in one picture

The diagram shows Khaled's weekend and his interview as one chain. Read it from the top, and look for the step where a decision should have been made.

```mermaid
flowchart TD
  A["Friday: ask the AI for a store"] --> B["AI picks: prices as text"]
  B --> C["Sunday: the store works"]
  C --> D["Sort breaks: patched, not asked why"]
  D --> E["Interview: why text?"]
  E --> F["'The assistant set it up'"]
  F --> G["No trade-off he can explain"]
```

What to notice: the choice happened at the second box, on the first evening, and nobody looked at it. Every later box depends on it. The store working on Sunday did not fix the gap; it hid it. The broken sort was a second chance to look, and a patch used it up. The takeaway is that the moment to understand a choice is when it enters your project, not when someone asks about it months later.

## Key words

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Trade-off** | What you gain and what you give up when you choose one option |
| **Area** | One part of a project you learn and build, such as a database or caching |
| **Learning share** | The part of your project hours you spend learning, not building |
| **Explain-back rule** | From chapter 1.5: keep a part only if you can explain it without notes |
| **Licence** | The terms that say what others may do with a piece of code |
