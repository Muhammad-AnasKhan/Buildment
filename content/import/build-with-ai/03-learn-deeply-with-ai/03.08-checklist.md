## Learning log

1. **Your feeling:** Before your recall test below, how well did you rate your understanding, from 1 to 5? How did the test compare?
2. **Your angles:** Which of the five angles found the most surprising gap for you, and why do you think the others missed it?
3. **Your AI use:** Where in this module did the AI save you the most time, and where did you have to check or correct it?
4. **Your next review:** On which two dates will you test yourself on this topic again, and what is the first question you will ask?

## Your topic from five angles

This task brings the whole module together on one topic. You learn it from five angles, test yourself with the text closed, and keep what you learned on one notes page. The point is not to prove you know everything. The point is to show your gaps honestly, and what you did about them. A submission that finds four gaps and fixes three is stronger than one that claims to have found none.

Choose a topic you need for your studies, your work or your project, small enough for a week. "Networking" is too big. "How a router chooses a route" is about right. Use AI for any part where it helps: explaining, drawing, quizzing. Then check what it gives you against a source you trust, and say which source.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional fellow. Samer, at a coding bootcamp, chose JavaScript promises. In his own words he wrote that a promise stands for a value that will arrive later, and is pending, then fulfilled or rejected. He asked a chat assistant to draw the three states, and checked the drawing against the official JavaScript documentation. The AI had left out that a promise settles only once, so he added it. His worked example fetched a list of products and showed what runs on success and what runs on failure. His failure case asked what happens if nobody handles a rejected promise: the error can go unnoticed. He taught it to a friend, who asked what happens if two promises run at once, and he wrote that down as an open question.

Samer's recall test had six questions, written before he looked at any notes. He answered four fully, one partly and missed one, and he listed the two gaps by name. His notes page used the template from chapter 3.6, with his sources, a "last reviewed" date and two dates for his next reviews. Notice the shape: every angle found something, every AI output was checked against a named source, and the recall test was honest.

Compare it with a weak answer on the same topic: "I asked the AI to explain promises five different ways, and now I understand them well. Recall test: I reread my notes and got everything right. Notes: attached summary." The five angles are all the same angle, explanation. The "recall test" was rereading. The notes are the AI's summary. It would feel thorough to write, and it shows nothing a mentor can check.

If you prefer not to use your own topic, use the provided one: spaced review, the habit from chapter 3.4. Your only sources are chapters 3.2 and 3.4 of this course, so you need no other tool, file or connection. Write it in your own words. Draw a timeline and check it against the diagram in 3.4. Work out a review plan with real dates for an exam three weeks away. Describe at least two ways a review plan goes wrong, and explain the habit to someone. Then write your recall test from memory and your notes page.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor reads your answer with the table below. Different topics give different gaps, and that is expected. What matters is that each part is there, that the AI's work was checked, and that your recall test was really done with the text closed.

You will not lose marks for gaps. A gap you found, named and wrote on your notes page is exactly what this module asks for. The only thing that counts against you is a gap hidden behind rereading, or an AI answer passed on without a check. If you are unsure whether a part is good enough, say so in your answer; your mentor will tell you.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Five angles | All five, each with a gap or a result named | Three or four angles | One or two angles |
| Recall test | Written with notes closed, gaps named | Done, but gaps not named | Rereading, or no test |
| AI checked | An AI picture or claim checked against a named source | Checked, but the source is not named | Nothing checked |
| Notes page | Own words, the template, two review dates | Own words, parts missing | A pasted summary, or no page |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example, they may ask: "Which gap did the recall test find that the five angles missed?" Or: "What will your first review question be, and on what date?" You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
