## Learning log

1. **Your feeling:** Before your recall test below, how well did you rate your understanding, from 1 to 5? How did the test compare?
2. **Your angles:** Which of the five angles found the most surprising gap for you, and why do you think the others missed it?
3. **Your AI use:** Where in this module did the AI save you time, and where did you have to check or correct it?
4. **Your next review:** On which two dates will you test yourself on this topic again, and what is the first question you will ask?

## Your topic from five angles

This task brings the whole module together on one topic. You learn it from five angles, test yourself with the text closed, and keep what you learned on one notes page. Showing your gaps honestly, and what you did about them, matters more than proving you know everything. A submission that finds four gaps and fixes three is stronger than one that claims to have found none.

Choose a topic you need for your studies, your work or your project, small enough for a week. "Networking" is too big. "How a router chooses a route" is about right. Use AI for any part where it helps: explaining, drawing, quizzing. Then check what it gives you, or your own picture or summary, against a source you trust, and say which source. If you used an audio overview or your phone's text-to-speech, checking one of its claims against the page counts too.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional fellow. Samer, at a coding bootcamp, chose JavaScript promises. In his own words he wrote that a promise stands for a value that will arrive later, and is pending, then fulfilled or rejected. He asked a chat assistant to draw the three states, and checked the drawing against the official JavaScript documentation. The AI had left out that a promise settles only once, so he added it. His worked example fetched a list of products and showed what runs on success and what runs on failure. His failure case asked what happens if nobody handles a rejected promise: the error can go unnoticed. He taught it to a friend, who asked what happens if two promises run at once, and he wrote that down as an open question.

Samer's recall test had six questions, written before he looked at any notes. He answered four fully, one partly and missed one, and he listed the two gaps by name. His notes page used the template from chapter 3.6, with his sources, a "last reviewed" date and two dates for his next reviews. Every angle found something, every check named its source, and the recall test was honest.

A weak answer on the same topic reads like this: "I asked the AI to explain promises five different ways, and now I understand them well. Recall test: I reread my notes and got everything right. Notes: attached summary." The five angles are all one angle, explanation. The "recall test" was rereading. The notes are the AI's summary. It would feel thorough to write, and it gives a mentor nothing to check.

If you prefer not to use your own topic, use the provided one: spaced review, the habit from chapter 3.4. Your only sources are chapters 3.2 and 3.4 of this course, so you need no other tool, file or connection. A chat assistant gave one fellow this plan for it, printed here so you can check it:

> 'Here is your plan for learning spaced review. Monday 3rd: read chapter 3.4. Tuesday 4th, Wednesday 5th and Thursday 6th: quiz yourself each day. Then rest until your exam on Monday 24th. Four reviews in a row will fix it in your memory.'

For the provided topic, write the habit in your own words. Draw a timeline and check it against the diagram in 3.4. Check the AI's plan above against chapter 3.4 and say what is wrong with its gaps. Work out a better review plan with real dates for the same exam. Describe at least two ways a review plan goes wrong, and explain the habit to someone. Then write your recall test from memory and your notes page.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor reads your answer with the table below. Different topics give different gaps, and that is expected. What matters is that each part is there, that something was checked against a named source, and that your recall test was really done with the text closed.

You will not lose marks for gaps. A gap you found, named and wrote on your notes page is exactly what this module asks for. What counts against you is a gap hidden behind rereading, or an AI answer passed on without a check. If you are unsure whether a part is good enough, say so in your answer; your mentor will tell you.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Five angles | All 5, each with a gap or a result named | 3 or 4 angles, or 5 with no gaps named | 0 to 2 angles |
| Recall test | At least 4 questions, notes closed, every gap named | Done with notes closed, some gaps not named | Rereading, or no test |
| Checked against a source | At least 1 AI output, picture or summary checked against a named source, with the result | Checked, but the source is not named | Nothing checked |
| Notes page | Every template part, in own words, with 2 review dates | 1 or 2 template parts missing | 3 or more parts missing, or a pasted summary |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet", at least three parts are "Met", and your reply to the mentor's follow-up question answers it with evidence.

After you submit, your mentor sends you one or two questions about your own answer. They might ask which gap the recall test found that the five angles missed, or what your first review question will be, and on what date. A good reply points to something in your work: a line from your notes page, a recall question, a source page. You can dictate your answer with your phone's microphone, and you may add a voice note link as well.
