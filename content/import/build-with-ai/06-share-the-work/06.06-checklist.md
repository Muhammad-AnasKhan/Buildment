## Course checklist

- [ ] I can name what an AI tool does not know in a task, I make the calls myself, and I never paste what a stranger should not read
- [ ] I choose tools by the job, keep my work in files I own, and have a fallback when a tool changes
- [ ] I learn a topic from five angles, test myself from memory, and space my reviews over days
- [ ] I run short daily labs, break one part on purpose, and log them on GitHub after a pre-push check
- [ ] I plan in months with a large learning share, check what AI suggests, keep only what I can explain, and share it honestly

## Your AI-as-enabler toolkit

The table holds one line for each habit in the course. It is short on purpose. When a new task arrives, at work, in study or in your project, you will not have time to reread six modules. You will have time to read these lines.

| Habit | One line to remember |
|---|---|
| What AI does not know | Your context, your private data, local and recent facts, and what nobody wrote down |
| You make the call | AI drafts, explains and compares; you decide, check and own the result |
| Hints, not answers | Ask AI to make you think; answers it hands you can weaken your practice |
| Before you paste | Would I be fine if a stranger read this? If not, remove it or do not paste it |
| Jobs, not tools | Choose the kind of tool for the job; keep two examples and a fallback |
| Own your files | Notes, saved prompts and logs live in files you own, not in a chat history |
| Recall, not rereading | Close it, recall it, study only the gaps, and recall again days later |
| Five angles | Explain it, picture it, work an example, break it, then build or teach it |
| Listen, then read | Audio to meet a topic; the sources to learn it and to check the audio |
| Daily lab | Study, build, break one part on purpose, write: about 30 to 45 minutes |
| Check before you run | Commands against the official docs; packages on the official package site |
| Plan in months | A large learning share and one new area at a time |
| Check it exists | Open every video and article an AI suggests; find a listing for every book |
| Explain-back | Keep no part you cannot explain without notes; understood beats big |
| Licences | No licence means read and learn, not copy; credit what you reuse |
| Write it yourself | Draft from your log, let AI edit and ask, then check every fact |
| AI-use line | Which tool, what it did, and what you checked |

Copy the table somewhere you will see it: the first page of your notes, a pinned note on your phone, or the README of your lab repository. Read it before you open an AI tool for a new kind of task. Much of the value of this course is in these lines being near you at the moment you need them.

You will not need every line every time. A new topic for your project needs "five angles", "recall, not rereading" and "check it exists". A client task needs "what AI does not know", "before you paste" and "you make the call". A Friday write-up needs the last two lines. Part of the skill is choosing the two or three lines that fit the task in front of you. You practised that choice in the mixed review in 6.4.

### How to look back at gate 1

The first question matters most. In module 1, you split a task into what AI can help with, what it does not know, and the calls that stay yours. You did it before you knew most of the habits in this course. Reading it again is a clear way to see how your thinking has changed.

Here is an example from a fictional fellow, Heba, whom you met in chapter 6.3. In gate 1 she used the provided case, the donation tracker for an NGO. Her answer then was short. AI could write the code, design the database and write the README. AI did not know the NGO's budget. The calls that stayed hers were the page colours and the app's name.

Reading it now, she sees three things. First, she handed AI the database design without any facts about the NGO, such as what kinds of donations it gets and in which currencies. She would now give it those facts first, and check its design against them. Second, she missed the biggest call of all: what data to keep about donors, and who may see it. That is a privacy decision, and it stays with the people involved, not with a tool. Third, she gave AI the parts she most needed to learn. She would now ask AI to explain and quiz her on database design, and keep the design itself as her own work, explained line by line.

Heba also noticed something she did well. She already listed the NGO's budget as missing context, months before she knew the words for it. Write your own look-back the same way: what you would now keep for yourself, what you would give to AI with more context, and one thing you already did well. Four or five honest sentences show your mentor more than a page of general words. If you would rather not write about your own work, answer with the donation tracker case or any case from the course.

## Your next four weeks

The course ends here, but your project does not. This last task turns your plan into the next four weeks of real work, so the habits keep running after the course is over. A plan for four weeks is short enough to be honest about, and long enough to show a pattern: new areas, daily labs, recall tests and write-ups.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about Firas, the student you met in the mixed review. His project is a small app where students on his university databases course share practice questions. Over the next four weeks, the database is his new area in week 1, and week 2 builds it into the app. Week 4 starts the API, his next new area. In most weeks he will run four labs of about 30 minutes, and in week 2 he will stop the database on purpose and record what the app shows. Each Thursday he will do a recall test on the week's area, with his notes closed. Each Friday he will write a short write-up from his log, with an AI-use line. Week 3 falls in his exam period, so it is a review week with two labs instead of four, and no new area.

Look at the shape of his answer. Every week has a clear area or task. The labs have a number and a break-on-purpose step. The checks are named, with days. And he planned for a week that will go badly, instead of pretending every week will be the same.

Compare it with a weak answer: "In the next four weeks I will keep learning and building, and post more on LinkedIn." It sounds fine, and it says nothing. There is no area, no lab, no check, and nothing a mentor could ask about in a month.

If you prefer not to use your own project, use Ibrahim's case from the capstone. Take the improved plan you wrote for him, or use this one. He has about 6 hours a week. Month 1 is the API and the database, and month 2 is the queue logic and the waiting screen. Before anything else, his database password must be dealt with. Write his next four weeks.

## How your mentor will read it

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weeks | Each of the four weeks has an area or a task | Some weeks are clear, others are vague | No weeks, only a wish |
| Labs | A number of lab days a week, and one break-on-purpose lab | Labs mentioned, but no number or no break | No labs |
| Checks and sharing | A recall test and a write-up with an AI-use line, with days | One of the two, or no days | Neither |
| Real life | One likely problem and what you will do then | A problem named, but no plan for it | Nothing |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

Your mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "Which day is your recall test?" Or: "What will you do if a power cut takes your lab day?" You answer them in writing or in a voice note. Your mentor will also read your learning log, not to grade it, but to see how your thinking has moved since module 1.
