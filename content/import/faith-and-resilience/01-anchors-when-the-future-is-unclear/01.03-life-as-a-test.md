## A thought late at night

It is now ten days since Yasmin sent the draft. She sent her polite follow-up on Sunday, and that has had no answer either. This week the power schedule changed again, and the grid comes mostly at night, from 01:00 to 07:00. Her younger brother has a fever, so she sits with him in the evenings and brings him water and soup. Since Saturday she has done only two hours of paid work.

On Wednesday night, sitting by her brother's bed, a thought comes to her. Maybe Allah is angry with me. Maybe I did something wrong, and that is why everything is stuck. The thought is quiet, but it is heavy. Once it arrives, everything in the week seems to fit it. The silent client, the night power, her brother's fever: all of it starts to look like a message.

Many sincere believers have had this thought. It usually comes when we are tired and the hard things add up. It feels humble, even religious. But it is not what the Quran and the Sunnah teach about hardship. This chapter looks at what they do teach, and at what changes when you read a hard week the way they describe it.

## Life is a test with a purpose

The Quran describes life itself as a test. Allah created death and life to test which of you is best in deeds (Quran 67:2).

> الَّذِي خَلَقَ الْمَوْتَ وَالْحَيَاةَ لِيَبْلُوَكُمْ أَيُّكُمْ أَحْسَنُ عَمَلًا

Meaning: the One who created death and life to test which of you is best in deeds.

Notice what the test is about. It is not about how much hardship you have, or how lucky your week is. It is about deeds: what you do with what you are given. A week with a silent client and a sick brother is not a failed test. It is one part of the test, and the question in that part is: what is the best thing to do now?

The Quran also tells us that the test is for everyone who believes. Do people think they will be left alone to say "we believe" without being tested? Those before them were tested too (Quran 29:2 to 3). So a test does not mean that something has gone wrong in your faith. It means you are a believer, living the life believers have always lived.

And the test has a purpose. Allah created jinn and humans only to worship Him (Quran 51:56). Worship here is wider than prayer. In module 6 you will see how a good intention turns daily work, study and caring for family into worship. For now, hold this: your life is not a series of random events. It has a purpose, and every part of it, easy or hard, belongs to that purpose.

## Hardship is not proof of Allah's anger

The hadith from the canvas deserves a closer look. Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas, may Allah be pleased with him, asked the Prophet which people are tested most severely. He answered: the prophets, then those most like them, and so on. A person is tested according to the strength of his religion (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2398, graded hasan sahih).

The prophets were the people closest to Allah, and they had the hardest lives. If hardship were proof of Allah's anger, this could not be true. So the hadith answers the late-night thought. A hard week cannot be read as proof that Allah is angry with you. The best people who ever lived had harder weeks than yours.

Be careful with what this hadith does not say. It does not explain why a particular hardship came to a particular person. Only Allah knows that. It does not say that your loss is a gift, or that a hard thing happened because Allah loves you. When someone you know is suffering, do not use this hadith to explain their pain to them. It is a truth about believers in general. Its job is to remove a false idea, so that a person can turn to what helps now.

## Not more than you can carry

The Quran adds a mercy to the test. Allah does not place on any soul more than it can bear (Quran 2:286). This is about what Allah asks of us: the duties He sets are within our capacity. It is a comfort.

Sadly, this verse is sometimes used the wrong way. Someone tells a tired person: "Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity, so stop complaining and cope." That is not what the verse is for. The same verse goes on with duas in which believers ask Allah not to burden them with more than they can bear. So the verse itself shows us asking for help. You are allowed to ask Allah for help, and you are allowed to ask people for help. Yasmin asking her cousin to sit with her brother is not a lack of patience. It is part of carrying the test well.

## With hardship comes ease

The last verse in this chapter is one many people know by heart. With hardship comes ease; truly, with hardship comes ease (Quran 94:5 to 6).

> فَإِنَّ مَعَ الْعُسْرِ يُسْرًا

Meaning: so truly, with hardship comes ease.

One translation renders it this way: "with hardship [will be] ease" (Quran 94:5, Sahih International). Keep the word "with". People often quote it as "after hardship comes ease", but the Arabic word here is *ma'a*, which means with. The verse places ease with the hardship. In Yasmin's week, she could already find small eases inside the hard days. There was a cousin who could help, two hours of power at 05:00, and a brother whose fever was going down.

## Worked example: Nada reads her week twice

Here is the same method in a different life. Nada is 25 and works as a junior designer for a small online agency. This month her mother had an operation, and Nada spent four days at the hospital. She missed one deadline, and the agency gave the task to someone else. Then her laptop charger broke. On Thursday evening she writes in her notebook: "Maybe this is a punishment." Then she decides to read her week twice, on purpose, step by step.

**Step 1: the facts.** Mother's operation; four days at the hospital; one missed deadline; one task given to a colleague; a broken charger. She writes only what is true, with no explanations.

**Step 2: reading 1.** "Allah is angry with me." She asks: what does this reading tell me to do tomorrow? The honest answer is: nothing. If the hardship is a punishment she cannot name, she has no next step. She can only wait for it to end. She notices that this reading makes her not want to reply to her manager at all.

**Step 3: check reading 1 against the sources.** The prophets were tested most (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2398). So the hard month cannot be read as proof of anger. Reading 1 fails the check. It came from tiredness, not from the Quran.

**Step 4: reading 2.** "This month is part of the test, and the test is about the best deed now (Quran 67:2)." She asks the same question again. Now there are answers. Caring for her mother is a good deed, not a delay. She can write to her manager, explain the four days briefly, and ask for the next task. She can borrow her brother's charger until she can buy one. She can ask Allah for her mother's recovery in her own words.

**Step 5: choose.** She keeps the reading that passed the check and gives her steps. On Friday morning she sends the message to her manager. The month is still hard. But it now has things in it that she can do.

The diagram below shows the rule behind Nada's notebook. It begins with one hard week and splits on a single question: how do I read it?

The anger reading does not only feel worse. It leads nowhere, because it gives no question you can act on. The test reading gives a question with an answer: what is the best deed now? The dotted line shows why you are allowed to drop the first reading: the hadith about the prophets rules it out. So when a hard week starts to feel like a message of anger, ask two things. Which reading do the sources support? And which one gives you a step?

## Where people go wrong

The first mistake is the one Yasmin and Nada almost made: reading hardship as anger. You can spot it when a hard week makes you stop praying for good things, or stop trying, because "there is no point".

The second mistake is the opposite one, and it often comes from kind people. They tell someone in pain that their loss is a gift, or that Allah took something because He loves them. The sources do not tell us to explain anyone's hardship. Say what the sources say in general terms, then turn to what helps now: being present, practical help, dua.

The third mistake is to use 2:286 as a reason to carry everything alone. The verse is a mercy, and it sits next to duas asking for relief. Asking for help is part of the test, not a failure of it.

## This week

The next time a hard day starts to feel like a message, do what Nada did. Write the facts of the day in two or three lines. Then read them twice: once as "Allah is angry with me", and once as "part of the test: what is the best deed now?". Check which reading the sources support, and write down the one step the second reading gives you. If you prefer, do this for Laith's two weeks from the quiz instead of your own.
