## Your fresh start plan

This task brings the module together on one setback. A fresh start plan is a short, honest note that turns a failure into a next step. It says what happened in plain words, and it keeps any wrong apart from the setback. It puts right what can be put right, and names what you will change so it does not happen twice. It sets your intention and a small daily habit. And it says what you will do when "if only" comes back. It is not a confession, and nobody will judge the failure itself. It shows how you will start again.

Choose a setback from your work or study in the past year: a missed deadline, a failed exam, a lost client, a project that did not work. It does not have to be large. If you use your own life, you may use a small or imagined example; the mentor checks the reasoning, not the event. If you prefer not to use your own life at all, use the provided case below. Both count the same.

As in the last gate, the help here is lighter on purpose. You have seen Tariq, Zuhair and Wijdan work through these steps in full. This time the example is short, and the plan is yours to build.

Here is the example. Hamdi failed his statistics module after studying only in the last week. He saw that nobody was wronged: it was a setback, not a sin, so there was no one to apologise to. He found two causes and wrote two changes: forty minutes of statistics most mornings, and one question to his tutor every week. He wrote an intention line, and a sentence to say when "if only" came back. His first step was an email to his tutor on Sunday.

If you prefer not to use your own situation, use this provided case. Shatha is 23, a final-year graphic design student who also takes small freelance jobs. Abu Saleh, a family friend, runs a bakery near her home. He asked her to design a new menu board and a printed menu for 250 shekels. He paid 100 in advance and needed them by the 15th of the month. That same week Shatha had two exams. She worked in long bursts late at night, fell behind, and did not tell him. She delivered on the 29th, two weeks late. In the rush, two prices were wrong: the large loaf showed 3 shekels instead of 5, and the sesame cake 8 instead of 6. The bakery printed 200 menus before anyone noticed, and reprinting them cost 180 shekels. Abu Saleh was polite, but he said he would ask someone else next time, and he has not yet paid the other 150 shekels. Shatha feels ashamed. She avoids walking past the bakery, and she is thinking of stopping all freelance work until she graduates. She has one small job left, a flyer for a tutoring centre due in three weeks, and her final project is due in two months.

There is also an optional, harder part. Sometimes two good plans pull against each other. In Shatha's case, putting things right with Abu Saleh may cost her time she needs for her final project and the tutoring flyer. If you take this part, add a short paragraph: which plan comes first this week, and how will you keep the other from being dropped? It is not graded on its own, but your mentor may ask about it.

If a setback is weighing on you for a long time, you do not have to carry it alone. If you feel overwhelmed for a long time, or you have thoughts of harming yourself, talk to your mentor or the fellowship coordinator today. Talk to a doctor or counsellor too, if you can. This course does not replace professional care.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor will read your plan with the table below. Different people will make different plans for the same setback, and that is fine. The rubric grades your reasoning and your plan only. It never grades your faith, your feelings, your personal circumstances or the size of the failure.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Honest account | What happened in plain words, with any wrong named apart from the setback | Events told, but with excuses, or setback and wrong mixed up | No account, or only blame on others |
| Putting right | A concrete step with the person affected, or a clear reason none is needed | A vague apology "if needed" | Nothing, where someone was wronged |
| Lesson and change | One to three specific changes, each removing a cause | A general lesson such as "be more careful" | No lesson |
| Intention and daily habit | A one-line intention, and a small habit with a time and a size | Only one of the two | Neither |
| Leaving "if only" | What you will say and do when regret returns, and a first step with a date | Regret named, but no plan for it | Stuck in "if only", or giving up |

For the honest account line: if you use your own life, you may use a small or imagined example; the mentor checks the reasoning.

**Pass rule:** your plan passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. One example: "Which of your changes removes the biggest cause?". Another: "What will you do on the first day your habit is missed?". You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
