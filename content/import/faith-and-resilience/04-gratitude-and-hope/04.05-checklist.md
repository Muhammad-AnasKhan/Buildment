## Module 4 checklist

- [ ] I can explain how gratitude in ease and patience in hardship fit in one life (Sahih Muslim 2999)
- [ ] I can practise shukr in the heart, on the tongue and in action, and I thank the people who help me
- [ ] I use "look at those below you" only on myself, by choice, and never as a reply to someone's pain
- [ ] I can tell hope from carelessness and fear from despair, and I keep fear about my own choices, not events

## Learning log

1. **Noticing:** Which blessing had you stopped noticing before this module? If you prefer, answer about the provided case below.
2. **Thanking people:** Who helps you regularly whom you have not thanked in a while, and what would a specific thank you sound like? If you prefer, answer about the provided case.
3. **Your missing wing:** When you feel stuck, is it more often hope or fear that is missing? What is one sign that tells you which? If you prefer, answer about the provided case.
4. **Comparison:** What will you do the next time you catch yourself comparing your worst day with someone else's best moment? If you prefer, answer about the provided case.

## Your one-week practice

This task turns the module into seven ordinary days. Gratitude and hope are easy to agree with and easy to forget. A practice with small, concrete actions makes them part of a week instead of an idea. The point is not to feel grateful and hopeful every day. The point is to notice, say and do, with both wings, even on a flat day.

Your practice has five parts: what you notice each day, who you thank and how, one action that uses a blessing well, one hope you hold in Allah's mercy with a step that shows it, and one thing in your own choices you will be careful about. If you can, keep the practice for at least three days before you submit, and add what you noticed. If you cannot, a clear plan is enough. You may write about yourself or about the provided case. Both count exactly the same, and nothing personal is graded.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional person. Sara is a junior data analyst who often compares herself with a cousin abroad. Her plan says that each night she will write one blessing from the day in her notes, and she lists the first three: her mentor's patient feedback, the two hours of solar power her neighbour shares on weekdays, and her own steady progress in a spreadsheet course. She will thank her neighbour on Tuesday, specifically, for the power that lets her finish her work before dark, and she will offer to set up a simple spreadsheet for his shop's stock. Her hope is about a job she has applied for: she holds hope in Allah's mercy, and the step that shows it is a polite follow-up on Wednesday and dua after the dawn prayer each day. Her care is about her own choice: she has been speaking sharply to her younger brother when she is tired, so she will stop work at 21:00 and apologise to him once, plainly. After four days she added one line: "Writing the blessing down made me see the neighbour's help every day, not only when the power was off."

Look at the shape of Sara's answer. Every part is concrete: a day, a person, an action. The thank you is specific, and it comes with something she does in return. The hope has a step in it. And the care is about her own choice, not about events in her life.

A weak answer would say: "This week I will be more grateful and more hopeful, and I will remember that others have it worse." It names no blessing, no person, no action and no choice, and the last part turns a tool for one's own heart into "others have it worse", which this course does not teach.

**The provided case.** If you prefer not to use your own situation, use this one. Hala is 24 and works part-time as a junior web developer for a small company abroad, three days a week. She is waiting to hear whether the company will offer her a full-time position next month. Her mother has diabetes and needs a check-up every month, and Hala takes her. Her neighbour, Abu Hatem, shares a battery charged by his solar panel, so she can work for four hours on the days when the grid power is off. Her younger sister, who is in secondary school, does most of the cooking on Hala's work days. Last month Hala spent many evenings looking at classmates' posts from abroad and felt she had achieved nothing. When she is tired, she has been answering her sister sharply. Write Hala's one-week practice.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor reads your practice with the table below. The rubric grades your reasoning and your plan only, never your faith, your feelings or your personal circumstances.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Noticing | A specific blessing for each day, or at least three named | Blessings named only in general ("my family") | No blessings named |
| Thanking | Allah thanked, and one named person thanked specifically, with how | Allah thanked, or a person thanked in general | No thanks at all |
| Action | One action that uses a blessing well, with a day | An action without a day or detail | No action |
| Hope | A hope in Allah's mercy with one step that shows it | A hope, but no step | No hope, or a hope stated as a guaranteed result |
| Care | One of your own choices to be careful about, with what you will do | A choice named, but no action | No care, or fear used to read events as punishment |

**Pass rule:** your practice passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions, for example: "What will you do on the day the practice feels flat?" or "How will the person you thanked know what their help made possible?" You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
