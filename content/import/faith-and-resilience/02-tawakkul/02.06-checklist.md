## Your means plan

This task brings the module together in one plan. You choose one goal, list the means you will take, count what needs counting, add a backup for the thing most likely to fail, and say where your trust comes in. The point is not a perfect plan. No plan controls the result, and you saw in the cave that a plan can only take you so far. The point is to show that you can put the heart's trust and the hands' work together, as the Prophet, peace be upon him, did.

Choose a goal that is a few days or weeks away and that depends on things that might go wrong: an online exam, a deadline, a call, a trip to an office, a delivery. If you prefer not to use your own life, use the provided case of Bilal below. Both choices are equal, and your mentor reads them the same way.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional person. Rawan is a freelance illustrator. She must deliver twelve drawings to a client by Sunday at 12:00. She draws on paper, scans the drawings at a print shop, and uploads them. In her plan, the goal comes first, with the date and time. Then the means, each with a time: finish three drawings a day from Tuesday to Friday; scan them at the print shop on Friday at 10:00, when it has generator power; upload on Friday evening. She counts what matters. Twelve drawings at three a day takes four days, so Friday is the last safe day to finish. The upload of the scanned files took about 30 minutes last time.

Her backup covers the thing most likely to fail: the print shop's scanner. On Monday she asks a friend whether she can use his scanner on Saturday if the shop's is broken. She also keeps a phone photo of each drawing, so the client could see the work even on a bad day. Then the trust: "When the drawings are uploaded, I have tied the camel. Whatever the client decides is with Allah" (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2517, graded hasan). Notice the shape: every means has a time or a person; the numbers are counted; the backup is arranged before the day; and the trust comes after the plan, not instead of it.

Compare it with a weaker answer: "I will finish the drawings this week, if Allah wills, and trust Allah with the rest." The trust in it is sincere. But it has no times, no count and no backup. It leaves the camel loose and calls it trust, which is the mistake of the pilgrims who came without provisions.

If you prefer not to use your own situation, use this provided case. Bilal is 23. He studied accounting and is now learning data analysis. To apply for a remote internship, he needs an English certificate, and the application closes in five weeks. He has booked an online English test for Thursday in three weeks, at 14:00. The test lasts two hours, his webcam must stay on the whole time, and the rules say a long break in the connection can cancel the test. At home, the grid comes about eight hours a day, on a schedule that changes every week, and his laptop battery lasts about two hours. He connects to the internet through his phone. His home is busy: he has three younger siblings, and the only quiet room is his parents' room in the afternoon. There is also a training centre forty minutes away by shared taxi. It has a testing room with generator power, and it charges 20 shekels for the afternoon. In his practice tests, Bilal's reading and listening are good. His writing is his weakest part. His mother has told him: "Do not worry, my son. Make dua, and Allah will make it easy."

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor reads your answer with the table below. The rubric grades only your reasoning and your plan, never your faith, your feelings or your circumstances.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goal | One goal, with a date and a time | A goal with no date or time | No clear goal |
| Means | Four or more means, each with a time or a person | Two or three means, or vague ones | No means, or only dua |
| Counting | The numbers that matter are counted and correct | Some numbers, or a count with an error | Nothing counted where it matters |
| Backup | A backup for the most likely failure, arranged before the day | A backup named but not arranged, or for an unlikely failure | No backup |
| Trust with means | Says where trust comes in, after the means, with a module 2 source | Trust named, with no source or no link to the plan | Trust used as a reason to skip a means, or missing |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "What will you do if the backup also fails?" or "Which number in your plan did you count, and how?" You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
