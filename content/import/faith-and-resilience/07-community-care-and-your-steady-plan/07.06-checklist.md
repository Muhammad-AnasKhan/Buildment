## Course checklist

- [ ] I can separate what only Allah knows from what is mine to do, and name today's step without "if only"
- [ ] I can explain tawakkul as trust in the heart with the means taken, and plan a backup
- [ ] I can meet loss and waiting with sabr that keeps acting, and I know words for hard moments
- [ ] I practise gratitude in heart, tongue and action, and keep hope and fear together
- [ ] I can decide with facts, shura and istikhara, work with a good intention, and start again after a failure
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 7.2 and 7.3, finished the mixed review in 7.4, submitted my capstone in 7.5, and submitted my answer below

Copy the table somewhere you will see it: the notes app on your phone, the first page of a notebook, or a message you send to yourself. Read it on the evening a client goes quiet, or the morning a result is due. Much of the value of this course is in these lines being near you at the right moment.

You do not need every line every time. A silent client needs "the unseen", "tie it and trust" and "waiting well". A choice between two offers needs "knowing first", "shura" and "istikhara". A missed deadline needs "starting again". A week that has become too heavy needs "care and telling" before anything else. Part of the skill is choosing which two or three lines fit the situation in front of you. You practised that choice in chapter 7.4, and it gets easier with use.

### How to look back at module 1

The first question in the learning log matters most. In the gate of module 1, you sorted one situation into what only Allah knows and what was yours to do. You did that before you had learned tawakkul, sabr, shura or any of the later teachings. Looking at it again is a clear way to see how your thinking has grown.

Here is an example from a fictional fellow, Bisan. In module 1 she used the provided case of a family member whose job was ending. She wrote that only Allah knew whether her uncle would find new work, that his part was to apply for jobs, and that she would make dua for him. It was a good start, and it was short.

Reading it now, Bisan sees four things she would add. First, a backup: her uncle could take short-term work while he searched, which is tying the camel (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2517, graded hasan). Second, how to wait well: keep doing good where he is, and use the dua for refuge from worry and grief at night (Sahih al-Bukhari 6369). Third, people to consult: a cousin who works in the same field. Fourth, the people around him: the family could serve him in small ways, and someone should check that he is not carrying the worry alone.

Bisan also noticed something she had done well. She had already separated Allah's part from her uncle's part, and she had not written "if only". That habit was there from the start. Write your own look-back the same way: what you would add, the source behind it, and one thing you already did well. Four or five honest sentences show your mentor more than a page of general words.

## One change in how you act

The last task of the course is short. You write about one change the course has made to how you act. It is not about how you feel or how strong your faith is. It is about something you now do differently: a step, a habit, a sentence you say, a person you ask. This time there is no list of parts to fill in, and there is less help on this page on purpose. By now you know what a mentor needs to see in a plan.

The task is here because a course like this can easily stay as ideas only. You can agree with every chapter and still act exactly as before. Naming one change, with one source and one date, turns agreement into a habit. One clear change is worth more than a list of ten.

### The provided case

If you prefer not to write about yourself, write about Bashir. Bashir is 24 and a freelance mobile developer. Before this course, this is how he handled uncertain weeks. When a client went quiet, he refreshed his messages late into the night and slept badly. He believed that planning a backup while waiting would show weak trust, so he never applied anywhere else until a client said no. After he lost a project, he said "if only" for days, and went over every decision he had made. And he never told anyone when he was struggling, because his brother's family had bigger problems.

This week, a client has not replied to his proposal for five days. His savings cover about one month of living costs. Imagine that Bashir has just finished this course. Write about one change he could make in how he acts, the source behind it, and what he will do this week.

### What a strong answer looks like

Here is a short example about a different fictional person, Shireen. Before the course, she said yes to every late favour and worked until 2:00. Now she keeps a sleep time of midnight on five nights a week, and moves late favours to Saturday. Her source is the hadith that your body has a right over you (Sahih al-Bukhari 5199), which the Prophet, peace be upon him, said to a companion who was doing too much. This week she will keep that sleep time on five nights.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor uses the table below. Each line has three levels. The mentor grades only the reasoning and the plan in your answer, never your faith, your feelings or your personal circumstances.

| Line | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| The change | What you did before and what you do now, as actions | The new action is clear, but not what changed | A feeling or a general hope only |
| The source | One verse or hadith that fits the change, with its reference | A source that fits, but no reference, or a loose fit | No source |
| This week | One time this week when you will do it | "Soon" or "more often", with no time | Nothing planned |

To pass, "The change" and "The source" must be Met, and no line can be Not yet. If your answer does not pass yet, your mentor replies with one or two short questions about the weakest line. You answer them by adding to your answer and submitting it again.

### What happens next

Your mentor will read your answer and your learning log together with your capstone. They are not grading the log. They are looking at how your thinking has moved since module 1, so they can suggest which teachings to keep practising. If the course has brought back hard feelings or memories for you, tell your mentor or the fellowship coordinator. That is always the right next step.

The teachings in this course will keep coming back in ordinary weeks. A silent client, a delayed result, a choice between two offers, a lost project and a friend who goes quiet are all places to use them. You do not need to remember the chapter names. You need the lines in your toolkit, the steady plan you built, and the habit of asking two questions: what is Allah's, and what is mine to do today?
