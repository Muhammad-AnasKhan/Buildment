## A date that cannot move

Rana is a freelance front-end developer. Her client, Paul, runs a small physiotherapy clinic in Canada. He wants a web app where patients book appointments, pay online, and where his staff see weekly reports.

Rana estimates the full app will take until June 20. Paul replies: "No. It must be finished by June 1."

Rana has a choice. She can defend her date, or she can try to understand his.

Rana asks: "What happens on June 1? It will help me plan." Paul explains that he is presenting at a health fair on June 3. He wants to show visitors how easy it is to book an appointment.

He does not need the payment page or the reports at the fair. He needs the booking screens to work and look good. So Rana offers a new plan: the booking flow, ready and tested by June 1, and the payment page and reports by June 20. Paul agrees at once. Neither of them "gave in". They found a deal that fits both needs.

## Positions and interests

A **position** is what someone says they want: "June 1", "a lower price", "we must use React". An **interest** is why they want it: a deadline they cannot move, a fixed budget, a worry about who will maintain the code later.

Roger Fisher and William Ury, from the Harvard Negotiation Project, made this idea famous in their book *Getting to Yes* (1981). Their advice: focus on interests, not positions. When two people argue about positions, each one holds on harder to their own position, and the best result is often a weak middle point. When they talk about interests, they can look for an answer that serves both.

| Position (what they say) | A possible interest (why) |
|---|---|
| "It must be done by June 1." | A demo at an event on June 3 |
| "Your rate is too high." | A fixed budget, or doubt that you will deliver |
| "We must use React." | Their other developer only knows React |
| "I can't join the 9:00 call." | The power is off in the morning |

One position can hide different interests. "Your rate is too high" could mean "I have no more money" or "I am not sure you are worth it". These need very different answers. Do not guess. Ask.

## How to find interests

Good questions are open and curious. They invite a story, not a yes or no.

- "What will this help you do?"
- "What happens if it is ready later?"
- "What worries you most about this part?"
- "What would a good result look like for you?"

Ask in a friendly tone. You are trying to help, not to trap the person. It also helps to explain why you are asking, as Rana did: "It will help me plan."

Share your own interests too. If you only ask questions, the other person cannot help you. Rana could say: "I want this to work well at the fair. I also work about six hours a day because of power cuts, so I need a plan I can really keep."

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not decide their interest for them">
It is easy to think "he just wants it cheap" or "she just wants control". Your guess may be wrong. Treat every guess as a question to check, not a fact.
</Callout>

## Look for options that serve both

Once both sides know the interests, look for more than one possible deal. Fisher and Ury call this inventing options for mutual gain: options where both sides get something they need.

Think of Yazan, a developer in Gaza, and his team lead Sofia, in Berlin. Yazan wants to work from 6:00 to 14:00 his time, because his area has power in the morning. Sofia wants him online late in the afternoon to answer questions. Their positions clash. Their interests do not: Yazan needs power to work, and Sofia needs answers when someone is blocked. Possible options:

- Yazan is online on his phone for one hour later in the day.
- Yazan writes a short handoff note at the end of his day.
- Sofia collects the team's questions and sends them early, while Yazan is still working.

Write several options before you choose one. The first idea is rarely the best.

The book has two more main ideas: separate the people from the problem, and use fair standards both sides can check. You practiced the first in module 4. You will use the second in the next chapter.
