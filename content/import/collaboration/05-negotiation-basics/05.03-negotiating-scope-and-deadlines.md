## "Just a few more pages"

Last month Yousef built a website for an NGO that runs youth training programs. The launch event is in two weeks. Today the coordinator, Grace, writes:

"The site looks great! Could you add a donations page and an Arabic version of every page before the launch? It shouldn't be much work."

Yousef's plan for the next two weeks is already full: final fixes, testing on phones, and moving the site to the live server. He also loses some hours each day to power cuts.

## Three things that move together

Every piece of work has three parts that are tied together:

- **Scope:** what is included.
- **Time:** when it will be ready.
- **Cost:** the money, or the people and hours, it needs.

Project managers often draw these as the three sides of a triangle. If one side changes, at least one other side must change too.

| If this changes | Then one of these must change |
|---|---|
| More scope | More time, or more cost, or something else leaves |
| Less time | Less scope, or more cost (for example a second person) |
| Lower cost | Less scope, or more time |

There is a hidden fourth part: **quality**. When nobody talks about the trade, quality is what quietly pays. Tests are skipped, reviews are rushed, and bugs reach the users. Never trade away quality without saying so.

## "Yes, and here is what it changes"

You do not need to say no. Say yes to the goal, then show the trade. Give two or three real options and let the other person choose. This uses what you learned in chapter 5.2: Grace's interest is a strong launch, not a long list of pages.

> Hi Grace, thank you, a donations page is a great idea for the launch. I can't add it and the Arabic version in the same two weeks and still test everything properly. Here are three options:
>
> 1. Launch on the planned date with the donations page. The Arabic version follows two weeks later.
> 2. Launch with both, one week later.
> 3. Launch with both on the planned date. I bring in a second developer I trust, which adds 300 US dollars.
>
> Which works best for you? I can start as soon as you choose.

The same shape works with a team lead. When Anke asked Yousef to ship the login screen on Thursday instead of Tuesday, he replied:

> I can ship email login on Thursday. Google sign-in needs two more days of testing. Shall I ship email login Thursday and Google sign-in on Tuesday?

A simple rule keeps trades fair: **when you give something, ask for something.** If you add work, ask for time or money. If you cut the price, cut the scope. This is not greed. It shows that your work has value, and it stops one side from carrying all the cost.

## Talking about your rate

For freelancers, the hardest trade is often the price. Prepare before the conversation, not during it.

1. **Know your numbers.** Estimate the hours. Know your lowest rate: the one that still covers your costs and your time.
2. **Know your walk-away option.** What will you do if there is no deal: take another project, or use the time to study? Fisher and Ury call this your BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Knowing it helps you say no calmly.
3. **Use fair standards.** Show how you reached the price: hours multiplied by your rate, or the price of a similar project. Fisher and Ury call these objective criteria. People accept a number more easily when they can see where it comes from.

When a client says "your price is too high", do not apologize, and do not drop the price at once. Ask about the interest behind it. Then trade scope, not only price. Here is Yousef's reply to Martin, the client from chapter 5.1:

> Hi Martin, thanks for being open about the budget. My quote of 900 US dollars is for about 30 hours: the booking screens, an admin page, and testing on phones. If your budget is 600 US dollars, I can build the booking screens and do the testing first. We can add the admin page later, when you are ready. Would that work for you?

If a client can only pay below your lowest rate, it is fine to say no politely: "I understand if this does not fit your budget. If things change, I would be happy to talk again." A deal you cannot keep is worse than no deal.

<Callout type="tip" title="Put the deal in writing">
After a call, send a short summary: what is included, what moved, the new date and the price. End with "Can you confirm this is right?" Two lines in writing can prevent a week of confusion later.
</Callout>
