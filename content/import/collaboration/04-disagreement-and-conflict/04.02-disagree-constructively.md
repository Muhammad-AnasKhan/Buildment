## Two messages about the same plan

Jumana is a junior developer at a small startup that builds a booking app. In a team call, the product lead, Marco, says they will drop the low-data version of the app to save time before launch.

Jumana thinks this is a mistake. Many of their users are on slow mobile connections. After the call she drafts two messages to the team channel.

> **Draft A:** "Dropping the low-data version is a bad idea. You clearly have not thought about users with slow internet."

> **Draft B:** "Marco, can I ask what we save by dropping the low-data version? I checked last month's logs: about a third of bookings came from slow connections. What if we keep it for the booking page only and drop it everywhere else?"

## Aim at the idea, not the person

When people feel attacked, they stop listening and start defending. So the first rule is simple: talk about the **plan, the code or the decision**, not about the person who made it.

| Aimed at the person | Aimed at the idea |
|---|---|
| "You clearly did not think about this." | "I am worried this plan misses slow connections." |
| "You always rush things." | "This date leaves no time for testing." |
| "That is a stupid design." | "I find this screen hard to use on a small phone." |
| "Why would you do that?" | "What made you choose this approach?" |

Words like "always", "never", "clearly" and "stupid" turn a question about work into a judgment about a person. Remove them, and the same point becomes much easier to hear.

## Three tools: question, evidence, proposal

**1. Ask a question first.** The other person may know something you do not. Marco may have a budget limit or a promise to an investor. A real question, such as "What do we save by doing this?", gives him a chance to explain. Be careful with trap questions like "Did you even test it?" They sound like questions, but they are attacks.

**2. Bring evidence.** Evidence is something others can check: a log, a test result, a message from a user, a screenshot. Say how sure you are. "I could be wrong, but the logs show..." is honest and still strong.

**3. Make a proposal.** A disagreement with no alternative leaves the other person stuck. A proposal turns "no" into "what about this?" It can be small, such as a one-week trial, or a smaller version of the plan.

<Callout type="tip" title="Pick the right place">
A short disagreement can happen in a thread. A bigger one is better in a call or a direct message. Never correct a teammate in front of a client. After a call, write a two-line summary of what you agreed, so everyone across time zones has the same record.
</Callout>

## When you are the junior person

Disagreeing with a team lead, a senior developer or a client can feel risky. You do not have to be loud. You have to be clear and respectful. Questions and evidence work well here, because they invite the senior person to check your point, instead of asking them to admit they were wrong.

If you are tired or angry, write your message, then wait before you send it. With teammates in other time zones, a reply sent at 1:00 at night is rarely your best one.

## After the decision

Sometimes you share your view clearly and the team still decides against you. Then a useful habit is **"disagree and commit"**: you said what you thought, and now you support the decision fully. Amazon lists this idea in its leadership principles. It means you do not secretly work against the decision, you do not work slowly on purpose, and you do not say "I told you so" later.

There is one exception. If a decision is unsafe, dishonest or harms users, committing to it is not the right answer. Chapter 4.4 covers when and how to take a concern further.

## Common questions

**Q: What if I am not sure I am right?**
A: Say so. "I might be missing something, but..." is still a useful disagreement. Your question may lead the team to the answer, even if your first idea was wrong.

**Q: Is it rude to disagree with a client?**
A: Not if you are respectful and focused on their goal. Clients pay for your judgment. A short question and a clear option usually earn more trust than silent agreement.
