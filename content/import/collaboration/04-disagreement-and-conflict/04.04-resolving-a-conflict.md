## A week of short messages

Samir and Leila are two freelancers building a website for the same client. Samir writes the backend. Leila writes the frontend.

Twice this week, Leila changed Samir's code to fix a bug, without telling him. Samir found out when his own changes broke. Leila, for her part, has been waiting for Samir's handoff notes, which often arrive late when his power is cut. Each of them thinks the other is the problem.

Their messages have become short and cold. Yesterday the client asked why the contact form is still not working. Both of them blamed "a coordination issue".

## Five steps toward an agreement

There is no perfect method, but these steps work for most work conflicts between two people.

**1. Pause, then pick the right place.** Do not reply while you are angry. Ask for a private conversation, by call if the internet allows, or in a direct message if it does not.

> "Hi Leila, I think the handoffs between us are not working well this week, and it is starting to show for the client. Could we have a 20 minute call tomorrow to sort it out? Any time after 10:00 my time works."

**2. Start with the shared goal.** Remind each other what you both want: "We both want the client to have a working site by the 20th." This reminds you both that you are working together, not against each other.

**3. Each person explains, and the other says it back.** Describe what happened and how it affected you, without labels. You practiced this in module 2. Then listen to the other side and say it back, as in chapter 4.3. Samir learns that Leila changed his code because the client was waiting and he was offline. Leila learns that her changes broke his work.

**4. Name the problem as a shared problem.** Not "you change my code", but "we have no clear rule for who changes which code, and no plan for when one of us is offline."

**5. Agree on specific next steps, and write them down.** Who does what, by when. Then send a short summary, so neither of you depends on memory.

```text
Summary of our call (Tuesday):
1. Only the owner changes a file. If it is urgent, message first and wait 2 hours.
2. Samir posts handoff notes before 18:00 his time, or a short "power out" message from his phone.
3. Leila tells the client about the contact form today. Fix due Thursday.
4. We check how this is working on Monday.
```

The last line matters. A short check-in a week later catches problems while they are still small.

## When to involve a team lead or mentor

To **escalate** means to bring a problem to someone with more authority, such as a team lead, a project manager or a client's main contact. Escalating too early can feel like going behind someone's back, which means acting without telling them. Escalating too late can let the work, or a person, get hurt.

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| A normal work disagreement you have not discussed yet | Talk to the person directly first |
| You tried twice and nothing changed | Involve your team lead |
| A deadline or client delivery is at risk and you cannot agree | Involve your team lead |
| The decision is not yours to make, such as priorities or who owns what | Ask the person who decides |
| Insults, threats, harassment, or anything dishonest or unsafe | Go to your lead, a mentor or the fellowship team straight away |

**How to escalate well.** In a normal work conflict, tell the other person first: "I would like to ask Hiba to help us decide this." Bring facts, not labels. Say what you tried. And be clear about what you are asking for: a decision, advice, or a call with both of you. You are asking for help with the problem, not for someone to be punished.

In a case of insults, harassment or anything unsafe, you do not need to warn the other person or try to fix it alone first. Keep the messages and dates, and report it. You can report it to your team lead. You can also talk to a mentor or the fellowship team at any time, for example if you do not feel safe raising it with your lead, or if the lead is part of the problem. You do not have to handle it alone.

<Callout type="tip" title="A mentor is not only for escalation">
You can talk to a mentor before anything goes wrong. Asking "How should I raise this with my teammate?" is preparation, not reporting. A mentor can help you plan the words, practice the call, or see the other side.
</Callout>
