## The client who wants too many updates

Ahmed is a freelance developer. He is building a small data dashboard for an NGO. His client, Claire, is a program manager in Brussels.

In the second week, Claire starts asking for an update every morning. Some days she asks twice. Ahmed feels that she does not trust him. Each message also breaks his focus, and with power for only some hours a day, his focus time is precious. He starts to feel annoyed every time her name appears.

Ahmed asks. Claire explains that the NGO's donor wants a progress report every Friday, and the next part of the project's funding depends on it. Last year a different developer went silent for three weeks, and she nearly lost the grant. Her daily questions were not about Ahmed. They were about her own risk.

Nothing about the facts changed. But Ahmed's view of Claire did, and now the problem is much easier to solve.

## Empathy is not agreement

Empathy means understanding someone's view. It does **not** mean you must agree with it or give them what they want. Ahmed can understand Claire's fear and still say that two updates a day is too many. The difference is that now he can offer something that answers her real need.

## Why people see the same thing differently

Most disagreements at work are not about good people and bad people. People see different things because they stand in different places.

| What shapes a view | Example |
|---|---|
| Different information | The client knows about the donor report. The developer does not. |
| Different pressure | A team lead is judged on the date. A tester is judged on bugs found. |
| Different past experience | Someone who lost data once wants backups for everything. |
| Different communication style | Some people say "no" directly. Others say "maybe" and mean no. |
| Different situation | A teammate who works nights is not "slow" to reply in your morning. |

## Three habits that build empathy

**1. Ask open questions.** An open question cannot be answered with yes or no. "What worries you most about this?" and "What would a good result look like for you?" invite the other person to explain their needs.

**2. Say it back.** Repeat what you heard in your own words, then check: "So the main worry is the Friday donor report, and you need to know early if something is late. Is that right?" This shows you listened, and it gives them a chance to correct you. People often calm down once they feel understood.

**3. Assume a reasonable reason.** When someone's behavior annoys you, think of three reasons a reasonable person might act that way. A teammate did not reply for a day: maybe the power was out, maybe they were sick, maybe your message was unclear. You do not know yet, so do not decide yet.

Here is how Ahmed could reply after he understands Claire's need:

> "Thanks for explaining the donor report. That helps a lot. How about this: every Thursday at 14:00 your time I send a short written update with what is done, what is next and any risk. If anything puts Friday at risk, I will message you the same day. Would that work for you?"

He did not give in, and he did not argue. He answered her real worry in a way that protects his focus time.

<Callout type="warning" title="Empathy is not mind reading">
Do not tell people what they feel: "You are just afraid of change." Ask, then say back what they told you. A wrong guess said with confidence feels like an attack.
</Callout>
