## Write while it is fresh

After a role-play, your memory of the exact words fades fast. In a day or two you remember only a feeling: "it went fine" or "it was awkward". A feeling is hard to learn from. The exact moment is easy to learn from.

So try to write your reflection within one day of the session. If the power or the internet is out, write short notes on paper or in your phone's notes app, and type the full reflection later.

## Step 1: Collect your notes

Before you write, gather:

- **The observer's notes** about your rounds. Ask the observer to send them in your group chat right after the debrief.
- **Your own memory** of one sentence you said that worked, and one sentence you would change.
- **Your mentor's comments**, if a mentor played the other role.
- **Your recording**, if everyone agreed to record. Listen to your own round once.

## Step 2: Use the five parts

A good reflection answers five questions. Write two or three sentences for each.

| Part | What to write |
|---|---|
| 1. What happened | Which script, which role you played, and how the conversation ended |
| 2. What worked | One moment that went well, with your exact words if you can |
| 3. What was hard | One difficult moment, and what you felt or did |
| 4. What the observer saw | One thing from the debrief that you did not notice yourself |
| 5. What you will change | A specific sentence or action for next time, and a real situation to use it in |

Part 5 matters most. Before you write it, compare two versions.

## An example

Aya played Mahmoud, the developer, in Script B. Here is part of her reflection.

> **What was hard:** At minute 2, Ingrid said "It has to be in the launch." I felt pressure and said "OK, I will try." I promised before I understood anything.
>
> **What the observer saw:** Malik noticed that after my promise I started to speak faster and explained the technical work for two minutes. Ingrid only told me about the event on the 27th when I finally asked why the date mattered.
>
> **What I will change:** When a client pushes, I will first say "Help me understand what this needs to achieve." I have a call with a real client next Tuesday about a new page, and I will use that question there.

Aya does not describe herself as good or bad. She names moments, words and results. That is what makes her reflection useful for her and for her mentor.

## Honest, not perfect

Your mentor does not grade your acting. A round that went badly often gives the best reflection, because there is more to learn from it. Write about what really happened, including the awkward parts.

<Callout type="warning" title="If you could not hold the session yet">
Do not write about a session that did not happen. Tell your mentor what got in the way, such as power cuts, time zones or finding partners. They can help you arrange a session, or play a role themselves. Then come back and submit.
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="What your mentor looks for">
Specific moments with real words, an honest look at what was hard, one clear change, and a real situation where you will use it. Expect questions and suggestions, not a score.
</Callout>
