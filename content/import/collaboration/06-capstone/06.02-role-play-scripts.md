## How to use the scripts

Each script has a situation that everyone reads, a card for each of the two roles, and a card for the observer. The role cards contain private information. Before your round, read the situation and **only your own card**. The observer reads everything. Not knowing what the other person wants is part of the practice, just like at work.

Do not memorize lines. A role-play is not theater. Use your own words and react to what the other person really says.

### One round

| Step | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Prepare | 3 min | Read your card and plan your first sentence |
| Play | 10 min | Role A and role B talk. The observer takes notes in silence |
| Debrief | 7 min | The observer speaks first, then each player |

The observer keeps the time. When ten minutes end, stop, even if the conversation is not finished. Real conversations often end with "let's continue tomorrow", and an unfinished round still gives you plenty to discuss.

### Three rounds, three people

| Round | Script | Person 1 | Person 2 | Person 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A | Role A | Role B | Observer |
| 2 | B | Observer | Role A | Role B |
| 3 | A or B | Role B | Observer | Role A |

In round 3, person 3 chooses the script. Three rounds take about one hour.

## Script A: the missed handoffs

**The situation (everyone reads)**

Yasmin and Hamza are developers at a small startup that builds a booking app for clinics. They both live in Gaza. Their tester, Priya, works from India, about three hours ahead of them, so her morning starts before theirs.

The team has one clear rule: before you stop work each day, post a short written handoff in the team channel. It says what you finished, what is blocked and what comes next. Last week Hamza did not post his handoff on two days. On Wednesday, Priya did not know that Hamza's changes to the payment page were not ready. She tested the old version for two hours, and her bug report was useless.

Yasmin leads this feature. She has asked Hamza for a ten-minute call.

**Role A card: Yasmin, who gives the feedback**

Your goals:

- Open calmly and say why you asked for the call.
- Give the feedback with situation, behavior, impact (module 2). Use one example, not a list.
- Ask for Hamza's view, and listen to the answer.
- Agree one clear next step, and how you will both know it is working.

Avoid: "you always" or "you never", guessing his reasons, and bringing up old problems.

A possible first sentence:

> Thanks for making time, Hamza. I want to talk about the handoffs last week, because they affected Priya's testing.

**Role B card: Hamza, who receives the feedback**

Private information: last week the power in your area was off most evenings. You worked until your laptop battery died, and then you could not post. You felt bad, but you told nobody, because you did not want it to sound like an excuse. You are also a little hurt: you worked late every night.

Your goals:

- React like a real person. At first, be a little defensive, for example: "I was working every night, you know."
- Then notice the defensiveness and pause. Try the steps from module 3: thank her, ask a clarifying question, decide what to act on.
- Share your real situation if Yasmin listens.
- Agree a next step you can really keep, even with power cuts.

Avoid: agreeing to everything just to end the call, or staying defensive the whole time.

**Observer card**

Read the situation and both role cards. Write notes with the minute, for example "minute 3: ...". Write down exact words when you can. In the debrief, answer these questions:

1. Did Yasmin describe the situation, the behavior and the impact? Which words did she use?
2. Did she talk about what Hamza did, or about who he is?
3. When Hamza became defensive, what did each person do next? Did the call calm down or heat up?
4. Did Yasmin ask a real question and wait for the answer? Did Hamza's private situation come out?
5. Did they agree a next step that both can keep, including on days without power?

## Script B: the extra feature

**The situation (everyone reads)**

Mahmoud is a freelance developer. He is building a volunteer sign-up web app for an NGO. His client is Ingrid, a program manager at the NGO's office in Oslo, Norway. The contract covers three things: a sign-up form, a volunteer list for staff, and email confirmations. Delivery is on the 20th of the month.

Today is the 14th. Mahmoud has about five working days left and is on schedule. This morning Ingrid wrote: "We also need SMS reminders for volunteers before each shift. Please add them for the launch on the 20th." SMS reminders are not in the contract. Mahmoud thinks they need three to four days of work and a paid SMS service.

Ingrid has asked for a ten-minute call.

**Role A card: Mahmoud, the developer**

Private information: you cannot add SMS by the 20th without dropping testing or working at night, and power cuts make night work unreliable. You want to keep this client, because they may have more work next year. You would happily add SMS in a second phase for an extra fee.

Your goals:

- Stay friendly and show that you want to help.
- Ask questions to find Ingrid's interest: why SMS, and why by the 20th (module 5).
- Explain the trade honestly in scope, time and cost. Do not just say yes or no.
- Offer at least two options.
- End with a clear agreement, and say you will confirm it in writing.

Avoid: promising SMS by the 20th to escape an awkward moment, or opening with "That is not in the contract."

A possible first sentence:

> Thanks for the call, Ingrid. Before we plan anything, I'd like to understand the SMS idea better.

**Role B card: Ingrid, the client**

Private information: your **position** is "SMS must be in the launch". Your real **interest** is different. Last year, many volunteers forgot their shifts. Your first big event is on the 27th. Your director will look at the app on the 20th, but what really matters is that volunteers turn up on the 27th. Many volunteers rarely check email, but they always read SMS. You have a small extra budget, but you do not want to mention it at first.

Your goals:

- Start firm: "It has to be in the launch."
- If Mahmoud asks good questions, share your real reason, a little at a time.
- Mention your budget only if he asks about cost or options.
- Accept a fair option if it protects the event on the 27th.

Avoid: giving in at the first question, and being rude. Real clients are usually polite, but under pressure.

**Observer card**

Read the situation and both role cards. Write notes with the minute and exact words. In the debrief, answer these questions:

1. Did Mahmoud ask about Ingrid's reasons before he offered a solution? Which questions did he ask?
2. When did the event on the 27th come out? What helped it come out?
3. Did Mahmoud name the trade clearly: what changes in scope, time or cost?
4. How many options came up? Did any of them serve both sides?
5. Did the call end with a clear agreement, and who will write it down?

## Running the debrief

The debrief is where most of the learning happens. Before you start, think about what kind of observer note helps most.

```predict
prompt: "You are the observer. Which note will help the players most in the debrief?"
options:
  - id: general
    label: "You sounded nervous at the start, and later you came across as a bit unfriendly"
  - id: specific
    label: "At minute 4 you said 'you never post', and Hamza stopped talking"
correctOptionId: specific
explanation: "A specific note gives the moment, the exact words and what happened next. The player can see it and change it. A general label only makes people defensive."
```

The observer gives feedback the way you learned in module 2: describe what you saw and heard, and what happened next. Talk about actions, not about the person. Start with one thing that worked well, then one or two things to try differently.

Then each player answers two questions: "What is one thing I did that worked?" and "What is one sentence I would say differently?" Keep it kind and short. Everyone in the room is practicing.

<Callout type="warning" title="Keep it practice">
If a script is too close to a real conflict you are in right now, choose the other script or change the details. If anyone becomes really upset, stop the round and take a break. Nobody has to share private stories in the debrief.
</Callout>

## Doing it with a mentor

If you cannot find two other fellows, a mentor can play role B (Hamza or Ingrid) while you play role A. There is no separate observer, so take two minutes after the round to answer the observer questions together. Your mentor can tell you what they felt as the other person, which is useful feedback that an observer cannot give.

If time allows, swap roles and run the other script.

## Recording, only with consent

A recording can help you write your reflection in chapter 6.3. Record only if **everyone** in the call agrees first. Audio is enough, and it uses less data than video. Share the recording only with your mentor.

## Check your understanding

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "In Script A, the person playing Hamza says: 'I was working every night, you know.' What is the most useful next move for Yasmin?"
options:
  - "Repeat the impact on Priya's testing, so he sees it is serious"
  - "Say she knows he works hard, then ask what made handoffs difficult"
  - "Say working late is not the point, because the rule is the rule"
  - "Move straight to the next step so the call stays short and positive"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "Repeating the impact before listening sounds like she did not hear him. His defensiveness will likely grow."
  - "Right. She accepts his effort without dropping the feedback, then asks a real question. This is how his power situation can come out."
  - "This may be true, but it ignores his feelings and closes the conversation. He will not share what really happened."
  - "A next step agreed before she understands the cause will likely fail. She needs to know why the handoffs were missed."
```
