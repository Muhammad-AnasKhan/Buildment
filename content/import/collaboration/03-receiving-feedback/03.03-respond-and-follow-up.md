## The message from Toronto

Noor is a freelance mobile developer. Her client, Laura, runs a small online shop in Toronto, usually seven hours behind Gaza. Noor wakes up to this message:

> The app feels slow. My team is not happy with it.

Noor has already learned to pause. Now she needs to reply. A simple pattern helps: **thank, ask, decide, show**.

## Step 1: Thank

Start with a short, real thank you. Giving criticism is uncomfortable for many people, and Laura took the time to tell Noor instead of quietly looking for another developer.

Keep it simple. "Thanks for telling me early" sounds sincere. "Thank you SO much for this amazing feedback!!" sounds forced. And thanking someone is not the same as agreeing with them. You are thanking them for the information, not promising to accept every word.

## Step 2: Ask a clarifying question

"The app feels slow" is hard to act on. Slow where? On which phone? Since when? A clarifying question turns vague feedback into something you can fix.

In module 2 you used **situation, behavior, impact** to give feedback. You can use the same pattern to ask for it:

| Part | A question you can ask |
|---|---|
| Situation | "Where or when did you notice this?" |
| Behavior | "What exactly did you see? Can you show me an example?" |
| Impact | "What problem did it cause for you or your customers?" |

Two more questions are often useful: "Is this a must-change or a suggestion?" and "What would good look like to you?" Ask one or two questions, not ten. Too many questions can feel like a test.

Noor writes:

> Hi Laura, thanks for telling me early. Which screens feel slow, and on which phone? A short screen recording would help a lot. I'll look at it as soon as I have it.

Laura sends a recording: the product list takes about six seconds to open.

## Step 3: Decide what to act on

Not every piece of feedback is right, and you do not have to accept every word. But you do have to decide. Ignoring feedback is also a reply, and people notice it.

| Decision | When it fits | What you might say |
|---|---|---|
| **Act now** | It is clear and you agree | "I'll fix this today." |
| **Act later** | You agree, but it is a big change | "I'll do this in the next version, by Thursday." |
| **Discuss** | You disagree, or you see a problem with the change | "I see it differently. Can we talk for 10 minutes?" |

When a client or team lead makes the final call, you can still share your view once, clearly and with a reason. Then accept their decision or agree on a next step. Module 4 goes deeper into disagreeing well.

Noor decides to act now. She finds the cause: the list loads all 2,000 product images at once. She changes it to load 20 at a time.

## Step 4: Show the change

The last step is to **close the loop**. This phrase means finishing a conversation by reporting back, so nobody is left wondering what happened. Noor writes:

> Hi Laura, thanks again for the recording. The product list was loading all 2,000 images at once. Now it loads 20 at a time, and on my test phone it opens in about one second. Could your team try it today and tell me if it feels better?

Following up also works for feedback about behavior, not only work. A few weeks after a team lead says your daily updates are too long, you might write:

> Last month you said my updates were too long. I've been using three short bullet points since then. Is that working better for you?

This kind of message is rare, so people remember it. It tells them you listened, and it makes them more willing to help you again.

## Check your understanding

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Your team lead writes under your design mockup (a picture of how the screens will look): 'This doesn't feel right yet.' Which reply is most likely to get you useful feedback?"
options:
  - "What do you mean? It follows the style guide we all agreed on last month."
  - "Thanks. Which screen feels most wrong to you, and what did you expect there?"
  - "Thanks for looking. I'll try a completely new style and send you three versions tomorrow."
  - "Sorry it isn't good. I'll keep changing it until it feels right to you."
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "The question is a good start, but the second sentence defends the work. It sounds like a challenge, not a wish to understand."
  - "Right. It thanks the lead and asks for a specific place and their expectation, which turns a vague feeling into something you can act on."
  - "This is a lot of work based on a guess. Without knowing what feels wrong, the three new versions may have the same problem."
  - "This is folding. It apologizes but asks nothing, so you may keep guessing for days."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "A reviewer leaves five comments on your pull request (a request to add your code to the main project). Three are clear bugs. One is a naming idea marked 'optional'. One asks for a change you believe will make the app slower. What is the best plan?"
options:
  - "Make all five changes exactly as written, so the reviewer sees that you accept feedback well."
  - "Fix the three bugs now, and leave the other two comments without a reply since they are not bugs."
  - "Reply to the speed comment first, and fix nothing until that discussion is completely settled."
  - "Fix the bugs, decide on the naming idea, and explain your speed concern with a reason."
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "Accepting everything without thought can make the code slower. A good reviewer would rather hear your concern."
  - "Silence on two comments looks like you ignored them. Even 'optional' comments deserve a short answer."
  - "The bugs are clear and do not depend on the speed discussion. Waiting delays work that could be done today."
  - "Right. You act on what is clear, make your own call on the optional idea, and raise your concern about speed openly."
```

## Practice: write a reply

```openquestion
prompt: "Mazen, a coordinator at an NGO, sends you this message about a volunteer sign-up form you built: 'The form is confusing. Two volunteers called us today because they did not know if they had registered.' Write your reply to Mazen. Include a thank you, one or two clarifying questions, what you will do and by when, and how you will follow up."
required: true

modelAnswer: "Hi Mazen, thank you for telling me, and sorry for the extra calls. Two questions so I can fix the right thing: did the volunteers reach the last page, and were they using phones or computers? While I wait, I will add a clear 'You are registered' message and a confirmation email after sign-up. I can have this ready by Wednesday. I will send you a link to test it, and next week I will check with you whether the calls have stopped."
```
