## A week of work, one sentence

Reem writes reports for an NGO that runs education projects. She spent a week on a report for donors (the people and groups who give the NGO money). In a video call, her manager Mariam says:

> This report is too long. Donors won't read it.

Reem feels a rush of heat. Many thoughts arrive at once: "Every section is needed." "Mariam has never written one of these." "Maybe I am just a bad writer."

All three thoughts feel true in the moment. None of them helps Reem find out what Mariam actually needs.

## Why criticism hits so hard

In their book *Thanks for the Feedback*, Douglas Stone and Sheila Heen describe three kinds of triggers. A trigger is something that starts a strong reaction.

| Trigger | What it sounds like inside | Reem's version |
|---|---|---|
| **Truth** | "That is wrong." | "Every section is needed." |
| **Relationship** | "Who are you to tell me?" | "She has never written one of these." |
| **Identity** | "I am bad at this." | "Maybe I am just a bad writer." |

Knowing the three triggers helps you name what is happening. Once you can name it, you can ask a calmer question: "Is there something useful here?"

## The signs of defensiveness

Defensiveness usually shows up in your body first, then in your thoughts, then in your words and actions.

| Where | Common signs |
|---|---|
| Body | Hot face, fast heartbeat, tight chest or stomach |
| Thoughts | "They don't understand." "That's unfair." "I'm useless." |
| Words | "Yes, but...", long explanations, blaming tools or other people |
| Actions | Replying within seconds, going silent, or redoing everything in a panic |

Notice that defensiveness goes in two directions. Some people **fight**: they argue, justify and point at others. Other people **fold**: they agree with everything, apologize again and again, and attack themselves. Folding looks polite, so it is easy to miss. But it also stops you from understanding the feedback, because you are busy with the bad feeling instead of the facts.

## Pause before you reply

Remote work gives you one advantage: most feedback arrives in writing, so you usually have time. Use it.

1. **Name it.** Say to yourself: "I feel defensive right now." Naming the feeling helps many people step back from it and choose what to do.
2. **Wait.** If the message is not urgent, wait at least 20 minutes, or until the next morning. If it helps, write your angry reply in a notes app. Do not send it.
3. **Separate the work from yourself.** "This report is too long" is about the report. It does not say "Reem is a bad writer."
4. **Look for the useful part.** Even unfair or badly worded criticism often has something true in it. Ask yourself: "If a small part of this is right, which part?"

In a live call you cannot wait 20 minutes. A short holding sentence gives you time without closing the conversation:

> "Thank you. Can you say a bit more about that?"

> "That's useful. Let me think about it and come back to you tomorrow."

<Callout type="warning" title="Hard days make it harder">
After a power cut, a long day of study and work, or worrying news, everyone has less patience. That is human. On those days, be kind to yourself and give your reply more time if you can.
</Callout>

## Common questions

**Q: What if the criticism is unfair?**
A: Pausing is not agreeing. You can understand first and disagree later, calmly and with reasons. Module 4 shows you how to disagree without damaging the relationship.

**Q: What if someone criticizes me rudely or in front of others?**
A: How something is said and what is said are two different things. You can take the useful part, and later, in private, tell the person how the delivery felt. If criticism turns into insults or repeated humiliation, it is no longer feedback. Talk to a mentor or someone you trust.

**Q: My voice shakes or my eyes fill with tears. Is that unprofessional?**
A: It is a normal human reaction. You can say "Give me a moment" and take a breath. Most people respect that more than a fast, angry answer.
