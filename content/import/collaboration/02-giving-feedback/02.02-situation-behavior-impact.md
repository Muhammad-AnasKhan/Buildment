## Hana tries again

In chapter 2.1, Hana stayed silent while Karim kept sharing design files late. She decides to speak. Her first draft says:

> Karim, you are not reliable with deadlines and it is really unprofessional.

She reads it again and stops. Karim will feel attacked. He will probably defend himself, and nothing will change. She needs a way to say the same thing that he can actually use.

## Where the pattern comes from

The Center for Creative Leadership, a leadership training organization in the United States, teaches a pattern called **Situation, Behavior, Impact**, or **SBI**. It is now used in many companies around the world. It has three parts:

| Part | Question it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | When and where did it happen? | "In last Sunday's planning call" |
| **Behavior** | What exactly did the person do or say? | "you shared the designs on Monday, not Sunday" |
| **Impact** | What effect did it have? | "I had to build the page in one day instead of two" |

Each part does a job. The situation tells the person which moment you mean, so they do not have to guess. The behavior tells them what to repeat or change. The impact tells them why it matters.

## Behavior: what a camera would record

The hardest part is the behavior. Most people describe their opinion of the person, not what the person did. Ask yourself: "Would a video camera record this?"

```predict
prompt: "Which sentence describes behavior, in the SBI sense?"
options:
  - id: judgment
    label: "You were careless with the release notes"
  - id: behavior
    label: "The release notes did not mention the new login step"
correctOptionId: behavior
explanation: "A camera could show that the login step is missing from the notes. It cannot record 'careless'. That word is a judgment about the person, and people argue with judgments."
```

Words like **lazy, careless, rude, unprofessional** or **not a team player** are labels. The person will defend themselves against the label and miss the point. Describe the action instead: what they wrote, said, sent or did not send.

Also avoid "always" and "never". "You are always late" is almost never exactly true, so the person can argue with it. Name the specific times.

## Impact: say what happened, not what they meant

For the impact, describe the effect on you, the team, the client or the work. Do not guess why the person did it. "You don't care about this project" is a guess about their intention, and you may be wrong. Perhaps Karim had no power for two days, or he has exams. The impact is what you know for sure.

## Then ask and listen

SBI opens a conversation. It does not end it. After you give the three parts, ask a question and listen to the answer. Here is Hana's new message:

> Hi Karim, can I share something about the website work? In the last three handoffs, the design files arrived a day after the date we agreed. Each time I had to build the page in one evening instead of two, and I made more mistakes. Is something making those dates hard for you? I'd like us to find dates that work for both of us.

It is longer than the first draft, but every sentence is useful. Karim knows which moments she means, what to change, why it matters, and that she wants to solve the problem with him.

## SBI works for praise too

Specific praise teaches people what to repeat. "Great work" tells them nothing. Compare:

> In yesterday's client call, you summarized the three open bugs in one minute before the client asked. The client said it was the clearest update she has had this month.

<Callout type="tip" title="Timing and channel">
Give feedback soon, while people still remember the moment. Give correction in private, never in a group chat. In a remote team, a written message is fine for small points. For harder feedback, offer a short call at a time that suits both time zones.
</Callout>
