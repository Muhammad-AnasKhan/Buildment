## The message in the group chat

The night before an exam, a message arrives in Maryam's study group. Her friend Nour forwarded it with three excited faces.

> FORWARDED MANY TIMES. Students, read this before your exam! A famous professor has proved that students who chew mint gum during an exam score 40 percent higher. Teachers do not want you to know this, because then everyone would pass. My cousin tried it and got full marks. Share with every student you care about!

Nour writes: "I am buying gum for all of us tomorrow!" Two more friends reply with hearts.

Maryam reads it again, slowly. She does not say it is false. Chewing gum is cheap and probably harmless. But she notices that the message is full of strong words and empty of things she can check.

- "A famous professor." Which professor? It gives no name.
- "Has proved." Where is the study? Who checked it?
- "40 percent higher." Higher than what? Than students who did not chew gum? In which exam?
- "My cousin got full marks." That is one person. Maybe the cousin studied well.
- "Teachers do not want you to know." Why would every teacher hide it? This line makes the message feel secret and exciting, but it is not a reason.

Maryam writes back kindly: "Gum is fine if you like it! But this message does not say who did the study or where to find it. I would not count on it. I am counting on our revision tonight." Nour laughs and agrees to join the revision.

## Why this matters

Every day, you meet messages that want you to believe something or do something: buy this, share this, pay this, trust this. Some have good reasons behind them. Some only sound good. The weak ones often use the same few tricks, again and again.

In this module, you learn to look inside a message. You split it into its claim, its reasons and its evidence. You learn the names of six common traps, so you can spot them quickly. You check job and scholarship offers for the signs of a scam. And you practice what Maryam did at the end: disagreeing politely, without making anyone feel foolish.

You do not need to win arguments. You need to see clearly what a message really gives you, and reply calmly.

## Key words

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | What someone wants you to believe or do |
| **Reason** | Why they say it is true, often after the word "because" |
| **Evidence** | Facts you could check that support a reason |
| **Weak argument** | An argument where the reasons do not really support the claim |
| **Scam** | A trick to take your money, your data or your work |
| **Fair summary** | Saying someone's view in a way they would agree with |
