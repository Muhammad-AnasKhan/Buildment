## A message for Basel

Basel is looking for a way to study abroad. One evening, this message arrives.

> Congratulations, Basel! You have been selected for the Bright Future Scholarship. Study abroad for free and receive 2,000 dollars every month. To keep your place, pay a 50 dollar processing fee today. This offer ends at midnight! Reply to our officer's personal email with a photo of your ID and your email password, so we can set up your student account.

The Bright Future Scholarship is a made-up scholarship. Basel's heart jumps. This is exactly what he hoped for. Then he remembers one small thing.

## Five warning signs

Scam messages change their words, but they use the same few tricks. Here are five signs to look for in any job or scholarship offer.

1. **You must pay to get paid.** A fee to "keep your place", "process your file" or "buy your training kit" before you earn anything.
2. **Pressure to act today.** "Offer ends at midnight." "Only two places left." Pressure stops you from checking.
3. **Too much money for little work.** Big pay for a few easy tasks, or a prize you did not apply for.
4. **A personal contact, not an official one.** A free email account with random numbers, or a private phone number, instead of an official address.
5. **Asking for passwords or ID copies too early.** A password is never needed. An ID copy should come only after you know the offer is real.

One sign is a reason to slow down and check. Several signs together almost always mean a scam.

## The worked example

Now Basel checks his message line by line.

| Line in the message | Warning sign | What Basel should do |
|---|---|---|
| "Pay a 50 dollar processing fee today" | Pay to get paid | Do not pay anything yet |
| "This offer ends at midnight" | Pressure to act today | Slow down. A real offer allows time to check |
| "Free study and 2,000 dollars a month" | Too much for little work | Ask: did I apply? What did I do to earn this? |
| "Reply to our officer's personal email" | Personal contact | Find the official contact himself |
| "Your ID and your email password" | Password and ID too early | Never send a password. Hold the ID copy |

The message shows all five signs. Basel does not reply. Instead, he takes calm steps that work for any offer.

- **Pause.** Do not pay, click a link or send anything yet.
- **Ask two questions.** Did I apply for this? Does the money fit the work?
- **Find the organisation yourself.** Look for its official contact on your own, not through the details in the message. Ask them if the offer is real.
- **Ask someone you trust.** A mentor, a teacher or a friend who has applied before can often spot a scam in a minute.

Basel searches for the scholarship on his own and finds nothing official at all. He shows the message to his mentor, who confirms it is a well-known kind of scam. Basel deletes it and warns his friends.

<Callout type="warning" title="Never share a password">
No real job, scholarship or company needs your password. If anyone asks for it, stop. Share an ID copy only after you have checked that the offer is real, and only through an official channel.
</Callout>

## Common mistake

Many people trust a message because of something that is easy to fake. A message may use your name, a smart logo or an official-sounding title. Friends in a group chat may say they got the same offer. None of this shows the offer is real. Scammers can copy names and logos, and they send the same message to many people at once.

It also helps to know what a real offer usually looks like. It comes after you applied, or from someone you can check. It uses an official contact. It gives you time to think and to ask questions. It explains the work and the pay clearly. It asks for documents only through an official process, and it never asks for a password.

Another mistake is thinking an offer is safe because it shows only one sign. Some scams are careful. They pay well, sound calm and wait until later to ask for a fee. Treat any single sign as a reason to check. Use the calm steps every time money or personal details are involved.
