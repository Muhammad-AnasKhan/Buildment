## The chat goes quiet

Nour writes in the study group: "Group study is a waste of time. I learn more alone." Omar replies at once: "That is a silly idea."

Nobody writes anything for an hour. Nour does not come to the next session. Omar may even be right that group study helps. But his reply changed nobody's mind. It only ended the conversation.

## Three steps

Disagreeing well is not about being soft. You still say clearly what you think. You just do it in an order that helps the other person listen.

1. **Say their view fairly.** Put their view in your own words, the way they would put it. Test it: would they say, "Yes, that is what I mean"? If you are not sure, ask.
2. **Reply with a reason.** First agree with any part that is true. Then give your reason, and your evidence if you have some.
3. **Ask or offer.** End with a question, or a small next step you could both accept.

For Omar, it could sound like this: "You feel you focus better alone, and our sessions often turn into chatting. That is fair, it does happen. But when I explain a topic to someone, I remember it better. Could we try one session next week with a clear plan and a time limit?"

Nour may still say no. But now she is answering a real reason, not a put-down.

## The worked example

Khaled is a freelancer. His uncle says: "Freelancing is not a real job. You should take an office job." Khaled feels annoyed. Here is how he works through the three steps.

**Step 1: a fair summary.** What is his uncle really worried about? Probably money that comes every month. Khaled writes: "You worry that freelance work is not steady, and that an office job pays every month."

**Step 2: a reason with evidence.** He agrees with the true part first, then gives his reason. "That is a real risk. For the last six months I have had two steady clients, and I keep enough savings for two months without work."

**Step 3: ask or offer.** "Can I show you my income for the last six months? If it drops for three months in a row, I will look for an office job too."

Compare a reply Khaled nearly sent: "You do not understand how work is done now." That reply attacks the person, a trap from chapter 6.3. It gives the uncle no reason and nothing to look at.

The same steps work when you reply to a weak claim, such as a forwarded message. Name what is missing, not what is wrong with the person: "Thanks for sharing. I am not sure about it, because it does not say who did the study. Do you know where it came from?"

## Words that help

Small changes in wording make a big difference to how a reply lands.

| Instead of | Try |
|---|---|
| "You are wrong." | "I see it differently, because..." |
| "That is a silly idea." | "I am not sure that works. Can you say more?" |
| "Everyone knows that." | "What makes you sure?" |
| "So you think we should all fail?" | "Do you mean...? I want to check I understood." |

The left side closes the conversation. The right side keeps it open and keeps it about the idea, not the person.

<Callout type="tip" title="Private is often kinder">
If someone shares a weak claim in a big group, a private message can be kinder than a public reply. They can change their mind without feeling embarrassed in front of everyone.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

The first mistake is a fake summary. "So you think we should never study together?" sounds like a summary, but it is the trap "twisting their words". A fair summary is one the other person would agree with.

The second mistake is thinking polite means giving up. Being polite is about your tone, not about agreeing. You can be kind and still say, "I see it differently, and here is why."

Last, remember that you do not have to reply to everything. If a claim is harmless and the person is not asking for your view, sometimes the best move is simply not to share it further. Save your careful replies for claims that could cost someone money, health or a chance.
