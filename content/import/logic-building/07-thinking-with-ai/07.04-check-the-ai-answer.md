## Fifteen minutes of internet

Huda wants to apply for the Bright Path Scholarship (a made-up scholarship). She has about fifteen minutes of internet before the connection drops again. She does not want to search through many pages. So she asks an AI chat tool two plain questions: what is the deadline, and how much does it pay each month?

The answer comes back fast and sure. In this chapter you follow what Huda does next.

<BigWordAlert term="Source" plainEnglish="The place a fact really comes from: the page, document or person that first said it." whyItMatters="If you cannot find the source, you cannot check the fact. A name of a source is not a source." />

## Three checks

You do not need special tools to check an AI answer. You need three habits, and you already know most of them from this course.

1. **Check the rough numbers.** Do the numbers in the answer agree with each other? Do they fit what you already know? This is the sanity check from module 5.
2. **Find the source.** Ask where the fact comes from. Then go and look at the source yourself. A name, such as "the official website", is only a claim.
3. **Solve it another way.** Work it out by a different route, or confirm it in a second place that does not depend on the first. If two different ways agree, you can trust the answer much more.

Each check catches different problems. That is why you use more than one. Before you step through Huda's story, try the first check yourself.

## What each check found

**Check 1, the rough numbers,** found that something was wrong. 2,000 dollars a month for 12 months is about 24,000 dollars, not 2,400. But this check could not say which number was wrong. Was it the monthly pay, or the total? Rough numbers can tell you "stop", but not always "why".

**Check 2, the source,** happened in two moves. First Huda asked the tool where the facts came from. The tool said "the official scholarship website", but it could not show the exact words. That is a name, not a source. So Huda made the second move. She opened the organisation's own website herself, not a forwarded message or a copy on another site.

The page said something different: the deadline is **1 March**, not 15 March. The pay is **200 dollars per month** for 12 months.

**Check 3, solve it another way,** tested the page's own numbers. 200 dollars a month for 12 months is 2,400 dollars. That matches the total. Now two things agree: the page's monthly pay and its total. Huda writes 1 March in her calendar.

Notice something important about the deadline. The rough numbers could never have found it. A date does not need to add up with anything. "15 March" sounded just as normal as "1 March". Only the source could show the real date. If Huda had trusted the AI, she would have missed the deadline by two weeks.

Real sources are not perfect either. A page can be old, hard to find or wrong. When a source and an AI tool disagree, trust the organisation's own, most recent page. If it is still unclear, ask the organisation directly.

## A second example: solve it another way

"Solve it another way" works well for sums. Tamer asks an AI chat tool: "I spend 15 shekels a week on phone credit. How much is that in a month?" The tool says: "About 45 shekels a month."

Tamer does not ask the tool again. He works it out by a different route. He adds four weeks: 15 + 15 + 15 + 15 = 60 shekels. A month is a little more than four weeks, so the real answer is about 60 to 65 shekels. The tool's 45 is too low. It looks like it counted only three weeks.

His second route was slow and simple. That is fine. A different route catches slips that the first route hid.

<Callout type="info" title="A name is not a source">
"Studies show", "experts say" and "the official website" are names, not sources. A source is something you can open and read yourself: the page, the notice, the document. If you cannot find it, treat the fact as unchecked.
</Callout>

## A common mistake

The most common mistake is to ask the same tool: "Are you sure?" The tool may say "Yes" in the same calm voice. Or it may change its answer. Neither reply tells you which answer is true. Asking the same tool again is not "another way". It is the same way twice.

A second mistake is to stop after one check. Huda's rough numbers found a problem with the money, but they could not find the wrong date. Use the checks together, and start with the cheapest one.
