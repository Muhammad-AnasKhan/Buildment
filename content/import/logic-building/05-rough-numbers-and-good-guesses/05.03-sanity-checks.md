## 8,000 cups of tea

Tamer reads a post in his training group: "What a study day! Our 40 students drank 8,000 cups of tea." Everyone sends a smiling face. Tamer stops and does one small sum. 8,000 cups shared by 40 students is 200 cups each. Nobody drinks 200 cups of tea in one day.

So the number cannot be right. Maybe someone typed two extra zeros, and the real number was 80, which is 2 cups each. Tamer did not need to know the real number to see that 8,000 was wrong. That is a sanity check.

## Three quick checks

You do not need to redo every sum to check a number. Three quick questions catch most big mistakes. Before you read them, try one yourself.

**1. Is it the right size?** Before you look closely, ask if the answer should be in the tens, the hundreds, the thousands or the millions. This is its <GlossaryTerm term="order of size" definition="Roughly how big a number is: tens, hundreds, thousands or millions. Two numbers of the same order of size are within about ten times of each other." />. A class of 25 students who each bring 4 notebooks have about 100 notebooks, not 1,000. If an answer is ten times too big or too small, this check catches it.

**2. Are the units right?** A unit is what the number counts: minutes or hours, shekels or dollars, per day or per month, per person or in total. A correct sum with the wrong unit is still a wrong answer. "50" means very different things as 50 minutes and 50 hours.

**3. Does it fit what you already know?** Compare the number with something you know well. The easiest way is to share it out: divide by the number of people, days or items. "8,000 cups for 40 students" becomes "200 cups each". "300 loaves for 5 people" becomes "60 loaves each". A shared-out number is small enough to picture, so you can feel if it is silly.

These three checks take seconds. They do not tell you the exact right answer. They tell you when a number is so wrong that you must not use it.

## The worked example: an AI answer

Maryam must type a report of 1,500 words. She types about 30 words a minute. She asks an AI chat tool how long the typing will take. It answers:

> 'At 30 words per minute, a 1,500-word report will take you about 50 hours to type.'

It sounds sure and it shows a sum. Maryam runs her three checks.

- **Does it fit?** 50 hours is more than two full days and nights of typing, with no sleep. For a report of a few pages, that feels far too long.
- **Right size?** She estimates from scratch. At 30 words a minute, 10 minutes gives 300 words. 1,500 words is 5 times 300, so it takes 5 times 10 minutes: about 50 minutes. The answer should be in tens of minutes, not tens of hours.
- **Units?** Now she sees the mistake. 1,500 / 30 = 50 is correct. But her speed is in words per **minute**, so the answer is 50 **minutes**. The AI tool did the sum right and gave the wrong unit.

Maryam now knows the typing takes under an hour. She can plan her evening. If she had trusted the first answer, she might have thought the report was too big to finish and asked for more time she did not need.

<Callout type="tip" title="Share it out">
When a big number surprises you, divide it by something you can picture: people, days, hours or items. A number per person or per day is much easier to judge than a total.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

A sanity check is quick, but people often forget it or expect too much from it. Watch for two mistakes.

- **Trusting a number because it sounds sure.** An exact number, a confident tone or a long sum does not make an answer right. The AI answer above sounded sure and was still wrong by sixty times.
- **Thinking a check proves a number right.** A sanity check catches numbers that are far off. If Maryam's answer had said 45 minutes or 60 minutes, it would pass the check too. To be sure of an exact number, you still need to redo the sum or find the source.

So use a sanity check as a filter. It throws out the impossible numbers. The numbers that pass still need care when the decision matters.
