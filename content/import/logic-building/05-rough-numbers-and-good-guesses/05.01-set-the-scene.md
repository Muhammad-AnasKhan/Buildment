## A question with no answer key

Rania makes paper bags at home. She wants to sell them to the bakery at the end of her street. Before she visits the baker, she wants to know one thing: about how many loaves of bread does the bakery sell in a day? If it sells 50, her idea is too small to matter. If it sells thousands, she needs a lot of paper.

She asks her friend Yousef. He laughs. "Nobody knows that. It could be anything."

Rania does not give up. She cannot count the real number, but she can make a few small guesses she understands.

- Her street and the streets around it have about 30 buildings. Each building has about 10 families. That is about 300 families.
- Not all of them buy bread here. Some go to another bakery. Say about 200 families buy here.
- A family buys about 5 loaves a day.

Then she multiplies: 200 families times 5 loaves is 1,000 loaves a day. She knows each guess could be off, so she gives a range: "Somewhere between 500 and 2,000 loaves a day. Most likely about 1,000."

When she visits, the baker smiles. "Not bad. On most days we sell about 1,200."

Rania's guess was not exact, and it did not need to be. It was the right size. It told her the idea was worth a visit, and it gave her a sensible first order of paper.

## Why a rough answer beats no answer

Many number questions in life have no answer key. How many people might buy your product? How long will this task take? Is this price sensible? You will not always have the real number. A rough answer, built from small guesses you can explain, still helps you decide.

Rough numbers do a second job too. They protect you from numbers that cannot be right. If a message, a person or an AI tool gives you a number, a quick rough check tells you if it is about the right size. You do not need hard maths. Adding, multiplying, dividing and good guesses are enough.

## Key words

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Estimate** | A rough answer built from small, sensible guesses |
| **Assumption** | One guess you treat as true for now, so you can keep going |
| **Round number** | An easy number to work with, such as 10, 50 or 1,000 |
| **Range** | A low guess and a high guess, with the real answer probably between them |
| **Sanity check** | A quick test to see if a number is about the right size |
| **Average** | The total shared out equally: add the numbers, then divide by how many there are |
