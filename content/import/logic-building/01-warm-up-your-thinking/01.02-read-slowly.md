## A bus puzzle

Here is a puzzle. Read it once, at your normal speed.

A bus leaves the station with 12 people on it. At the first stop, 3 people get off and 5 get on. At the second stop, 6 get off and 2 get on. At the third stop, 4 people get on. How many stops did the bus make?

Most people start counting passengers: 12, then 14, then 10, then 14. But the question did not ask about passengers. It asked about stops. The answer is 3.

If you counted passengers, you are in good company. Your brain saw numbers and started adding before it finished reading. This chapter is about slowing down at the start, so you answer the question that was really asked.

## Three ways a question can fool you

Before you read about the tricks, try one more puzzle. Read it slowly, word by word.

```predict

  - id: four
    label: "4 bags"
  - id: eleven
    label: "11 bags"
correctOptionId: four
explanation: "'All but 4' means all of them except 4. She sold 11 bags, and 4 are left. A fast reader sees 15 and 4 and subtracts."
```

Puzzles like these use the same few tricks again and again. Once you know them, you start to see them everywhere.

1. **The question asks something you did not expect.** The bus puzzle gives you numbers about passengers, then asks about stops. The fix is to find the question sentence and read it first, or read it twice.
2. **Extra details that do not matter.** Some numbers are there only to pull you away. These details are <GlossaryTerm term="noise" definition="Details in a question that do not help you answer it." />. You do not have to use every number you see.
3. **Small words that change the meaning.** Words such as "all but", "more than", "at least", "not" and "each" are short, but they decide the answer. In Rania's puzzle, "all but 4" means "all except 4".

These three tricks have one cure. Know exactly what is asked before you start working.

## The read-slowly habit

Here is the habit in three short steps. It feels slow at first. After a few puzzles it becomes automatic, like looking both ways before you cross a road.

1. **Find the question.** Read the question sentence on its own. What exactly does it ask for: a number of people, a time, a price, a name?
2. **Say it in your own words.** For example: "They want the number of stops, not people."
3. **Check your answer against the question.** When you finish, read the question again. Does your answer match what it asked, in the right unit?

The first two steps happen before any adding or subtracting. That is the point: most trick questions are lost in the first few seconds, not in the sums.

## A worked example: Maryam's bus

Let us use the habit on a puzzle with more numbers.

Maryam leaves home at 8:40 to catch a bus that should come at 9:10. The walk to the bus stop takes 15 minutes. On the way, she stops at a shop for 10 minutes. The bus is 5 minutes late. How many minutes does Maryam wait at the bus stop?

**Step 1, find the question.** It asks how long she waits at the bus stop. Not how long her trip takes, and not how late the bus is.

**Step 2, say it in your own words.** "From the moment she reaches the stop to the moment the bus arrives."

**Step 3, work it out.** She walks for 15 minutes and shops for 10 minutes, so she reaches the stop 25 minutes after 8:40. That is 9:05. The bus should come at 9:10, but it is 5 minutes late, so it comes at 9:15. From 9:05 to 9:15 is 10 minutes.

**Step 4, check.** Read the question again: minutes waiting at the stop. The answer is 10 minutes. As a second check, add up her whole morning: 15 minutes walking, 10 shopping and 10 waiting make 35 minutes. 8:40 plus 35 minutes is 9:15, exactly when the bus comes. Everything fits.

A fast reader might say 5 minutes, because the bus is 5 minutes late. Or 30 minutes, from 8:40 to 9:10. Both use real numbers from the puzzle. Both answer a different question.

## A common mistake

The most common mistake is not bad maths. It is answering the question you expected instead of the question on the page. A close second is using every number just because it is there.

In real life, extra details are everywhere. A job message may describe the company's history, its office and its awards. The part you need may be one line: what is the pay, and when is the deadline? Reading slowly means finding that line.

<Callout type="tip" title="Cover the numbers">
If a puzzle has many numbers, cover them with your thumb and read only the words first. Once you know what is asked, look at the numbers and choose the ones you need.
</Callout>
