```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Tamer says: 'This phone has the biggest battery in the shop, so it will last the longest each day.' What hidden assumption must be true for his claim to work?"
options:
  - "The phone really has the biggest battery in the shop"
  - "Tamer will charge the phone every single night"
  - "The phones all use power at about the same speed"
  - "Phones with big batteries cost more than others"
correct: 2
explanations:
  - "Tamer already said this. It is his stated reason, not a hidden assumption."
  - "Flip it: even if he charges it less often, the biggest battery could still last longest. This is not the bridge."
  - "Right. Flip it: if this phone uses power much faster, for example with a bright, large screen, a big battery may not last longest."
  - "Price is not part of the bridge from 'biggest battery' to 'lasts longest'. Flip it, and the claim still stands."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Amal sews clothes for customers. She says: 'Customers stopped ordering because my new prices are too high.' Her prices rose in the same month that a new tailor opened nearby. What is the best first step to test her assumption?"
options:
  - "Ask a few past customers why they stopped"
  - "Lower all her prices for the next six months"
  - "Ask her friends whether the new prices seem high"
  - "Wait to see if orders come back on their own"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. This is small and cheap, and it can tell her if the reason is the price or the new tailor."
  - "This is a big, slow change. It also cannot tell her whether price or the new tailor was the real reason."
  - "Her friends are not the customers who stopped ordering, so their view does not test her assumption."
  - "Waiting tests nothing. She would still not know why customers stopped."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "In this example, a town finds that neighbourhoods with more bakeries also have more babies born each year. What is the most likely explanation?"
options:
  - "Bread from bakeries helps families have more children"
  - "New babies make families open more bakeries nearby"
  - "It must be chance, because the link makes no sense"
  - "More people live there, so there is more of both"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "This treats the link as a cause without asking about a third factor."
  - "This is reverse cause, but babies do not open bakeries. A simpler explanation fits."
  - "A link that seems strange is not always chance. Here a clear third factor explains it."
  - "Right. The number of people is a third factor. More people means more bakeries and more babies."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "A report says shops with more workers have more customers. Basel decides to hire two extra workers so that more customers will come. What is the main problem with his reasoning?"
options:
  - "Two extra workers will cost him too much money"
  - "Busy shops may hire more workers, not the reverse"
  - "Workers in bigger shops may be better trained"
  - "The report did not say which city the shops are in"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "Cost matters for his decision, but it is not the problem with his reasoning about cause."
  - "Right. This is reverse cause. Shops that already have many customers need more workers, so workers may not bring customers."
  - "This may be true, but it does not explain why more workers and more customers go together."
  - "The city does not change the main problem: the cause may run the other way."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "A chart compares two bus companies. Out of 100 trips, Company A was on time for 90 and Company B for 93. The axis starts at 88, so B's bar looks more than twice as tall. What is the honest summary?"
options:
  - "B was on time for 3 more trips out of every 100"
  - "B is more than twice as reliable as A"
  - "A is late more than twice as often as B"
  - "The chart is false, so we can ignore the numbers"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. 93 minus 90 is 3. The bars look very different only because the axis starts at 88."
  - "This trusts the bar heights. Above 88, the bars show 2 and 5, which is why B looks over twice as tall."
  - "A is late on 10 trips and B on 7. That is not twice as often."
  - "The numbers are true. Only the picture is misleading, so read the numbers and say them plainly."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Maryam asked an AI tool: 'Does sitting in the front row improve marks?' It answered: 'Yes. Students who sit in the front row get higher marks, so sitting in the front row causes higher marks.' What should Maryam check first?"
options:
  - "Whether the answer is long enough to trust"
  - "Whether another AI tool gives the same answer"
  - "Whether the back row is better for quiet study"
  - "Whether keen students choose the front row"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "Length is not evidence. A short answer can be right and a long one can be wrong."
  - "Another tool may make the same jump. Two answers that agree still need a reason you can check."
  - "This changes the question. The problem is how the answer jumped from a link to a cause."
  - "Right. The answer turns a correlation into a cause. Keen students may choose the front row and also study more: a third factor."
```
