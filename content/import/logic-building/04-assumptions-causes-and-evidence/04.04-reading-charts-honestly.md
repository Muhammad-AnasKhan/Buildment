## Three times as popular?

Nour gets a forwarded advert for a shop in her area. It shows a bar chart of customers last week. The bar for Shop A is three times as tall as the bar for Shop B. Big letters say: **"Shop A: three times as popular!"** Nour looks closer. Small labels on the bars say 106 and 102.

Every number in the chart is true. So how can the picture say "three times"?

## Trick 1: the cut-off axis

The answer is in the <GlossaryTerm term="axis" definition="The line along the side of a chart that shows the numbers, from the smallest at the bottom to the biggest at the top." />. In an honest bar chart, the axis starts at 0. In Nour's advert, the axis starts at 100. Before you read on, guess.

```predict
prompt: "Shop A had 106 customers and Shop B had 102. The chart's axis starts at 100, so A's bar looks three times as tall. How big is the real difference?"
options:
  - id: big
    label: "Big, about three times"
  - id: small
    label: "Small, about 4 in 100"
correctOptionId: small
explanation: "106 minus 102 is 4 customers. That is about 4 more for every 100, a small difference. The bars look very different only because the bottom 100 is cut off."
```

Here is how the trick works, step by step.

1. **Find where the axis starts.** It starts at 100, not 0.
2. **Work out what each bar really shows.** Above 100, Shop A's bar shows 106 minus 100 = 6. Shop B's bar shows 102 minus 100 = 2. And 6 is three times 2. That is why A looks three times as tall.
3. **Work out the real difference.** The real numbers are 106 and 102. The difference is 4 customers. Out of about 100, that is about 4 percent.
4. **Say it honestly.** "Last week Shop A had 4 more customers than Shop B." That is true, but it is not exciting, so the advert hid it.

Cutting the axis is not always dishonest. Sometimes it helps you see small changes, such as the price of bread moving from 5 shekels to 5.5. But when the picture is used to make a claim like "three times", always read the numbers, not the heights.

## Trick 2: the picked time period

A shop owner posts: **"Customers up three months in a row!"** The chart shows March to June. Here are the full numbers for the first six months of the year, in this example.

| Month | Customers |
|---|---|
| January | 400 |
| February | 380 |
| March | 300 |
| April | 330 |
| May | 360 |
| June | 390 |

The claim is true: from March to June, customers rose each month. But look at the whole table. June (390) is still lower than January (400). Over six months, customers went **down** a little. The owner picked the start of the chart to begin at the lowest month.

The fix is one question: **what happened before and after the part I am shown?** If you cannot see it, ask for it. A short time period is not always a trick, but it is a reason to look wider.

## Trick 3: the small sample

Khaled hears from his cousin: "Three of my friends took the Fast Career course (a made-up course), and all three got jobs. It works!" This is not a chart, but it is the same kind of evidence: a number used to support a claim.

<BigWordAlert term="Sample" plainEnglish="The small group you actually looked at, used to guess about a bigger group." whyItMatters="A small or unusual sample can make almost any claim look true. Asking 'how many, and who?' protects you from paying for things that do not work." />

Three people is a very small sample. Here are the questions Khaled can ask, and why each one matters.

| What to ask | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| How many people took the course in total? | Three out of 5 is very different from three out of 300 |
| Who do we hear from? | People who did not get jobs rarely tell their friends about the course |
| Were the three different in other ways? | Maybe they already had skills, or were already looking hard |

The second question is easy to forget. Success stories travel. Failures stay quiet. So even an honest person only passes on the good results they heard about. This does not mean the course is bad. It means three happy friends cannot tell Khaled if it works.

<Callout type="tip" title="Three quick questions for any chart or story">
Where does the axis start? What time period, and what happened before and after it? How many people, and who? You can ask these in under a minute.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

The first mistake is trusting the picture over the numbers. Our eyes judge bar heights fast, and the trick works on that. Read the labels and do one small sum.

The second mistake is the opposite: deciding every chart is a lie. Most charts are honest. The goal is not to distrust everything. It is to ask the three questions, and then believe the chart as much as it deserves.
