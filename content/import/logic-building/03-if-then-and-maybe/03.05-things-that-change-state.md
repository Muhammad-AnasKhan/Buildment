## Where is my parcel?

Basel orders a phone charger from a shop in the city. He checks the shop's page every day. On Sunday it says **Packed**. On Monday it says **On the way**. On Tuesday it says **Delivered**. It never says two of these at once, and it never jumps from Packed straight to Delivered. Each day, the parcel is in exactly one place in a fixed list.

That fixed list is a list of states, and the parcel moves through it by rules.

<BigWordAlert term="State" plainEnglish="The one situation something is in right now, out of a fixed list of possible situations." whyItMatters="When you know the states and the rules for moving between them, you can say what can happen next, and what cannot. Apps, forms and offices all work this way." />

## States and moves

A thing with states follows three ideas.

- **It is always in exactly one state.** A parcel is not packed and delivered at the same time.
- **Rules decide which moves are allowed.** Each rule is an if-then rule, like the ones in chapter 3.2: if the parcel is packed and a driver takes it, then it is on the way.
- **A move the rules do not allow changes nothing.** The thing stays in the state it was in.

Some states have no moves out of them at all. This is an <GlossaryTerm term="end state" definition="A state with no moves out of it. When something reaches it, its story is finished." />. Delivered is an end state for Basel's parcel.

## Rania's application

Rania applies for a remote internship. Her application can be in one of five states: **Draft**, **Submitted**, **Interview**, **Not chosen** and **Offer**. While it is a draft, she can change anything. When she presses send, it becomes submitted. The company has one strict rule: if an application is submitted, then it cannot be edited.

The day after she sends it, Rania finds a spelling mistake in her answers. Before you read on, guess.

## What the chart showed

The chart follows two people applying for the same internship. Here is what each step showed.

1. **Draft.** Rania writes her application. Edits are allowed. She presses send, and it moves to submitted.
2. **A refused move.** Rania tries to fix a spelling mistake. The rule "if submitted, then no edits" refuses the move. The state stays submitted. Nothing changed, not even a little.
3. **Two possible moves.** From submitted, only two moves exist: "invited", or "no" (not invited). Rania is invited, so the state becomes interview.
4. **Offer.** After the interview the company says yes, and the state becomes offer. Offer is an end state.
5. **Another path.** Omar applied too, but he was not invited. His application went straight from submitted to not chosen. It never passed through interview, so it could never reach offer.

Here are all the allowed moves in one table. This is the whole set of rules the chart follows.

| State | Allowed moves | Leads to |
|---|---|---|
| Draft | send | Submitted |
| Submitted | invited, or not invited | Interview, or Not chosen |
| Interview | yes | Offer |
| Not chosen | none | (end state) |
| Offer | none | (end state) |

Now look at the second idea from the chart: can an application go from draft straight to offer? Look at the draft row. Its only move is send, to submitted. So no: every offer passes through submitted and interview first.

In real life, an interview can also end in a no. The chart leaves out that arrow to stay small. Real applications can also have more states, such as a test task or a waiting list.

<Callout type="warning" title="A refused move is not a small move">
When a rule refuses a move, people sometimes think "it partly worked". It did not. The state is exactly what it was before. Rania's spelling mistake is still in her application.
</Callout>

## Why this helps you think

States give you a calm way to answer "what can happen next?". Find the state something is in now. Read only the arrows that leave it. Everything else is impossible from here.

This also helps you read people's claims. Suppose a friend says, "I sent my application yesterday, so I have an interview." The table shows two moves from submitted, not one. Sending is not the same as being invited. You learned in chapter 3.3 to ask "is there another way?". With states, you ask "which arrows leave this state?".
