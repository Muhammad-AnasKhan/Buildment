## One choice, five parts

This is the capstone of the course. You take one choice and think it through with the habits you have built: steps, assumptions, rough numbers, checking an AI answer, and deciding without all the facts. It takes about an hour. You write it here, and you can add a voice note if you like.

You can use a real choice from your study, your work or your daily life. Or you can use one of the three scenarios below. Both are fully welcome, and your mentor reviews both the same way. Nobody has to share anything personal. Pick whichever lets you think most clearly.

## The five parts

Here is what you will write. Each part uses habits from earlier modules.

| Part | What you write | Habits from |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Steps | The problem broken into steps, or a simple flowchart written as a list | Module 2 |
| 2. Assumptions | Two hidden assumptions, and how you would test one of them | Modules 4 and 8 |
| 3. A rough number | One estimate, with every assumption written out | Module 5 |
| 4. The AI check | The question or answer, what you checked, and what was wrong or not checkable | Module 7 |
| 5. The decision | What you would do, why, and what would change your mind | Modules 6 and 8 |

Short and clear beats long. A list is fine for every part. Your English does not need to be perfect.

## A worked example: Lina's study group

Before you start, here is one example in full. Lina is a university student. She is thinking about running a small paid maths group for younger students in her neighbourhood, twice a week, in a room at the community centre.

**Part 1, steps.** Lina writes her plan as a list, with one yes or no point:

1. Ask parents if their children want to join, and at what price.
2. If at least 4 students say yes, book the room. If not, offer lessons at home instead.
3. Plan the first four lessons.
4. Run one free trial session.
5. Run four weeks of paid sessions.
6. Count the money and the hours, and decide whether to go on.

**Part 2, assumptions.** Lina finds two things she has not said out loud. First: parents will pay 15 shekels per session. Second: the students can come twice a week, even with the bus times and the power schedule. To test the first, she will ask five parents directly and offer one free trial. If at least 4 children sign up for paid sessions after the trial, the assumption holds.

**Part 3, a rough number.** How much might she earn in a month?

- 6 students x 15 shekels = 90 shekels per session.
- The room costs 20 shekels per session, so she keeps 90 - 20 = 70 shekels.
- 2 sessions a week for 4 weeks is 8 sessions. 8 x 70 = 560 shekels a month.

Her assumptions: 6 students come every time, each pays 15 shekels, the room costs 20 shekels, and a month is about 4 weeks. She also checks a worse case: with only 4 students, 4 x 15 = 60, minus 20 is 40, and 8 x 40 = 320 shekels a month. So her range is about 320 to 560 shekels.

**Part 4, the AI check.** Lina asked an AI chat tool: "How much does it cost to rent a small room at a community centre?" It answered: "Community centres usually let students use their rooms for free." It sounded sure and kind. But it was a general fact about community centres, not about hers. So she used the "find the source" check: she called her community centre. The room costs 20 shekels per session. The AI answer was likely-sounding, but not true for her.

**Part 5, the decision.** Lina will run the free trial session first, then four weeks of paid sessions if at least 4 students sign up. Why: even the worse case earns her money, and the risk is small, about 20 shekels per session. What would change her mind: fewer than 4 students sign up, or the group takes more than 6 hours a week away from her own study.

Notice how each part feeds the next. The steps showed the yes or no point. The assumptions showed what to test. The rough number gave a range, not a promise. The AI check fixed a cost. And the decision is a small test, with a clear line for changing her mind.

<Callout type="tip" title="Start with part 5 in your head">
Before you write, say your first feeling out loud: "Right now I would choose ___." Then work through parts 1 to 4. It is fine, and common, if your final decision is different. That means the thinking did its job.
</Callout>

## Choose your choice

You have two ways to do this capstone.

**A real choice.** Pick a choice you face now, or faced recently, at study, at work or at home. It should be big enough to need thought, but small enough to explain in five minutes. For part 4, ask an AI chat tool a question about your choice and check its answer. If you do not have an AI tool, use any one of the three AI answers below for part 4.

**A provided scenario.** Pick one of the three scenarios below. Each one gives you the facts you need, in this example. Each one includes an answer from an AI tool, captured for you. **Each AI answer contains one problem you can check.** Finding it is your part 4. You may add your own sensible guesses for missing facts, as long as you say they are guesses.

## Scenario 1: Sami's two courses

Sami wants to learn basic bookkeeping, so he can find work keeping accounts for small shops. He has found two online courses.

- **Course A** is free. It has about 40 hours of videos. There is no teacher to ask and no certificate.
- **Course B** costs 25 dollars a month and takes 4 months. A teacher answers questions once a week. At the end, there is a 15 dollar fee for the certificate.

Sami earns about 150 dollars a month from tutoring. He can study about 10 hours a week, but the internet at home is slow in the evenings. Two shop owners he asked gave different views: one said "I like to see a certificate", and the other said "Show me you can do it".

Sami asked an AI chat tool: "What is the total cost of Course B?" Here is the answer it gave:

> "Course B costs 25 dollars a month for 4 months, which is 100 dollars. With the 15 dollar certificate fee, the total is 105 dollars. Course A is free, but you will not get a certificate."

## Scenario 2: Maryam's laptop

Maryam studies and does small design jobs for clients. Her old laptop is very slow. In this example, she loses about 3 hours a week waiting for it.

- A seller offers her a **used laptop**, two years old, for 1,200 shekels. She has exactly 1,200 shekels saved. The seller will let her try it for one day before she pays.
- A **new laptop** of the kind she wants costs 2,000 shekels. She can save about 300 shekels a month.

Maryam asked an AI chat tool: "Should I buy the used laptop now, or save for three more months and buy a new one?" Here is the answer it gave:

> "If you save 300 shekels a month for 3 months, you will have 2,100 shekels, which is enough for the new laptop. Also, most used laptops stop working within one year of being sold again, so the new laptop is the safer choice."

## Scenario 3: The family shop and home delivery

Amal's family runs a small grocery shop. A few regular customers have asked if the shop could deliver. Amal's brother could deliver by bicycle for about 2 hours each evening.

In this example, the shop has about 60 customers on a normal day. An average order is about 40 shekels, and the shop keeps about 6 shekels from each order after paying for the goods. Each delivery would cost about 2 shekels, for bicycle repairs and phone credit. The family is thinking about a delivery fee of 3 shekels.

Amal asked an AI chat tool: "Will home delivery help our shop?" Here is the answer it gave:

> "Shops that offer home delivery have higher sales than shops that do not. So if your shop starts delivery, your sales will go up."

## How your mentor reads it

A mentor reviews your capstone in about 15 minutes, using five lines, one for each part. Read them before you write, so you know what "clear" looks like.

| Part | What clear looks like |
|---|---|
| 1. Steps | In order. Someone else could follow them. At least one yes or no point |
| 2. Assumptions | Two things that must be true but were not said. One has a real, cheap test |
| 3. A rough number | Every assumption written. The sums are right. A range is fine |
| 4. The AI check | Says the question or answer, the check used, and what was wrong or not checkable |
| 5. The decision | A clear choice, a reason that uses parts 1 to 4, and what would change your mind |

Then comes the **five-minute demo**. You walk your mentor through the five parts, one minute each. Your mentor may ask questions like these:

- Which assumption worries you most?
- What would change your estimate by ten times?
- What did the AI get wrong, and how did you find out?
- What would make you change your decision?

You do not need perfect answers. Your mentor wants to see how you think, and "I am not sure, but here is how I would find out" is a strong answer.

<Callout type="info" title="Running out of time or power?">
You can write the five parts in more than one sitting. Your answer saves as you type. A voice note is optional: some people find it easier to talk through their thinking first, then write the short version.
</Callout>
