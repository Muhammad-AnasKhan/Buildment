## Your habit toolkit

The table below holds one line for each tool in the course. It is short on purpose. When a habit starts to slip in a busy month, you will not have time to reread seven modules. You will have time to read about twenty lines.

| Tool | One line to remember |
|---|---|
| What a habit is | Something a familiar situation starts for you, learned by repeating it there |
| Cue, routine, reward | Find what starts it, what you do, and what you get from it |
| Months, not days | Habits grow fast at first, then slowly; expect about two months, often longer |
| The smallest version | Shrink the habit until it fits your hardest day; grow it once it is easy |
| If-then plans | Name a moment you will surely meet and a first step you can see |
| Anchors | Tie the habit to something you already do every day |
| Second anchor | If your anchor does not happen on some days, give those days a second anchor |
| Reminders are not cues | An alarm helps you remember; the situation builds the habit |
| Places and times | Habits live in places, times and people; a change of routine is a chance to start |
| Easier and harder | Fewer steps to the good choice, more steps to the unwanted one |
| Backup versions | Full, backup and smallest, so no power or weak internet does not break the habit |
| Fewer temptations | Plan your surroundings so you meet the urge less often, instead of resisting it |
| Same need, new routine | Keep the cue, meet the same need, change the action |
| Remove the cue, watch the start | Avoid the cue in advance; in the moment, notice the start and say "not this time" |
| Track the action | A tick or a cross for what you control, with a few words on each miss |
| Restart at the next cue | One missed day did not undo progress in research; a longer gap makes the next start harder |
| Kind, then a plan | Be kind to yourself after a slip, then change one thing |
| One habit first | Start the habit that matters most now; add the next once the first runs by itself |
| Focused work | A start signal, the phone in another room, and tomorrow's first step written down |
| Daily rhythm | Use fixed points such as a meal, a prayer time or a shift; an event or a clock time both work |
| Seasons and changes | An event moves with the season; if you use a clock time, check it when the season or your hours change |
| The 30-day review | Count, find when, find why, change one thing, test it for two weeks |

Copy the table somewhere you will see it: the first page of a notebook, the notes app on your phone, or a paper card inside the book you are reading. Read it at the start of a new month, or when a habit has slipped for a few days. Much of the value of this course is in having these lines near you at the moment you need them.

You do not need every line every time. A habit that keeps failing on days with no power needs "backup versions". A habit you keep forgetting needs "anchors" and "if-then plans", not a louder alarm. A habit whose anchor disappears on some days needs a "second anchor". An unwanted habit that returns every evening needs "remove the cue, watch the start" and "same need, new routine". Part of the skill is choosing which two or three lines fit the problem in front of you. You practised that choice in chapter 7.3, and it gets easier each time you do it.

### How to look back at your first habit

The first question in the learning log matters most. In gate 1 you described a habit before you knew most of the tools in this course. Looking at it again is a clear way to see how your thinking has changed, and it often shows you something to try next.

Here is an example. Imagine Fares, from the capstone, had done gate 1 when he started his spreadsheet course. He might have described this habit: when trade at the stall was slow, he picked up his phone and scrolled the news. He might have written the cue as "boredom", the routine as "scrolling" and the reward as "something to do". Reading it again now, he would see that his cue was too vague. The real cue was a place and a moment: standing behind the crates with no customers, usually between 10:00 and 11:00. He would also see a need he did not name then: a short rest after the busy early morning.

With the tools he has now, he would not try to resist the urge. He would keep the cue and meet the same need with a different action. In the quiet moment, he would sit on the stool and read one formula card, which also helps his spreadsheet habit. He would make the phone a little harder to reach by keeping it in his bag under the counter. He would also notice something he had done well: he had already seen that the habit was started by a situation, not by a decision.

Write your own look-back the same way: what you missed, what you would change, which tool you would use, and one thing you already did well. Four or five honest sentences show your mentor more than a page of general words. If you would rather not write about your own life, answer the questions with the cases you met, such as Samir's reading, Nada's sentence or Ziad's walk. Your mentor reads these answers for how you think, not for details about your life.

## Your next 30 days

The capstone looked back. This task looks forward. You will write a short plan for your next 30 days, so that the course ends with a habit still running, not just a finished review. At the course's pace, most fellows finish with about two to three weeks of tracking. Your next 30 days carry that same card forward. When your card reaches day 30, you may send your mentor the full 30-day review. The plan can be for the same habit with the one change you chose in the capstone, or for a new habit if your current one now starts by itself.

A good plan has five pieces. It names the habit as a cue and a small first step. It names the one change you are making and why the data points to it. It gives a smallest version for your hardest days and says where you will restart after a miss. It says what you will count, for how long, and what result would mean your change helped. And it names the two days when you will review: day 14 for a hint, and day 30 for a decision.

Here is a strong plan, from Fares. "Next 30 days: same habit, after the dawn prayer, one 10-minute spreadsheet lesson at the kitchen table. One change: on the mornings after a delivery night, reading one formula card counts, and the cards now live on the table. My data points here because 7 of my 9 misses came after delivery nights. Hardest day: one card, and if I miss, I restart at the next prayer. I will count the mornings after delivery nights, about 8. Before, I did 1 of 8. If I do 5 or more, the change helped. I will look on day 14 and decide on day 30." Every piece is there, and each one is short.

Compare a weak plan for the same month: "I will be more disciplined, study every morning and evening, and never miss a day." It has no cue, no small step and no change linked to the data. It plans to push through tiredness with willpower, and it promises perfection, so the first miss will feel like failure. It would feel serious to write, and it would change nothing about the mornings after delivery nights.

If you prefer not to use your own habit, write the plan for Nada. Her capstone month ended with 24 of 30 days done. Her second anchor works on mornings with no breakfast at home, and her last misses fell on two visit days and one exam day. Plan her next 30 days with one change and a clear way to know whether it helped.

If your month was too disrupted to show a habit forming, your plan still counts in full. Write it for the month you expect, with a smallest version that fits your hardest days and the cue you will restart at. A plan built for a hard month is a strong plan.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor uses the table below. Each line has three levels.

| Line | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| The habit | A moment you could point to and a first action someone could watch | One of these is vague, such as "in the evening" or "study more" | No cue, or a goal instead of a habit |
| The change | One change, with a count from the tracker as the reason | One change with no count, or two changes | No change, or "try harder" |
| Hard days | A smallest version and the cue to restart at | One of these | Neither, or "never miss a day" |
| How you will know | What to count, for how long, the result that means it helped, and both review days | Two or three of these four | Nothing to count |

**Pass rule:** "The change" and "How you will know" must be Met, and no line can be Not yet.

Your mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "What will you do on the first morning you miss?" or "What result on day 30 would make you drop this change?" You answer them in writing, and you may add a voice note as an extra. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
