## Your weekly rhythm plan

This task asks you to put your habits where they can live: on the fixed points of a real week. A daily habit is easy to plan for an imaginary day with no guests, no lectures and no changes. Real weeks have lecture days and home days, visits, family meals and prayer times that move with the seasons. The point of the plan is to give each habit a home in that real week, so that it does not depend on finding free time, and to decide in advance what happens on the days that do not look normal.

Use your own course habit if you have been tracking one, or use Nada's week below. Either way, the plan should show the same thinking: fixed points first, habits second, one new habit at a time, and a plan for the days that change.

Here is what a strong plan looks like, told about a fictional person. Rana teaches at a primary school from Sunday to Thursday. Her habit, reading one short English story after the school bell at 13:30, has run on most days for six weeks. She starts her plan with the fixed points of her week: waking at 6:00, the school bell at 13:30, lunch at home at 14:30, the sunset prayer and the family's evening meal after it, and going to bed at about 22:30. She marks the sunset prayer and the meal as the points that move through the year: about three hours later in summer than in winter, in this example.

Then she places her habits. The story stays after the school bell on school days. On Tuesdays and Thursdays, when she has canteen duty until 14:30, it follows the end of duty instead. On Fridays and Saturdays it follows lunch. Her one new habit is lesson planning, because a school inspection is coming. It will follow the evening meal, so it moves with the meal through the seasons, and she will start it next week, because her story habit now starts without a push. Her smallest versions: one page of the story, three lines of planning. For the busy inspection week she writes, "Three lines only, every evening." And she protects one thing that is not work: "Fridays after lunch, an hour with my mother at the market, phone at home."

Look at the shape of Rana's plan. Every habit is tied to something that already happens on the days it is planned, and the days are named. She knows which anchors move with the seasons. She adds one habit, with a reason and a start date that follows from her tracker. She has a smaller version ready for hard days and a plan for at least one changed week. And her plan makes room for family, not only for work.

Compare it with a weak plan: "Every day I will study English for 30 minutes, walk, sleep early, review my lectures and read, in my free time." That plan has no fixed points, so every habit depends on free time, and Dina's month showed what happens to free time. It starts five habits at once. It has no smaller versions and nothing for visitors or lecture days. It would feel ambitious on the first day and fall apart in the second week.

If you prefer not to use your own week, use this provided case. Nada is 22, studies accounting and lives with her family. Her habit since gate 2: "After I wash my breakfast cup, I open my English vocabulary notebook and write one sentence with a new word." Since gate 5, on mornings with no breakfast at home, her anchor is "after I fill my water bottle", at the tap next to the shelf where her notebook lives. Her smallest version is to read yesterday's sentence aloud. She has ticks on days 19 to 24. Here is her normal week.

| Day | Morning | Afternoon and evening |
|---|---|---|
| Sunday, Tuesday, Thursday | Breakfast at home, leaves at 7:45; lectures 8:30 to 12:30 | Home by about 13:15; family meal after the sunset prayer |
| Monday, Wednesday | Breakfast at home; studies at home | Helps her mother cook lunch; family meal after the sunset prayer |
| Friday | Breakfast at home | Family lunch at her grandmother's; relatives often visit |
| Saturday | Breakfast at home; washing day | Cousins often visit; family meal after the sunset prayer |

Nada prays, and the afternoon and sunset prayers are fixed points in her day that move through the year. She wants two more habits: 15 minutes reviewing each lecture on lecture days, before her exams in two months, and a regular bedtime, because she goes to bed anywhere between 23:00 and 1:00.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your plan with the table below. There is more than one good plan for any week, so your mentor checks the thinking, not whether your plan matches theirs.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed points | Five or more fixed points of the week, with the ones that move through the year marked | Fixed points listed, but fewer than five, or none marked as moving | No fixed points; habits placed on free time |
| Habits on anchors | Every habit follows a fixed point that happens on the days it is planned, and the days are named | Most habits have an anchor, but one depends on free time or a clock time that drifts | No anchors |
| One at a time | At most one new habit, with its anchor and how the tracker will show it is time to start | A new habit with no sign for when to start it, or two at once with a reason | Three or more new habits at once |
| Changed days | A smallest version and a plan for at least one changed day, such as visitors, a season change or exam week | A smallest version, but no plan for a changed day | Nothing for days that change |

**Pass rule:** your plan passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "Which of your anchors will move when summer comes, and does the habit still land in a good part of the day?" or "What does your tracker need to show before you add the next habit?" You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
