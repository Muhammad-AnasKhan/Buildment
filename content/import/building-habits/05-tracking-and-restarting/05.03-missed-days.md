## "Two, after twenty-three"

On the evening of day 30, Yousef finished his practice questions and drew a tick. His brother looked up from his phone. "So what is the streak now?"

"Two," said Yousef. Saying it out loud hurt more than he expected. Two days in a row, after twenty-three. The streak counter made his whole month look like a beginning.

Later, when the flat was quiet, he counted the calendar a different way. Days done: 23, then 5 empty squares, then 2. That is 25 days done out of 30. He had answered practice questions on 25 evenings this month. Nothing about the five dark evenings could take those 25 away. Same calendar, same ticks, two numbers that told very different stories: "2" and "25 of 30".

This chapter is about which numbers to watch after a missed day, and about the one rule this course asks you to follow when you miss.

## The rule: restart at the next cue

The rule is short. **Restart at the next cue.** When you miss a day, the next time your cue comes, do the habit, even in its smallest version. Do not wait for Monday, the first of the month or a day when you feel ready. Do not try to make up for the missed day. Just do the habit at the next cue.

Why this rule? Chapter 1.4 gave you two facts from research, and the rule follows from both. The first fact: in the study that followed people building a daily habit for twelve weeks, missing one day did not undo their progress. The habit kept growing soon after a single miss. The second fact: the length of the gap does matter. In a large study of gym members, the longer it had been since someone's last visit, the less likely they were to go again. A one-day gap is easy to cross. A two-week gap is much harder, because the cue has gone by many times without the habit.

So the miss itself is not the danger. The danger is what comes after the miss. After a slip, it is easy to think "I have already failed, so why bother". Researchers who help people avoid relapse call this a common trap, and they teach people to see a slip as a lesson, not a verdict. Yousef met this trap on day 29, when he looked at five empty squares and thought about throwing the calendar away. The five misses cost him five evenings. Giving up would have cost him the habit.

You may have heard a similar rule. The writer James Clear gives a popular rule: never miss twice. It is good advice, not a research finding. This course's version is simpler: restart at the next cue. It does not ask you to count misses or to panic after the first one. It asks one thing: what is the next cue, and what is the smallest version I can do there?

## What research says about streaks

A **streak** is the number of days in a row you have done a habit, and many people find streaks motivating. Research on streaks agrees that they can help, with a warning. Across seven studies, people who saw an intact streak in their record kept going more than people who saw a broken one, even when their real past behaviour was the same. The effect of a broken streak was stronger when people blamed themselves for the break, and weaker when they had a way to repair it.

That gives two practical lessons. First, a streak is useful while it is going, so you do not have to throw it away. Second, if you use a streak, build in a repair before you need one. Two repairs work well. One is a **grace day**: one day a week may be missed without breaking the streak, as long as you write the reason. The other is to count the smallest version as done, so a hard day can still keep the streak going.

One more finding points the same way. A very large study tested 54 different programmes designed to get gym members to exercise more. The programme that worked best gave people small rewards for coming back after they missed a workout. Coming back, not never missing, was the behaviour worth rewarding.

## Yousef's month, counted properly

Here is Yousef's first month counted the way this chapter suggests. Every number comes from his calendar: done on days 1 to 23, missed on days 24 to 28, done on days 29 and 30.

| Number | Yousef's month | What it tells him |
|---|---|---|
| Days done | 25 of 30 | How much practice he actually did |
| Streak on day 30 | 2 days | How long since his last miss |
| Longest run | 23 days | The habit was strong before the power cuts |
| Longest gap | 5 days | The danger point, and what caused it |

**Days done** is the headline. 25 of 30 is a strong month by any fair measure. It means 25 evenings of 20 minutes: more than eight hours of practice questions.

**The streak** is the least useful number here. It says "2" because his last miss was three days ago. That is true, and it says nothing about how good the month was.

**The longest run** says the habit was working: 23 days in a row while the evenings had power.

**The longest gap** is the number to act on. Five days in a row is long enough to make restarting hard, and he nearly did not restart. The cause is clear from the dates: the gap is exactly the five evenings without power. Chapter 5.2 showed the change that follows: a paper version for dark evenings. With that version, a dark evening becomes a small tick instead of a cross.

Now try the other road. If Yousef had listened to the thought on day 29 and waited for the first of next month, days 24 to 30 would all be crosses. Days done: 23 of 30. Longest gap: 7 days, and the next cue would be another day away. Waiting does not give a clean start. It gives a longer gap.

Finally, Yousef adds a repair to his tracker so that the next hard week does not break his streak for no good reason. His rule: "A day counts if I do the full version or the smallest version: 5 questions on paper. If I am away from home and cannot do even that, I may use one grace day a week, with the reason written down." He writes the rule at the top of his card, before he needs it.

## A repair rule, as a decision

A repair rule should be decided in advance, when you are calm, not on the evening you miss. The diagram shows Yousef's rule as a short set of questions he can answer on a hard evening.

```mermaid
flowchart TD
  A["Hard evening"] --> B{"Full version possible?"}
  B -->|"yes"| C["Do it: tick"]
  B -->|"no"| D{"Smallest version possible?"}
  D -->|"yes"| E["Do it: tick, streak safe"]
  D -->|"no"| F["Grace day: write the reason"]
  F --> G["Restart at the next cue"]
```

Follow the questions from the top. Most hard evenings stop at the second question, because the smallest version needs almost nothing: no power, no laptop, five minutes. Only a truly impossible day reaches the grace day, and even then the last box is the same as always: restart at the next cue. Notice that every road ends with the habit happening, soon. The rule protects the streak, but its real job is to keep the gap short.

## Where people go wrong

**"I broke it, so I start over from day 1."** This treats a habit like a counter that resets. Habits do not work that way: the progress from 23 days is still there. Restarting the count can be fine as bookkeeping, but restarting the habit from zero in your head is the trap.

**"Missing days does not matter at all."** That is the opposite mistake. Many misses lowered how strong habits became in the research, and long gaps made the next time less likely. A miss is information to act on, not something to ignore.

**Waiting for a clean start.** Monday, a new month or the end of a busy week can feel like a better moment. Each day of waiting is another day added to the gap. Chapter 5.4 shows how to use a new week as a push without waiting for it.

**Catching up.** After two misses, some people plan a double session: 40 minutes instead of 20. A double session is harder to start than a normal one, so it raises the barrier just when you need it lowest. Restart with the normal version, or the smallest one.

**A repair that hides a pattern.** A grace day every few days is no longer a repair; it is a way of not seeing your misses. Keep grace days rare and always write the reason, so your notes can still show what keeps going wrong.

<Callout type="info" title="Two numbers for your card">
At the end of each week, write two numbers at the bottom of your tracker: days done, and the longest gap. Watch the gap. If it reaches two or three days, ask what is in the way and plan the next cue.
</Callout>

## Try it today

Look at your course habit tracker, or Yousef's month if you prefer. Count days done so far and find your longest gap. Then write a repair rule at the top of your card, in one or two lines: what counts as done on a hard day (your smallest version), and whether you allow a grace day. Finally, name your next cue in words: "The next time I finish breakfast...". If you missed today, that next cue is your restart, and nothing else needs to happen first.
