## Learning log

1. **Your resisting:** Think of a time you tried to stop a habit with willpower alone, or use Maha's promise to stop at 23:30. What happened, and what does chapter 4.2 suggest instead?
2. **Your need:** For one unwanted habit, yours or Hamza's, what does the habit give? How could you test that in one day?

4. **Your slips:** When a habit slips through, what do people often tell themselves, and what would you rather tell yourself next time? You can answer for Maha's slip night if you prefer.

## Your plan to weaken one habit

This task brings the module together on one unwanted habit. You will name what starts it and what it gives. You will decide what to change before the moment comes, and choose a better routine for the same moment. Then you will plan what to do when the urge arrives and when a slip happens.

The point is not to promise that the habit will never happen again. The point is a plan that makes it happen less often, and that still works on a tired day.

Choose an everyday habit that costs you some sleep, money, time or focus: a game before work, late scrolling, a daily purchase, checking messages in the middle of a task. If a habit feels out of your control or close to a health problem, do not use it for this task. Talk to your fellowship mentor, a doctor or a counsellor about it instead, and use the provided case below.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about Khaled from module 3. On his day off, the family eats a long lunch together on Fridays at 13:00. During that lunch Khaled keeps checking his work chat on his phone, and his mother has started to complain. His promise of "no phone at lunch" failed on most Fridays.

He looked closer. The cue is a buzz from his pocket while he sits at the table. His team often posts questions at that hour, as they get ready for the day. The need is a feeling of responsibility: he does not want a customer problem to go unanswered.

His plan has four parts. In advance, the phone goes on the hall shelf at 12:55 with the sound off. He moved the job the phone was doing by telling his team, "On my day off, I check messages once, at 15:00." That fixed check meets his need for responsibility, so the lunch does not have to.

At the table, his replacement is to ask his niece one question about her week. In the moment, if he feels the urge to go to the hall, he says to himself, "That is the work chat habit. It can wait until 15:00." If he slips and finds the phone at the table, he puts it back on the shelf, writes one line about what brought it there, and starts again the next Friday.

Look at the shape of Khaled's answer. The cue and the need come from what he noticed, not from a guess. The in-advance change removes the cue and adds friction. The phone's job, staying in touch with his team, moves to a fixed time instead of disappearing. The replacement fits the same moment. The words for the moment are short enough to remember, and the slip plan leads back to the plan.

Compare it with a weak answer: "I will stop checking my phone at lunch. I just need to be stronger, because my family matters more." It is a plan to resist. It names no cue, no need and no replacement, and it adds shame, which makes every slip feel like proof of weakness. It would fail on the first busy Friday, because the phone would still buzz in his pocket.

If you prefer not to use your own habit, use this provided case. Hamza is 24 and works at a print shop in the city centre. He takes the 7:30 bus from a stop near his home, six days a week, Saturday to Thursday. He usually arrives about 10 minutes early, because he leaves right after breakfast. Next to the stop there is a small shop.

While he waits, he buys a cold sweet drink for 5 shekels, almost every working day. That is 30 shekels a week, or 120 shekels over four weeks, and he wants to save that money toward a course fee. On the two mornings a week when his neighbour takes the same bus, they talk while they wait, and Hamza usually forgets about the shop.

He says, "It passes the waiting time, and I like having something cold in my hand." He tried "no drink this week" once; it lasted two days. He always has coins in his pocket. Write his plan as a money and routine habit.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer with the table below. There is more than one good plan for any habit. If yours is different from the one your mentor would write, that is fine, as long as each part fits the facts.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cue and need | Both named (cue as a place, time, person or action), each backed by one fact | Only one of them named, or no fact given | Neither is named |
| In advance | One cue removed or avoided and one added step, each something you could point to | Only one of these, or only vague words | Nothing changes before the moment |
| Replacement | A new routine at the same cue, with the part of the need it meets named | A new routine that misses the need or the cue | No replacement |
| Moment and slips | Exact words for the urge, and at least two steps for after a slip | Only one of these | Neither, or only "try harder" |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

Write your answer in plain English. Short sentences are fine. You may add a voice note link as an extra.

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "How would you test whether your replacement meets the need?" or "What will you do the morning after a slip?" Answer them in writing; a voice note can be added as an extra. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
