## Learning log

1. **Your fights:** Think of a time you tried to stop a habit with willpower alone, or use Maha's promise to stop at 23:30. What happened, and what does chapter 4.2 suggest instead?
2. **Your need:** For one unwanted habit, yours or Hamza's, what does the habit give? How could you test that in one day?
3. **Your cue:** Which cue starts the habit most often? Does it do another job that would need a new home if you moved it, as Maha's phone was also her alarm?
4. **Your slips:** When a habit slips through, what do people often tell themselves, and what would you rather tell yourself next time? You can answer for Maha's slip night if you prefer.

## Your plan to weaken one habit

This task brings the module together on one unwanted habit. You will name what starts it and what it gives, decide what to change before the moment comes, choose a better routine for the same moment, and plan what to do when the urge arrives and when a slip happens. The point is not to promise that the habit will never happen again. The point is a plan that makes it happen less often, and that still works on a tired day.

Choose an everyday habit that costs you some sleep, money, time or focus: a game before work, late scrolling, a daily purchase, checking messages in the middle of a task. If a habit feels out of your control or close to a health problem, do not use it for this task; talk to a mentor, a doctor or a counsellor about it instead, and use the provided case below.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional person. Anas coaches a youth team at a sports club. Every evening at the family dinner, at 19:30, he checks the club's group chat on his phone, and his children have started to complain. His promise of "no phone at dinner" failed on most nights. He looked closer. The cue is a buzz from his pocket while he sits at the table, and the group is busy at that hour because parents ask about training. The need is a feeling of responsibility: he does not want a parent's question to go unanswered. His plan has four parts. In advance, the phone goes on the hall shelf at 19:25 with the sound off, and the group is muted until 20:30. He moved the job the phone was doing by telling the parents, "I answer messages at 20:30 every evening." That fixed check at 20:30 meets his need for responsibility, so the dinner moment does not have to. At the table, his replacement is to ask each child one question about their day. In the moment, if he feels the urge to go to the hall, he says to himself, "That is the group chat habit. It can wait until 20:30." If he slips and finds the phone at the table, he puts it back on the shelf, writes one line about what brought it there, and starts again the next evening.

Look at the shape of Anas's answer. The cue and the need come from what he noticed, not from a guess. The in-advance changes remove the cue and add friction, and the phone's job, staying in touch with parents, moves to a fixed time instead of disappearing. The replacement fits the same moment. The words for the moment are short enough to remember, and the slip plan leads back to the plan instead of away from it.

Compare it with a weak answer: "I will stop checking my phone at dinner. I just need to be stronger, because my family matters more." It is a fight plan. It names no cue, no need and no replacement, and it adds shame, which makes slips feel like verdicts. It would feel sincere, and it would fail on the first busy evening, because the phone would still buzz in his pocket.

If you prefer not to use your own habit, use this provided case. Hamza is 24 and works at a print shop in the city centre. He takes the 7:30 bus from a stop near his home, six days a week, Saturday to Thursday. He usually arrives about 10 minutes early, because he leaves right after breakfast. Next to the stop there is a small shop. While he waits, he buys a cold sweet drink for 5 shekels, almost every working day. That is 30 shekels a week, or 120 shekels over four weeks, and he wants to save that money toward a course fee. On the two mornings a week when his neighbour takes the same bus, they talk while they wait, and Hamza usually forgets about the shop. He says, "It passes the waiting time, and I like having something cold in my hand." He has tried "no drink this week" once; it lasted two days. He always has coins in his pocket. Write his plan as a money and routine habit.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer with the table below. There is more than one good plan for any habit. If yours is different from the one your mentor would write, that is fine, as long as each part fits the facts.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cue and need | The cue and the need are both named, and linked to something noticed | Only one of them is named | Neither is named |
| In advance | The cue is removed or avoided, and one step of friction is added, both concrete | Only one of these, or only in vague words | Nothing changes before the moment |
| Replacement | A new routine at the same cue that meets the same need | A new routine that misses the need or the cue | No replacement |
| Moment and slips | Exact words for noticing the urge, and a plan for a slip | Only one of these | Neither, or "try harder" |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "How would you test whether your replacement meets the need?" or "What will you do the morning after a slip?" You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
