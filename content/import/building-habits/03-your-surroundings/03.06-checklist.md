## Learning log

1. **Your cues:** Think of one habit you already have, or use Khaled's evening from chapter 3.1. Which kind of cue starts it most strongly: a place, a time, other people, or the action just before?
2. **Your steps:** For your course habit, or for Nada's, how many steps did it take to start before this module, and how many does it take after your redesign?
3. **Your hard days:** Which kind of day is hardest for your habit: no power, weak internet, no time, or a busy house? If you prefer, answer for Khaled's study habit instead.
4. **Your next change:** Which tool from this module do you expect to help you most: moving a cue, changing the steps, or writing versions? Say why in one or two sentences.

## Redesign one place or routine

This task brings the module together on the habit you started in module 2. You will change one place or one routine so the habit's cue and its tools meet, count the steps before and after, and write the versions that keep the habit going on hard days. The point is not a perfect room. The point is to show your reasoning: which cue starts the habit, what stood between you and the first step, what you changed, and what happens on the days when the power or the internet is off.

Keep it to one place or one routine. A redesign of the whole house is hard to test and hard to keep. One shelf, one table corner, one charger moved to another room is enough. If your course habit already needs no power or internet, your backup and your smallest version may be the same thing. Say so, and explain which hard day it covers instead, such as a morning with no time.

Here is what a strong answer looks like, told about a fictional person. Iyad, a computer science student, practises code for 15 minutes after breakfast. Before his redesign, the laptop lived in a bag under his bed and the phone charged beside his pillow, so practice took 4 steps and the phone took none. He moved one thing at a time. The laptop now stays open on his desk with yesterday's file showing, which cuts practice to 1 step, and the phone charges on the shelf across the room, which adds 3 steps to it. His full version is 15 minutes of code on the laptop. His backup version, for mornings when the power is off and the laptop is flat, is one small exercise written by hand on paper, 10 minutes. His smallest version, for mornings with no time, is reading one page of his printed notes. He wrote two if-then lines: "If the laptop is flat, then I write one exercise on paper," and "If I have no time, then I read one page of notes." He added a preparation line, "When the power comes on, I charge the laptop and copy tomorrow's exercise onto paper," and a return line, "On the first morning with power, I go back to the full version."

Look at the shape of Iyad's answer. The cue stays the same in every version: after breakfast. He counted steps before and after, for both the habit and the phone. Each version removes what a hard morning takes away. The preparation is tied to a moment that really happens, the power coming on, and the return line stops the small version from becoming the only one.

Compare it with a weak answer: "I will put my books somewhere better and try to study even when the power is off." It names no place, no cue and no steps. "Try to study when the power is off" is a hope, not a version: it does not say what to do, for how long, or with what light. It would feel honest, and it would change nothing on the first dark evening.

If you prefer not to use your own habit, use Nada's, which you met in module 2. Nada is 22 and studies accounting. Her habit is: "After I wash my breakfast cup, I open my English vocabulary notebook and write one sentence with a new word." It takes about 3 minutes, and she tracks it with a tick or a cross and a few words on a paper card kept inside the notebook. So far she has done it every morning, but it is harder than it needs to be. The notebook lives in her bag in the bedroom she shares with her sister, so after washing her cup she walks to the bedroom, finds the notebook in the bag and looks for a pencil: about 4 steps. Twice she nearly gave up because her sister was still asleep and she did not want to go in. Next to the kitchen sink there is a drying rack with a small shelf beside it. On some mornings the power is off and the kitchen is dim, and on lecture mornings she has only a minute or two. Redesign her place, and write her full version and her smallest version for those mornings.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your answer with the table below. There is no single right redesign. If your plan is different from the one your mentor would choose, that is fine, as long as it fits the facts you give.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cue and place | The tools wait at the cue, with steps counted before and after | A new place, but not at the cue, or no steps counted | No change of place or routine |
| Easier and harder | At least one step removed from the habit, and any rival made harder, each named | Only "make it easier", with no step named | Nothing made easier or harder |
| Versions | A backup with no internet or no power, and a smallest of 2 minutes or less, at the same cue | One version only, or a backup that needs what the full one needs | No backup or smallest version |
| Hard-day plan | An if-then line for a hard day, plus a preparation or return line | Only one of these | No plan for a hard day |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions. For example: "Which step did you remove first, and why that one?" or "What will tell you it is time to go back to the full version?" You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
