## The problem

Picture a customer who wants fresh groceries from **local shops** — the bakery on the corner, the farm stall at the market, the small grocer down the street. Today that usually means **three separate trips**, three phone calls, or three different websites. Each seller runs their own world: their own stock, their own hours, their own way of taking orders. There is no simple way to **discover** what's available across the neighborhood and **buy from more than one shop in one go**.

On the other side, small sellers want to reach more customers without building a full e-commerce site from scratch. They need a **shared storefront** — somewhere customers already shop — where they can list products, upload photos, and fulfill orders without owning the whole platform.

That gap is the problem this course solves.

## The solution

You will build a **multi-vendor marketplace**: one platform where **many sellers** run their shops and **buyers** browse, add items to a cart, and check out — even when those items come from different vendors.

- **Sellers** register, open a shop, list products (with photos and stock), and manage incoming orders.
- **Buyers** search the catalogue, fill one cart across vendors, pay once, and track their orders.

The platform handles the hard parts in the middle: keeping each vendor's data separate, splitting one checkout into per-vendor orders, and locking in the prices the customer actually paid.

## What the platform does

At a high level, your application connects three kinds of people:

| Role | What they can do |
|---|---|
| **Vendor** | Opens a shop, lists products with photos, updates stock, and fulfills orders from their dashboard. |
| **Customer** | Browses every shop, adds items to one cart, pays once at checkout, and tracks order status. |
| **The marketplace** | Connects vendors and customers in one place — each shop stays separate, one checkout can split into several orders, and payments are handled safely. |

This is not a single-shop website. It is **many shops, one API, one customer experience** — the shape of real marketplaces like Etsy, Uber Eats, or local grocery aggregators, at a scope you can finish and deploy.

## Name the product yourself

**FreshMarket** is only a placeholder name in this course. Your app might be called **GreenBasket**, **LocalHarvest**, **Neighborhood Pantry**, or something you invent on day one. What matters is the **architecture and domain** — multi-vendor grocery, local sellers, shared checkout — not the brand on the logo.

You'll create the Git repository in Chapter 2. From that moment, treat it as **your** product: pick a name, commit daily, and make decisions as if you were the founder.

## What you'll ship by the end

By the time you finish this course, you'll have a **real, deployed application** — not a folder of half-finished exercises.

A vendor can register, verify their email, open a shop, photograph their produce, and manage incoming orders. A customer can browse every shop on the platform, fill one cart with apples from one vendor and olive oil from another, check out once, and watch the platform split that purchase into separate orders behind the scenes — with the prices they actually paid locked on the receipt forever.

**In one line:** a live marketplace where vendors run their shops, customers buy from many sellers in one checkout, and you deploy it to a real URL for your portfolio.

## Common beginner questions

**Q: I've never built a full-stack app. Can I still do this?**
A: If you can build a small REST API and a React page that fetches data, yes — read the **Learn it** sections slowly and use the **In plain English** boxes in each chapter. If CRUD is completely new, spend a few days on a tiny todo app first (see 1.5).

**Q: Why one repo with `server/` and `client/`?**
A: So you always know where files live and can demo both halves together. The server handles data and rules; the client is what users see in the browser.

**Q: Is this a copy-paste course?**
A: No. The course tells you **what** each route must do and **how to verify** it — you write the handler logic yourself.

**Q: Why grocery and not a generic shop?**
A: Because units (kg vs pieces), perishables, and multi-vendor checkout change real schema and API decisions — not just the product name.

**Q: What if I get stuck?**
A: Read the error first, check **Troubleshooting** at the bottom of the sub-chapter, re-read **Bridge from last chapter** if the chapter feels like a jump. See 1.7 for working rhythm.

## What makes this project different

Many learning projects end up as a todo list or a single-user blog. Those are fine for practice, but they do not show how real products handle **more than one owner** sharing the same system.

This marketplace is different because you will work through problems that come up in real jobs:

### 1. More than one shop on one platform

Each vendor has their own products and orders. Your code must make sure Vendor A never sees or edits Vendor B's data. That is called **data isolation**, and you will apply it on almost every API route you write.

### 2. Checkout must remember what the customer paid

If a product price changes tomorrow, old orders should still show the price from the day of purchase. You will learn **price snapshots** at checkout — a small detail that matters a lot in e-commerce.

### 3. Speed when the catalogue grows

Browsing hundreds of products should stay fast. You will add **pagination**, avoid slow database patterns, and use **caching** where it helps — not as buzzwords, but because you measured a slow page and fixed it.

### 4. Production deployment with Docker on AWS

Localhost is where you build. **Production** is where real users open your app in a browser.

Later in the course you will **containerize** your API, worker, and related services with **Docker** so they run the same way on your machine and on a cloud server. Then you will deploy to **AWS** — for example an EC2 instance or a managed service — with HTTPS, environment variables, and a process that keeps your app running after a restart.

That matters because many tutorials stop at `npm run dev`. You will finish with a **live URL** you can share in a portfolio and explain step by step: how the image was built, where it runs on AWS, and how the client, API, database, and worker connect in production.

### 5. You ship the full stack

You are not only writing API routes. You will connect a React frontend, store product photos in a file service, take test payments, and put the whole system on the internet. That is what "full-stack" means here: from database to browser to a cloud deployment you can demo.

When you talk about this project in an interview, you are not listing tools you heard of once. You can walk through how you built each part and what broke along the way.

## What this course is — and what it is not

### What this course is

This is a **guided build**. Each chapter tells you what to implement and how to check that it works. You write the code yourself. The course gives you clear specs, not copy-paste solutions.

Progress is tied to **features**, not pages read. You move forward when the app actually does the next thing — a health check, a login form, a working cart — not when you finish scrolling.

The difficulty is honest: **medium**. You will hit errors, re-read sections, and fix bugs. That is normal. The goal is a working product and understanding why each piece exists.

### What this course is not

This is **not a copy-paste tutorial**. If you paste code without knowing what it does, the next chapter will not make sense and the app will break in ways you cannot debug.

This is **not a theory-only textbook**. You will not pass by summarizing concepts in a document. You verify your work by running the app and completing checkpoints in the platform.

This is **not a course on delivery logistics or paying out vendors**. We focus on the core marketplace: shops, products, carts, checkout, and orders. Payouts to sellers and shipping integrations are out of scope for now.
