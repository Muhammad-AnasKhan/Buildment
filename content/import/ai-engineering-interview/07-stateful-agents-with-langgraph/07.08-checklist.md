## Learning log

1. **Your reducer choice:** Pick one key from your 7.6 state. Why does it have, or not have, a reducer? What would break if you changed that?
2. **The re-run trap:** In your own words, explain to a teammate why the "under review" email in 7.4 was sent twice. How did your build avoid the same mistake?
3. **Two layers:** Your build stops a double refund in two places: the graph and the tool. Which one would you trust if you could keep only one, and why?
4. **Memory and privacy:** Name one thing your capstone agent should remember across conversations and one thing it must never store.
5. **One or many agents:** Would your capstone agent be better as one agent or several? Give the benefit you would gain, or the cost you would avoid.

## What a strong answer sounds like

Before you write, compare two answers to the same question. A weak answer says: "The state holds the messages and the tool results, and the agent asks for approval before refunds." It is not wrong, but an interviewer learns nothing from it. Which keys? Which reducer on each? Which node calls `interrupt()`, and what happens if the reviewer clicks twice?

A strong answer is concrete and short. It lists four to seven keys with their types.

For each key it says whether there is a reducer and why: one owner and the latest value wins, or several writers and an append or merge rule. It names the node that pauses and states that nothing risky sits above the pause. It names what the reviewer sees, such as the amount, the reason and the evidence, with the source of each. It says how the risky action stays safe if it runs twice, and what happens to a request nobody answers. One or two numbers, such as an approval limit or a 48-hour expiry, show that you have thought about real use.

Keep it to what you would say in about two minutes. If you notice you are describing LangGraph in general, stop and return to your agent.

## Your interview answer

In the hands-on or system design round, interviewers often say: "Design the state for this agent." They want to hear the keys, the reducer on each key and which node owns each key. They also want to know where the graph pauses and what makes the risky action safe.

Write your answer as you would say it aloud. Use the agent you plan to build in the capstone, or the Bustan Books agent if you have not chosen yet. Use real key names and at least one number, such as an approval threshold or a timeout. A mentor reads your answer and may send you one to three follow-up questions; you answer them by editing and resubmitting.

If you prefer, you can also record yourself giving the answer, as practice for the recorded defence in module 12. The written answer is still required.
