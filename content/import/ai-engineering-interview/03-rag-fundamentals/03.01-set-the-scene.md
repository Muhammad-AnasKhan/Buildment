## A confident answer from an old rule

Huda is a fellow who joined Najm Microfinance (a fictional lender for small businesses) as a junior AI engineer. Najm has 60 loan officers in 14 branches. Each officer works from a stack of policy PDFs: loan sizes, interest rules, late fees, who must approve what. The stack is about 900 pages, and it changes every few months.

On her second day, a loan officer named Sami shows Huda a problem. He typed a question into a general chat assistant: "What is the largest first loan we can give a women's savings group in a rural branch?" The assistant answered at once, in perfect English, with a number. The number was wrong. Najm raised that limit in March, and the assistant had never seen any Najm policy, old or new.

Sami did not notice the error until his branch manager did. The loan was already drafted. Nobody was hurt this time, but Najm's managers now ask a fair question: can a model answer from *our* rules, and can it show where each answer came from?

## Why a bigger model does not fix this

Huda's first thought is to try a larger model. That does not help. A model learns from the text it was trained on. Najm's policies are private, so they were never in that text. And even a public rule that changed in March may be newer than the model's training data. A bigger model only gives a wrong answer with more confidence.

Her second thought is to paste all 900 pages into every prompt. That fails too. In module 2 you saw that every token in a prompt costs money and time. In this example, 900 pages are roughly 450,000 words, more than many models can read at once, and paying for that text on every question would be wasteful even where it fits. Most of those pages have nothing to do with Sami's question.

What Sami needs is simpler. Before the model answers, the system should *find* the two or three paragraphs of the current policy that matter, put only those into the prompt, and tell the model to answer from them and cite them. If nothing relevant is found, the assistant should say "I don't know" instead of guessing.

## Why interviewers ask about this

Many teams hire AI engineers for one reason: they want a model to answer from their own documents. That makes RAG one of the topics you are most likely to meet in the project deep dive. If your project uses RAG, expect the interviewer to walk through it with you: "How did you split the documents? Why that chunk size? Which embedding model, and why? What happens when nothing relevant is found?"

Weak answers name a framework and stop. Strong answers name each step, the choice made at that step, the number that justified it, and one failure the candidate saw and fixed. By the end of this module you will have all four for a small system you built yourself.
