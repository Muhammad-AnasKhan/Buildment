## Learning log

1. **Two paths:** Think of a bug you could meet in your own pipeline. Is it on the indexing path or the query path, and which test or log line would show it first?
2. **The threshold:** In 3.5, "What is the capital of France?" scored above 0.1 against a shipping chunk. What does that tell you about a fixed score threshold, and what would you measure before trusting one?
3. **Chunk size:** Your chunker in 3.5 uses 60 words with 10 words of overlap. For the Bustan Books documents, would 30 or 120 words be better? Which questions would get better, and which worse?
4. **Your own documents:** Look at the documents you plan to use in your capstone. Where are the natural boundaries: headings, numbered rules, table rows, or none at all?
5. **Explain it simply:** How would you explain the "I don't know" path to a loan officer who is not technical, in two sentences?

## Your interview answer

The interviewer says: "Tell me how you chunked your documents and why." This is one of the first follow-ups in any RAG deep dive, and a vague answer ends the topic badly. Answer it for the documents you plan to use in your capstone. In module 12 you choose one of three scenarios: policy documents for an NGO or a microfinance lender, support policies for a small online shop, or runbooks and decision notes for a remote software team. If you are not sure yet, pick the one you are most likely to build.

Write it the way you would say it: what the documents look like, your splitting rule, your chunk size and overlap in numbers, the metadata you store, and how you would check the choice. Name one risk and what you would do about it. A mentor reads your answer and may send you one or two follow-up questions, the way an interviewer would.

A strong answer starts from the documents and the questions, not from a number you read somewhere. "I used 512 tokens because that is the default" tells the interviewer you did not choose. "Our runbooks have short numbered steps, users ask about single steps, so I capped chunks at 250 words" tells them you did. Then it gives a way to check: a handful of real questions, each with the chunk that should answer it, and a count of how often that chunk lands in the top 3.

Be honest about what you have not measured yet. You have not built your capstone, so you cannot quote real results. Say what you would measure and what result would make you change your mind, for example "if fewer than 8 in 10 questions find the right chunk in the top 3, I would try heading-based chunks with small-to-big retrieval". Interviewers trust a candidate who names the test more than one who claims a perfect number.
