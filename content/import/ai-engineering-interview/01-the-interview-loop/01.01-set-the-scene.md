## Karim's first interview

Karim is a final-year student. Over two weekends he built a chatbot that answers questions about his university's course rules. It reads the rule documents, finds the right paragraph and asks a language model to write an answer. His friends used it before exams. He was proud of it, and he put it at the top of his CV.

A remote team invited him to an interview for a junior AI engineer role. The interviewer was friendly and asked about the chatbot. Then the questions got harder. Why did you split the documents into pieces of that size? How often did the bot find the right paragraph? What did one question cost? What happened when the model provider was slow? How did you know the answers were still correct a month later?

Karim answered honestly: "It worked well. My friends liked it." He did not have numbers. He had never measured how often retrieval found the right paragraph. He had never thought about a slow provider. He did not get the role.

Karim did not lack skill. He had built something real, which many candidates have not. What he lacked was the second half of the work: measuring it, explaining each choice, and knowing how it would behave under load and over time. That second half is what this course is about.

## What these interviews test

An AI engineering interview is not a maths exam about neural networks. Most of the questions are about the system around the model. How do you find the right information for the model? How do you know the answer is good? What does it cost, and how fast is it? What happens when something fails? Can you explain your choices to a teammate or a manager?

The loop you are preparing for usually has three rounds. The first is a deep dive into a project you built. The second is hands-on: you write practical Python and you design a system on a whiteboard or in a shared document. The third is a managerial conversation about past projects, deployment and how you checked quality in production. Chapter 1.2 explains each round and how it is scored.

## What you will build

Across the course you build one small service on your laptop, called `rag-agent-service`. It answers questions from a few policy documents and cites its sources. It can look up an order and ask a human to approve a refund before it acts. It runs behind a small web endpoint with input checks, retries and a time limit. Its quality is measured on a set of test questions, and that measurement runs as a test that fails when quality drops.

It uses a fake, predictable model, so you never need a paid API key, and the tests run offline. In the last module you adapt it to one of three scenarios, write an evaluation report and defend it in a recorded mock interview. That defence is the answer Karim could not give.

## What you need

Most chapters work on a phone. Six parts need a laptop or a desktop with Python 3.11 or later: chapters 1.4, 3.5, 5.5, 7.6 and 9.6, and the capstone. Chapter 1.4 explains what to do if you do not have a laptop of your own.

The chapters add up to about 52 hours. Power cuts, work and family will interrupt you, and that is normal. Your answers save as you type, so a lost connection does not lose your work. Plan more weeks rather than rushing a build chapter.

## Before you continue
