## Two hundred unhelpful answers

Three months after launch, the policy assistant Huda built for Najm Microfinance (a fictional lender) is used by every branch. Loan officers can click "not helpful" under an answer. Huda and Omar, a fellow who joined the team last month, export the 200 questions with that click and read every one.

They expected bad answers from the model. Instead, most failures happened before the model was called. In their notes, the 200 fell into four groups.

**Exact codes and names (about 60).** "What documents does form CR-17 need?" Vector search returned chunks about forms in general. The embedding knows that "form" is about paperwork, but "CR-17" is just a code, and the chunk that names it was not near the question.

**Questions that need two documents (about 50).** "Can a farmer with an open farming loan also get an emergency loan, and what is the most they can owe in total?" The farming loan policy and the emergency loan policy each hold half of the answer. Search returned three chunks from one of them.

**Tables (about 40).** "What is the monthly rate for a 12-month tier B loan?" The rate table was cut into chunks of rows with no headers, so a chunk said "12 | 1.9 | 2.1 | 2.4" with nothing to say what those numbers mean.

**Questions that never needed search (about 30).** "Thanks, that helped." "Make this message to a borrower more polite." The assistant searched the policies anyway and answered with a citation to the late-fee rule.

The last 20 were real model mistakes. So 180 of 200 failures were retrieval problems, and a better model would have fixed almost none of them.

Sorting took them one afternoon, and the method is worth copying. For each question they opened the log, looked at the chunks that were retrieved, and asked the check from chapter 3.2: was the right passage in the prompt? If not, they searched the documents by hand to find where the answer really lived, and wrote one short label for why search missed it. The labels, not the techniques they had read about, decided what to build next. Omar admitted he had wanted to start with a knowledge graph. The labels showed that a graph would have fixed almost none of the 200.

## What this module adds

Plain vector search is one stage: embed the question, take the nearest chunks. Each failure above needs an extra stage before or after it. Interviewers expect you to know these stages, what each one fixes, and what each one costs in latency and money.

## Why interviewers ask about this

Once you have described a basic RAG pipeline, a senior interviewer pushes on it: "Your recall at 5 is 0.72. What would you try next? How much latency does that add? How would you know it worked?" They are not looking for the longest list of techniques. They want you to match a technique to a failure you can see in your data, and to say its price.

Omar and Huda's 200 questions are the right starting point for that answer. Before you pick any technique, you sort your failures. Then each chapter in this module is a tool for one pile.
