## The question that ends weak answers

Huda is in a project deep dive for an AI engineer role. For ten minutes it goes well. She explains her document assistant for Sahel Relief (a fictional NGO that writes grant and field reports). She draws the pipeline. She explains her chunk size.

Then the interviewer asks one short question: "How do you know it works?"

Huda says she tried about twenty questions and the answers looked good. The interviewer nods and asks three more questions. Which twenty questions? How often did the right report come back in the top three? When you changed the chunk size last month, did quality go up or down, and by how much? Huda has no numbers. The rest of the round feels like a slow slide downhill.

Huda's assistant may be good. That is not the problem. The problem is that she cannot show it, and she cannot tell if a change made it worse.

## The quiet failure

The same thing happens in production, and there it costs more than an interview.

At Sahel Relief, a teammate called Tamer shortens the chunks from 300 words to 120 to make answers faster. He asks the assistant five questions, the answers look fine, and he ships the change. For two weeks, field staff get answers that miss half of the facts they need. Nobody notices, because nobody measures. Staff simply stop trusting the tool and go back to searching folders by hand.

Nothing crashed. No error appeared in any log. The quality dropped, and the system had no way to say so. This is the normal way an LLM system fails: quietly, one change at a time.

## What changes when you measure

A good answer to "How do you know it works?" has three parts. You have a fixed set of test questions with known right sources. You measure retrieval and answers with named metrics. You run that measurement again after every change, so a drop fails like a broken test.

Here is the kind of answer Huda will be able to give by the end of this module: "I keep 60 questions with labelled source chunks. Recall at 5 is 0.88 and MRR is 0.71. I check faithfulness on 40 answers each week. The eval runs on every pull request, and it fails if recall drops below 0.85." That answer is short. It is full of numbers. It invites follow-up questions that Huda can answer.

## What this module covers

This module moves from measuring retrieval to measuring answers, then to making quality a test that can fail.

- **Retrieval metrics** (5.2). You compute recall@k, precision@k and mean reciprocal rank by hand, and see how they move as k changes.
- **Faithfulness and grounding** (5.3). You check if every claim in an answer is supported by the retrieved text, and you learn ways to catch made-up claims.
- **LLM-as-judge** (5.4). You design a judge prompt with a rubric, and you learn the biases that make a model judge wrong.
- **Build a regression eval** (5.5). On your laptop, you write metric functions, a golden set of Bustan Books questions, and a test that fails when quality drops.
- **Quiz and gate** (5.6, 5.7). Graded scenarios, then a written answer to "How do you know your RAG system works?"

## Why interviewers keep asking this

Many candidates can build a RAG demo in an afternoon. Far fewer can say how good it is, or prove that last week's change did not break it. So the evaluation question separates people who have shipped from people who have only built a demo.

In the project deep dive, the interviewer asks about your metrics and your golden set. In the system design round, they ask how you would evaluate a new system before launch. In the managerial round, they ask how you noticed a quality problem in production. The same idea answers all three: decide what good means, measure it with numbers, and run the measurement every time something changes.

## Before you continue

Think about the last LLM feature you built or used. Write how you would show someone that it works. Be honest: if you only tried a few questions by hand, say so. You will come back to this answer in the gate.
