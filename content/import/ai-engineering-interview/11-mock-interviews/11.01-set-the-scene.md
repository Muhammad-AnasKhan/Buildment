## Huda's first mock

Huda finished modules 3 to 10. She built the retrieval pipeline, the regression eval, the approval graph and the async service. Her tests pass. She knows what recall@k means and why a reranker only sees a short list.

Then her friend Tamer ran a mock interview with her over an audio call. His first question was simple: "Tell me how you chose your chunk size." Huda talked for six minutes. She explained tokens, overlap, three splitting rules and a blog post she once read. She never said which size she chose, and she never gave a number from her own tests.

Tamer's second question was harder: "What would you do differently?" Huda went quiet, then said, "I think it went well, actually." The call ended early because the power went out, but Tamer had already written his notes. Strong knowledge, weak answers. He could not tell what she had decided or how she knew it worked.

## Knowing is not the same as answering

An interview is a short, timed sample of how you think. The interviewer has 45 to 60 minutes and a scoring sheet. They listen for a few signals: a clear decision, a number that supports it, a trade-off you can name, and an honest limit. A long answer with no decision scores lower than a short answer with one.

That is why this module is about answering, not about new topics. Modules 2 to 10 gave you the material. Here you practise turning it into answers that fit each round, under a timer, and you get used to the questions that come after your first answer.

## How to use the question banks

Before you read, decide how you will practise. Huda had 30 minutes on the evening after her mock, while the power was on.


The method in every bank is the same. Read the question. Close your eyes or look away, start a timer, and answer out loud. Only then open the item and compare. Score yourself with four checks: did my first sentence answer the question, did I give a number, did I name a trade-off or alternative, and did I stop inside the time? Then answer once more, better. The second answer is where you learn.

Model answers use fictional projects. Do not memorise them. Use their shape with your own project from module 1 and your build from modules 3 to 9.

## Practising alone

You need a phone and a quiet place. Record yourself on the phone's voice recorder, which works offline and uses no data. Listen back once at normal speed. Count your filler words and note where you lost the thread. Most people find that their answers are twice as long as they thought.

Keep a short log in a notebook: the question, your time, your four-check score, and one thing to change. Ten minutes a day for a week is better than one long session.

## Practising with a partner

A partner is better than practising alone, because a partner can surprise you with probes. One person is the interviewer. They pick a question, read the probes in advance, and ask two of them, even if the answer was good. The candidate answers. Then the interviewer gives two lines of feedback: one strength and one thing to change. Swap roles.

An audio-only call is enough, and it uses much less data than video. If the connection is weak, run the mock in text chat: the interviewer types the question, the candidate types the answer within the time limit. It is slower, but it still trains structure.

<Callout type="tip" title="Ask probes even after a good answer">
The interviewer's job in practice is to push. Asking "Why not the other option?" after a strong answer is how the candidate learns to give depth without being asked twice.
</Callout>
