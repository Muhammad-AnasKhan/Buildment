## Learning log

1. **Your hardest round:** Which round felt hardest when you answered aloud? Which of its common mistakes did you make most?
2. **Your four checks:** Across your practice answers, which check failed most often: a first sentence that answers the question, a number, a trade-off, or staying inside the time?
3. **A probe that stopped you:** Write down one follow-up probe you could not answer well in practice. What would you say now?
4. **Before and after:** Compare your first recorded or timed answer with your latest one. What changed in length, structure and numbers?
5. **Your plan:** How many mock interviews will you run before your next real interview, with whom, and when?

## Your interview answer

This is the same task as the design round in chapter 11.4, but written. A mentor reads your answer and replies with one to three follow-up probes, just as an interviewer would. You answer those by editing and resubmitting.

Here is the prompt, as an interviewer would say it. Nabd University (a fictional university) has 8,000 students. It wants an assistant that answers students' questions from its 300-page student handbook and exam regulations, in English and Arabic. The assistant can also submit a request to defer an exam, which a staff member must approve. Traffic is low most of the year and high during exam weeks.

Prepare it the way you would in a real round. Set a 40-minute timer and talk through the seven steps aloud, writing short notes on paper. Then write the answer in the box. Writing usually takes another 30 to 40 minutes. You cannot draw a diagram in the box, so describe the parts and the request path in words, as a short list if that is clearer. Where the prompt does not give a number, state an assumption and use it, as you would with an interviewer: "I assume each student asks two questions a day in exam weeks."

Your mentor looks for the same things an interviewer would. Did you clarify the users and what a good answer is before you designed anything? Did you turn the prompt into numbers, and did those numbers change a decision? Is the approval step in the right place, before the deferral request is sent? Does your evaluation plan name a golden set, at least two metrics, and a threshold that would fail a test? Did you name at least two failure modes, each with a defence? A short, clear answer that covers all seven steps scores better than a long one that stops at the diagram.

If you can, also record yourself. Two options work. You can present this design aloud in five minutes, as if the interviewer had just said "Walk me through it". Or you can answer one question from the 11.2 or 11.5 bank in under three minutes, then the two follow-up probes. Use your phone's voice recorder. Audio only is fine, and it uploads faster on a weak connection than video. Put the file somewhere your mentor can open with a link and without signing in. Do not show your surroundings or anyone else. The recording is optional; the written design is required, so write it even if you record.
