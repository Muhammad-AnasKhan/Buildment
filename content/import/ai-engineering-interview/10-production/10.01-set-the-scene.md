## Two weeks of quiet failure

Wadi Labs (a fictional remote software team of 12) runs an internal knowledge bot. Engineers ask it questions about runbooks and past decisions: how to roll back a deploy, who owns the billing service, why the team chose one queue over another. Ahmad built the bot and looks after it next to his main work.

One week, two small changes went out. A teammate changed the chunker to split runbooks at every heading, which made chunks much shorter. Ahmad updated the prompt from version 4 to version 5 to make answers more concise. Both changes looked harmless. The tests passed, the service stayed up, and every request still returned 200 in about 3 seconds.

Two weeks later, at 2 a.m., the database for the main product stopped accepting writes. The engineer on call, Nour, asked the bot how to fail over to the standby database. The bot gave the steps for the old database setup, which the team had retired months before. Nour lost 25 minutes before a colleague woke up and pointed to the right runbook.

## Looking back

In the review the next day, the team found that the problems had started two weeks earlier. In this example, the share of answers marked "not helpful" had grown from about 6% to 19%. The share of "I don't know" answers had grown from 4% to 15%. The data existed, but nobody looked at it, and no alert watched it.

Then the team tried to find the cause, and every question was hard to answer:

- **Which change did it?** Nobody had recorded which chunk size, prompt version and embedding setup were live on which day. The evaluation from launch day lived in a notebook on Ahmad's laptop, with no record of its settings.
- **What did the bot see?** The logs had one line per request: the path, the status and the time. There was no record of which chunks retrieval returned for Nour's question, so nobody could tell a retrieval failure from a model failure.
- **Why did nobody notice?** The dashboards showed uptime, error rate and latency. All three were fine the whole time. Nothing measured whether the answers were good.

It took Ahmad two days to rebuild the old index, re-run the evaluation by hand and show that the shorter chunks had split the failover runbook so that the important steps landed in a chunk retrieval rarely found. The fix took one hour. Finding it took two days.

## What this module covers

Every part of that story maps to one chapter. Experiment tracking records the settings, metrics and files of each evaluation run, so "which change did it?" has an answer in minutes. Tracing records each step of each request, such as retrieval, the model call and tool calls, so you can see what the bot saw. Deployment is the choice of where the service runs, and it shapes cost, cold starts and how long a request may take. Monitoring watches quality in production, not only uptime, and alerts a person when it drops.

The managerial round asks about exactly this. "How did you know your system worked after launch?" and "Tell me about an incident" are two of the most common questions for AI engineers. A candidate who can describe what they tracked, what they traced and what alerted them, with numbers, sounds like someone who has run a system, not only built one.
