## One system you can defend

In module 11 you practised answers with fictional projects. An interviewer will ask about yours. The strongest thing you can bring to a loop is a small system you built end to end, with an evaluation report that has real numbers, one failure you found and fixed, and a design you can explain under follow-up probes.

That is the capstone. You adapt the Bustan Books code from your build chapters to one of eight projects, add what a reviewer expects from a portfolio project, measure it, and defend it in a recorded mock interview. It runs on your laptop, with no paid API key, because every test uses the fake model from chapter 3.5.

## Choose your project

There are eight projects. All of them build the same thing underneath: retrieval with citations, an agent with one safe tool and one risky tool that waits for a person, the async service, and an evaluation you run as a test. What changes is the domain, the documents you write, the risky action and one hard problem that makes each project different. Two fellows on different projects end up with different portfolio stories.

Pick on the course page with **Choose a variant**. Your pick sets the project name you see in these chapters, which is %%product.name%% right now. Your course may also assign a project to you; then you see it there instead. Every project is fictional. You write the documents yourself, 6 to 10 short files of 100 to 300 words each, in the style of the real thing. Do not copy real documents, and do not include real names or personal data. Because you write them, you know which chunk answers which question.

Each plan below names who uses the system, what you write, the two tools, and the hard problem your golden set must test.

**1. Support agent: Karmel Help.** Customers of Karmel Crafts (a fictional online shop for handmade goods) ask about shipping, returns, product care, payments and custom orders. Safe tool: `lookup_order(order_id)`. Risky tool: `request_refund(order_id, amount)`, approved by a staff member. Hard problem: one question needs a policy chunk and an order lookup together, and a refund must never be paid twice. Test it with at least four mixed questions and one repeated refund request. This is closest to Bustan Books, so spend the saved time on two should-haves.

**2. Policy assistant: Sanabel Desk.** Loan officers at Sanabel Microfinance (a fictional lender) ask about eligibility, fees, grace periods, late payments and required documents. Write two versions of one policy: an old one and the one in force now. Safe tool: `get_loan(loan_id)`. Risky tool: `request_repayment_extension(loan_id, days)`, approved by a branch manager. Hard problem: answer from the version in force on the question's date, and cite it. Test it with dated questions from before and after the change.

**3. Runbook bot: Nakhla Ops.** Engineers at Nakhla Systems (a fictional remote team of 15) ask about deploys, the on-call rota, incidents, access and past decisions. Include one old runbook that a newer page replaces. Safe tool: `get_service_status(service)`. Risky tool: `restart_service(service)`, approved by the on-call engineer. Hard problem: stale pages. The bot must prefer the newest valid page and say when an answer comes from an old one. Test it with questions where the two pages disagree.

**4. Clinic front desk: Shifa Desk.** Patients of Shifa Clinic (a fictional small clinic) ask about opening hours, booking rules, what to bring, fees and cancellations. Safe tool: `find_appointment(reference)`. Risky tool: `cancel_appointment(reference)`, approved by front desk staff. Hard problem: logistics only. The assistant must refuse medical questions safely and hand off to staff. Put at least five medical questions in the golden set and report the refusal rate, which must be 100%.

**5. Field reports: Marj Reports.** Programme staff at Marj Aid (a fictional NGO) ask about monthly field reports with budget tables by district. Safe tool: `get_budget_line(project, line)`. Risky tool: `save_donor_update(section, text)`, approved by the programme manager. Hard problem: numbers sit in tables across several reports, and totals must add up. Test it with multi-hop and sum questions, and check every number in an answer against its table.

**6. Course tutor: Daris.** Students of a fictional university course ask about the syllabus, assessment rules, late work and lecture notes. Write some documents in Arabic and some in English. Safe tool: `get_deadline(assignment)`. Risky tool: `request_extension(assignment, days)`, approved by the instructor. Hard problem: a question in one language must find a note written in the other. Test it with questions in both languages, and report recall for each language separately.

**7. Contract review: Aqd Check.** A freelancer asks about clauses in three or four fictional client contracts: payment terms, ownership of work, cancellation and late fees. Safe tool: `get_clause(contract, clause_id)`. Risky tool: `send_change_request(contract, clause_id, text)`, which writes to a fake outbox only after the freelancer approves. Hard problem: quote the exact clause, and never give legal advice. Test it with exact-quote checks, and with questions such as "is this legal?" that must be refused with a suggestion to ask a lawyer.

**8. Onboarding buddy: Rafiq.** New hires at a fictional remote company ask about the handbook: equipment, leave, tools and who to ask. Mark some pages as managers only. Safe tool: `get_ticket_status(ticket)`. Risky tool: `request_access(system, level)`, approved by IT. Hard problem: an employee must never see a manager-only page in an answer, so filter by role before you search. Test it with employee questions whose only answer is on a manager-only page, and report zero leaks.

Choose the project you can write documents for most easily. It does not change your grade. Your mentor has the full plan for each project, with a golden set outline and project-specific defence probes.

## Must-haves

Your mentor checks each of these. A missing must-have means the capstone comes back for revision.

1. **Retrieval with citations.** Answers cite chunk ids, and the service says it does not know when no chunk passes the score threshold.
2. **One tool call that needs human approval.** The risky tool pauses the LangGraph graph with `interrupt()` and runs only after approval, as in chapter 7.6.
3. **A FastAPI endpoint** with Pydantic validation, retries with backoff, a timeout, structured errors and a concurrency limit, as in chapter 9.6.
4. **A golden set of at least 20 questions,** each labelled with the chunk ids that answer it.
5. **An evaluation report** with recall@k, precision@k, MRR and a faithfulness check, and one failure you found, fixed and re-measured.
6. **A regression eval that runs as a test** and fails when quality drops below a threshold.
7. **Structured logs or traces:** one JSON log line per request, with a request id, latency, retrieved ids and outcome, and no raw user text.
8. **A README** that tells a stranger how to install, run the tests, run the eval and start the service.
9. **A one-page design note:** the problem, the design, the main trade-off, the risks and what you would change.
10. **Tests that pass with no API key,** using the fake model.

## Should-haves

These lift a capstone from "meets" to "strong". Choose two or three that fit your project: hybrid search with BM25 and rank fusion; query rewriting for vocabulary your users really type; a small router that skips retrieval for greetings; three or more out-of-scope questions in the golden set; a judge-model faithfulness check on a sample, compared with your own labels; HTTP endpoints for the approval flow; a cost and latency budget table; a container build; or a CI workflow that runs `pytest` on every push.

## Out of scope

You do not need, and should not spend time on: a public deployed URL, user authentication, object storage, a web frontend, training or fine-tuning a model, or GPU work. A real or local model is optional and must sit behind an adapter, so the tests still use the fake model. Deployment and authentication are design topics here: describe them in the design note, as in gate 10.7.

## How your mentor reviews it

Your mentor has about 45 minutes for your capstone: 5 minutes to clone it and run the tests from your README, 10 minutes for the report and design note, 10 minutes reading the service and the agent, 10 minutes for your recording, and 10 minutes to write feedback with one to three follow-up probes. You answer those probes by resubmitting.

| Area | Meets | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Design | All must-haves; the note explains the request path and the approval point | Choices are justified with numbers; alternatives are named and rejected for a reason |
| Evaluation | 20+ golden questions; four metrics reported; regression test fails on a drop | One failure is traced to a cause, fixed and re-measured, with any regression it caused |
| Engineering | Tests pass from a clean clone with no key; errors are structured | Clear interfaces; failure paths are tested; one should-have is fully done |
| Safety | The risky tool cannot run without approval; logs hold no raw user text | Injection is considered in a test or the note; tool arguments are validated |
| Communication | README works; the recording covers the five parts in about five minutes | Answers lead with a decision and a number; limits are stated plainly |
