## Your interview answer

In module 1 you wrote a first project story, before you had learned most of this course. It was a baseline. Now you write the story again, about your capstone, as you would tell it when an interviewer says "Tell me about a project you are proud of." A mentor reads both and replies with feedback and, often, one or two follow-up probes, which you answer by editing and resubmitting.

Use the five parts from chapter 1.3: the problem, your design, the numbers, a trade-off, and what you would change. Your capstone gives you material for every part. The problem is your scenario's users and what a good answer means for them. The design is the request path, including where the human approval sits. The numbers come straight from your evaluation report. The trade-off is one choice you made and what it cost. What you would change is the next step from your report, with the number you expect it to move.

Keep the story to about two minutes spoken, which is roughly 250 to 300 words. Lead with the decision or the number in each part, as you practised in chapter 11.2. Say "I" for what you built. Include one honest limit, such as what your faithfulness check cannot see or what your golden set does not cover. A story with a limit is more convincing than a story where everything worked.

After the story, write two or three sentences comparing it with your first one. Be specific. "My first story had no numbers; this one has four" is useful. "I improved a lot" is not. If your first story was about a different project, compare the shape rather than the content: which of the five parts were missing then, and which are strong now?

If you can, also record the story. Speak it once with a two-minute timer and listen back before you share it. Audio only is fine. The recording is optional; the written story and comparison are what your mentor reviews.

Your mentor reads with the same eyes as an interviewer. They look for a first sentence that says what you built and for whom, at least three real numbers from your report, a trade-off with its cost, a limit stated plainly, and a change that follows from your evidence. They also look at the comparison, because seeing how your answers changed is the best measure of what this course gave you.
