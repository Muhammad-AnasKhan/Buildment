## Your loop diagram

This task asks you to look at one system and describe its loops in words. You do not need to draw boxes and arrows, although you can if you like. A clear list of links, each with "more" or "fewer", is a loop diagram in words. The aim is to show two things. You can see causes that come back round, and you can find the delay or bottleneck that shapes how the system behaves over time.

Writing a loop in words is simpler than it sounds. Take one link at a time, and write it as a short sentence with "more" or "fewer" in it. "More students means less time for each one." "Less time for each one means smaller gains in marks." Keep going until your last sentence leads back to the thing you started with. If it does not lead back, you have a chain, not a loop. That is fine to notice, and it tells you to look for the missing link.

You can use a system from your own study, work, family or community. It could be a small business you know, a team you work in, a study habit, or a group you belong to. Pick something you have watched for at least a few weeks, so you have seen it change. If you prefer not to use your own life, use the provided case below. It has every fact you need.

**The provided case: Salma's tutoring group.** Salma is a university student who tutors school students in maths at her home, in the afternoons. Her room holds 8 students at a time, and she runs one session a day. Six months ago she had 3 students. Their marks improved, their parents told other parents, and now she has 8 students and a waiting list of 5. With 8 students, she has less time for each one. Two parents have said their children get less help than before. Marks only show at the end of each school term, which is months after a change. Salma is thinking about running a second session each day, but she also has her own exams coming.

If you use Salma's case, read it twice before you write. Mark each fact that says "more" or "fewer", because each one is a possible link. Then look for the fact that is about time, and the fact that is about a fixed amount of space or hours. You will need both. There is more than one good answer, so your mentor will look at your reasons, not for one set of words.

A good answer contains these parts:

- the system and its main parts, named in a sentence or two
- one loop where more leads to more, written link by link, with the direction it is running now
- one loop that pulls back toward a limit, with the limit named
- one delay or one bottleneck, and what it does to the system
- what you expect to happen over the next few months if nothing changes
- one change you would make, and one number you would watch, for how long

The parts work together. The loops explain what has happened so far. The delay or bottleneck explains why the system will not simply keep going the same way. Your prediction follows from both. Your change should act on one link of a loop, or on the bottleneck, and the number you watch should tell you whether that link really moved.

There are some slips to avoid. Do not blame a person for what a loop is doing; name the link instead. Do not suggest "work harder" unless you can show the extra work goes to the slowest step. And do not claim the change will surely work. Say what you will watch and for how long, as you learned in module 3. A careful "I expect this, and I will check it by watching that" is stronger than a confident promise.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor reads your loop diagram with four criteria. Each criterion has three levels, and the table says what each level looks like.

| Criterion | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loops | Two loops, each link has "more" or "fewer", each closes back on itself | One loop is clear, the other does not close | No loop, only a list of causes |
| Direction and limit | Says which way the reinforcing loop runs and names the limit | Names one of the two | Names neither |
| Delay or bottleneck | Named, with what it does to the system | Named, but its effect is not said | Missing |
| Change and measure | One change on a link or the bottleneck, one number, and how long to watch | A change, but no number or time | No change, or "work harder" |

**Pass rule:** your answer passes when Loops is Met and no criterion is Not yet. A short answer can pass; the criteria matter, not the length.

**What happens next:** your mentor replies with one or two short questions. For example: "If Salma adds a second session, where will the new bottleneck be?" You answer them in writing or in a voice note. Your answers count as part of the gate, so an answer at "Partly" can still pass once your replies fill the gap.
