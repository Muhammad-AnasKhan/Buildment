```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Raghad runs a 10-week evening sewing course at a community centre in her town. Forty students started it. On the last evening, she asks the 18 students still coming what they liked most. She plans next year's course from their answers. What is the biggest gap in her evidence?"
options:
  - "Eighteen answers are too few to plan a course"
  - "She should have asked on the first evening"
  - "Students may not be fully honest at the end"
  - "The 22 students who left were never asked"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "Eighteen answers can still be useful. The bigger problem is which students they come from."
  - "On the first evening, students have not seen the course yet, so they cannot say what worked."
  - "Honesty may be a small issue, but it is not the biggest gap. Even fully honest answers come only from those who stayed."
  - "Right. The students who left may have left because of what did not work. Asking only those who stayed is survivorship bias."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Ghada manages a team of 12 at a taxi company's booking desk. Last month, the 3 staff who got the most help from senior colleagues made 14 of the team's 20 mistakes. She decides that senior help is causing mistakes. Which check fits best?"
options:
  - "Check whether the help came after the mistakes"
  - "Count how many senior colleagues the team has"
  - "Stop all senior help for a month and then wait"
  - "Ask the seniors whether they enjoy helping"
correct: 0
explanations:
  - "Right. The cause may run the other way: staff who make mistakes are given more help. Checking what came first shows the direction."
  - "The number of seniors does not show which way the cause runs between help and mistakes."
  - "This changes the team before checking the simplest idea. Staff who need help would lose it, and the result would still be hard to read."
  - "Whether seniors enjoy helping says nothing about whether help causes mistakes or mistakes bring help."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Kinda runs a clinic's front desk. March's average wait was 48 minutes, the longest in a year. In April she started a booking card, and the wait fell to 31 minutes. The usual wait is 30. The clinic's second desk, which changed nothing, went from 45 to 32. Which conclusion fits?"
options:
  - "The booking card cut waiting by about 17 minutes"
  - "Most of the fall would have happened anyway"
  - "The booking card made no difference at all"
  - "The second desk needs the booking card too"
correct: 1
explanations:
  - "The second desk fell by 13 minutes with no change at all. So most of the 17 minutes cannot be credited to the card."
  - "Right. March was unusual at both desks, and both moved back near the usual 30 minutes. The card desk ended only 1 minute lower than the other desk."
  - "One month and a 1-minute gap cannot show that the card did nothing. The facts only show that most of the fall was not the card."
  - "The second desk went back to 32 minutes without the card, close to the usual 30. Nothing here shows it needs one."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Wafa sells honey at a farm stall every Friday. On her last 10 Fridays with her old sign, she sold 8 jars on average, anywhere from 5 to 12. She then tried a new sign for 2 Fridays and sold 9 and 11 jars. Which conclusion fits these facts best?"
options:
  - "The new sign adds about 2 jars each Friday"
  - "The new sign does not work and should go"
  - "Two Fridays are too few to tell; try more"
  - "The sign only helps on the busier Fridays"
correct: 2
explanations:
  - "Both new results, 9 and 11, sit inside her normal range of 5 to 12. With the old sign she had Fridays like these too."
  - "Sales were not lower with the new sign. Nothing in the facts shows it does harm."
  - "Right. Her sales already move between 5 and 12 jars. Two Fridays inside that range could be luck, so this is a hint at most."
  - "Nothing in the facts says which Fridays were busy. This adds a new idea that the numbers do not show."
```

```quiz
type: mcq
question: "Loay runs a barber shop. In 6 months, his 60 members made 2 complaints about waiting and his 40 walk-ins made 9, counting those who later left. Members book fixed weekly times; walk-ins come at busy hours. An AI chat tool says: 'Members complain far less, so membership makes customers happier.' What is the main mistake?"
options:
  - "It misreads the numbers: members complain more"
  - "It ignores the customers who later left"
  - "Eleven complaints are too few to see a pattern"
  - "It misses that fixed times may explain both"
correct: 3
explanations:
  - "The numbers are read correctly: 2 complaints from 60 members, against 9 from 40 walk-ins. Members do complain less."
  - "Loay's count already includes the customers who later left, so they are not missing."
  - "The gap is large: 2 in 60 against 9 in 40. The bigger problem is reading the gap as the effect of membership itself."
  - "Right. Members book fixed times, so they wait less, and people who want fixed times are the ones who join. That third factor could explain both, so signing up walk-ins may change little."
```
