## Test one claim four ways

This task brings the whole module together. You take one claim where two things move together, and you test it with each of the four checks. They are a hidden third factor, reverse cause, chance and the drift back to normal, and selection. You do not need data or a calculator. You need to think clearly about what else could produce the same pattern, and what evidence would tell the explanations apart.

You can use the claim you wrote in chapter 2.1, or any claim you have heard at work, in study or online. If you prefer not to use your own, use this provided claim. "Students who sit in the front row of a lecture get higher marks than students who sit at the back." It works well for all four checks. A mentor will read it in exactly the same way as a claim of your own.

## What a strong answer looks like

Here is a worked example, so you can see the level of detail that helps. Reem is a freelance copywriter. In an online group for freelancers, she reads a post: "Freelancers who reply to clients within one hour get better ratings. Reply fast and your ratings will go up." The post compares 12 freelancers the author knows. Reem decides to test the claim before she changes her whole day around fast replies.

First, a hidden third factor. Reem asks who has more of both. Experienced freelancers often have good systems and a manageable number of clients. That lets them reply fast, and it also lets them do careful work, which earns good ratings. Too much work could push both sides the other way: an overloaded freelancer replies slowly and also delivers rushed work. To compare like with like, Reem would compare freelancers with similar experience and a similar number of clients.

Second, reverse cause. Could good ratings lead to fast replies? Freelancers with high ratings can choose fewer, better clients. With fewer clients, they have time to reply quickly. So the ratings may come first. Reem would check timing: did these freelancers reply fast before their ratings rose, or only after?

Third, chance and the drift back to normal. The post is based on 12 freelancers, which is a very small group. A pattern in 12 people can easily appear by luck, so Reem treats it as a hint. She also asks how the 12 were chosen. If the author picked people after a very good month, their next month will probably look more ordinary.

Fourth, selection. The author knows these 12 freelancers from a group for successful freelancers. Freelancers who replied fast and still got poor ratings, or who left freelancing, are probably not in that group. The missing people could tell a different story.

Reem ends with a smaller, true sentence: "Among 12 successful freelancers one person knows, the fast repliers had better ratings. That does not show that replying fast causes better ratings." She also names her next check. For one month, she will flip a coin for each new client: heads, she replies within an hour; tails, she replies within her usual half day. Then she will compare. Module 3 teaches how to plan a test like that properly.

Notice what makes Reem's answer strong. Each check names a specific explanation, not just the word "chance" or "selection". Each one says what evidence would tell the explanations apart. And her conclusion is no bigger than her evidence.

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor is not grading whether your claim is true or false. Many claims will still be uncertain after this task, and that is an honest result. The mentor looks for the four checks, each with a specific possible explanation and a way to check it. The mentor also looks for a final sentence that matches the evidence, and for one sensible next step. If one of the four checks really does not fit your claim, say so and explain why. That is better than forcing an explanation that does not make sense.

You can write in short paragraphs, or one check per line. If writing in English is slow for you, you can also add a voice note and keep the written answer short and clear. Your mentor will listen for the same parts: the claim, the four checks, the smaller true sentence and the next step. The table shows what your mentor looks for in each part.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claim | One clear sentence about two things that move together | Clear, but mixes in a second claim | Missing, or not about two things |
| Third factor | Names a factor that could push both sides, and how to compare like with like | Names a factor, but no way to check it | No factor, or one that pushes only one side |
| Reverse cause | Says how the cause could run the other way, and how to check what came first | Mentions the other direction, with no check | Not considered |
| Chance or drift | Names the group size or an unusual start, and what that means | Says "it could be chance", with no reason | Not considered |
| Selection | Says who may be missing from the group, and why | Says people may be missing, but not who | Not considered |
| Conclusion | A smaller true sentence and one next check that fits the evidence | Has one of the two | Claims more than the evidence shows |

Your answer passes when at least four parts are Met and no part is Not yet. After reading, your mentor replies with one or two short questions. For example: "What evidence would tell your third factor apart from a real effect?" You answer them in a few sentences. The gate is complete when you have answered.
