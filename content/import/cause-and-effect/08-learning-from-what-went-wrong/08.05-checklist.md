## Course checklist

- [ ] I can draw a chain of causes and tell a direct cause from a contributing one
- [ ] Before I call a pattern a cause, I check for a hidden third factor, reverse cause, chance and selection
- [ ] I can plan a fair test: one change, a comparison, and the measure and number of cases decided in advance
- [ ] I describe a problem exactly, test one cause at a time, and find a root cause with evidence and a strong fix
- [ ] I look for loops, delays and the slowest step, and I ask "and then what?" before a change
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 8.2 and 8.3, submitted my capstone in 8.4, and submitted my review below

## Your cause-and-effect toolkit

The table below holds one line for each habit in the course. It is short on purpose. When a new problem comes to you at work or at home, you will not have time to reread eight modules. You will have time to read twelve lines.

| Habit | One line to remember |
|---|---|
| Chains | Draw causes back from the problem; most effects have several causes |
| After is not because | Coming first is not proof; ask what else changed at the same time |
| Moving together | A clue, not proof: check a third factor, reverse cause, chance and selection |
| Fair tests | Change one thing, compare with a group that did not change, fix the measure first |
| Root causes | Ask why, answer by checking, and stop at a process you can change |
| Fishbone | List possible causes in groups, then choose which ones to check |
| Strong fixes | A reminder asks people to remember; a better design means they do not have to |
| Describe exactly | What, where, when, how much, and what is not affected |
| Test and split | One cause at a time; in a chain of steps, test the middle |
| Loops | Look for more leading to more, loops that pull back, delays and the slowest step |
| And then what? | Follow a decision forward; watch for side effects and numbers hit without the work |
| Reviews | Ask what made the mistake easy, and write what will change, with an owner and a date |

Copy the table somewhere you will see it: the notes app on your phone, the first page of a notebook, or a message you send to yourself. Read it before a meeting where someone will explain why something went wrong. Read it before you send a client an explanation. Much of the value of this course is in these lines being near you at the right moment.

You do not need every line every time. A broken charger needs "describe exactly" and "test and split". A claim that a new tool raised sales needs "moving together" and "fair tests". A rule your team wants to introduce needs "and then what?". Part of the skill is choosing which two or three lines fit the problem in front of you. You practised that choice in chapter 8.3, and it gets easier with use.

### How to look back at your first chain

The first question in the learning log matters most. In module 1 you drew a chain for a problem you had seen, or for the provided case of Hana's phone. You drew it before you knew most of the habits in this course. Looking at it again is a clear way to see how your thinking has changed.

Here is an example from a fictional fellow, Nisreen. In module 1 she used Hana's phone, whose battery was empty by about 3 in the afternoon. Her chain was short: the phone is three years old, so the battery is worn out, so it runs out early.

Reading it now, she sees three things. First, it is a single-cause chain, the trap from module 1. Two other things changed in the same two weeks: a new video app, and the screen brightness turned up to full. Second, she skipped the clearest evidence in the case. The battery page said the video app used 40 percent of the battery for only 20 minutes of watching. The app also keeps playing quietly after Hana leaves it. That is much more than timing.

Third, she never planned a test. She would now change one thing at a time. First she would set the app so it cannot play in the background, and keep the brightness the same. For a few whole days, she would write down the battery level at the same hour each evening. Only then would she try the brightness.

Nisreen also noticed something she had done well. Her chain went back several steps instead of stopping at "the battery is bad". That habit was there from the start. She also sees that changing the app setting is a strong fix. "Remember to close the app" is a weak one, because it asks Hana to remember every time.

Write your own look-back the same way: what you missed, what evidence you would collect, what you would test, and one thing you already did well. It does not need to be long. Four or five honest sentences show your mentor more than a page of general words.

If you would rather not write about your own work or home, you can answer questions 2 to 4 with the cases you met. Use Huda's bakery, Amal's deliveries, Nour's laptop, Manal's study centre or the Young Coders Club form. Your mentor reads these answers for how you think, not for details about your life.

## Your review without blame

In chapter 8.2 you saw a full review of the lost sign-ups at the Young Coders Club. Now you write one yourself. It is short, about 120 words. The aim is to practise the four parts that make a review useful: a timeline, the impact, causes in steps, tools or information, and actions that someone owns.

You can review something small that went wrong for you, in study, work or a project. Change any names, and leave out private details. Or you can use the provided incident below, so that you do not need to share anything personal.

### The provided incident

Taghreed runs a small cleaning company (a fictional company) with 6 cleaners. On Friday, an office client phoned to move Monday's cleaning to Tuesday. Sahar took the call and wrote the change in her own notebook. The shared booking sheet still said Monday. On Monday at 8:00, two cleaners arrived at the office and found it locked. They waited an hour, then went back. That morning, Taghreed had turned away another client who wanted a Monday slot, because the sheet showed no free cleaners. The office client was not charged. The two cleaners were paid for a morning with no work.

### What a good review contains

- A timeline: the events in order, with days and times, written as facts
- The impact: who was affected, and how much
- Two causes, each about a step, a tool or information, not about a person
- Two actions, each with one owner and a date, and at least one strong fix

### How your mentor will read it

Your mentor uses the table below. Each line has three levels.

| Line | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Timeline | Events in order, with days or times, written as facts | Events listed, but out of order or mixed with blame | No timeline |
| Impact | Who was affected, and how much, with a number | Who was affected, but no amount | No impact given |
| Causes | Two causes in steps, tools or information; nobody blamed | One cause, or a cause that still ends at a person | Only "someone made a mistake" |
| Actions | Two actions, each with one owner and a date; one is strong | An owner or a date is missing, or only reminders | No actions |

To pass, "Causes" and "Actions" must be Met, and no line can be Not yet. If your review does not pass yet, your mentor replies with one or two short questions about the weakest line. You answer them by adding to your review and submitting it again.

### What happens next

Your mentor will read your review and your learning log along with your capstone. They are not grading the log. They are looking at how your thinking has moved since module 1, so they can suggest which habits to keep practising.

The habits in this course will come back in your later courses and in real work. When you debug a program, you will describe the fault exactly and split the problem in half. When something breaks for a client, you will write a short review without blame. When your team sets a new target, you will ask how someone could hit the number without doing the work. You do not need to remember the chapter names. You need the twelve lines, and the habit of reaching for them.
