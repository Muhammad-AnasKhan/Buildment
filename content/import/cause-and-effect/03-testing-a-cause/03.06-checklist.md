2. **Your hopes:** Where are your own hopes most likely to affect a test: choosing the groups, choosing the measure, or deciding when to stop? Why that one?

## Your fair test plan

This task asks you to plan a fair test, not to run one. Many people skip the planning, and that is where many tests go wrong. A plan written before you start protects you from three things. A lucky first week cannot decide the answer. A measure chosen afterwards cannot decide it. A hand-picked group cannot decide it either. If you later run your test, the plan will be ready.

Choose one cause you would like to check. It can come from your study, your freelance work, a small business, a team you are part of, or your daily routine. It should be something you could really test with what you have: a few customers, a few weeks, a few messages. It does not need to be big. "Does studying with my phone in another room help me finish my exercises?" is a good question. "What makes a business succeed?" is too big for one fair test.

Your plan should name six things. First, the cause you want to check, as one exact question. Second, the one change you will make, and the things you will hold still. Third, your comparison: the group, the days or the cases that will not get the change, and how chance will decide which is which. Fourth, the measure you will count, chosen now. Fifth, how many cases or weeks you will run, decided now. Use whole weeks if the days of the week matter. Sixth, what result would show that you were wrong, and how sure a test of this size can make you.

Here is what a good plan looks like, told about a fictional person. Mona sells handmade bags through her online page. She wonders whether a short video of each bag sells more than photos alone. Her question is exact. "Do bags posted with a short video get more orders in their first 14 days than bags posted with photos only?" Over the next eight weeks she will post 16 new bags. Before posting each one, she flips a coin: heads, the bag gets a video and photos; tails, photos only. She keeps everything else the same: the price range, the time of day for posting, and the style of caption. Her measure, chosen now, is orders in the first 14 days after each post. If the video bags get about the same number of orders as the photo bags, she was wrong. Then she will stop spending an hour on each video. She writes that 16 bags is a small test, so even a clear win will be a hint. She will repeat the test next season before she makes videos for everything.

Notice what makes Mona's plan good. The coin decides which bags get a video, so she cannot give videos only to her favourite bags. The measure is fixed before the first post, so she cannot pick a number afterwards. The number of bags and weeks is fixed, so she cannot stop on a lucky week. And she says clearly how sure she can be. None of it needs a formula.

Compare it with a weak plan for the same question. "I will add videos to my next few bags and see if sales go up." That plan has no comparison, so the season decides the answer. It has no fixed measure, so any good number will do. It has no end, so Mona can stop whenever she feels pleased. Weak plans usually fail in these same three places. Check your own plan against them before you submit it.

If you prefer not to use your own situation, use this provided case. Haneen runs a free weekly sewing class at a community centre. Thirty women are on her list, and between 12 and 16 come each week. Haneen wonders whether a short reminder message the evening before each class would bring more people. Write a fair test plan for Haneen, with all six parts. Think about how chance could decide who gets a reminder, and what Haneen should count. Think about how many weeks the test should run, and what would show that reminders make no difference.

## How your mentor will read it

A mentor will read your plan with the table below. They will not mark you down for a small test, or for a cause that turns out to be wrong. A plan that could prove you wrong is a good plan. What matters is that each part is decided before the test starts.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| One change | One change named; at least two things held still | One change, but nothing held still | Two or more changes at once |
| Comparison | Cases split by coin, names or another chance method | A comparison group chosen by hand or from another time | No comparison |
| Measure and length | One measure and a number of cases or weeks, both fixed now | Only one of the two fixed | Neither fixed |
| How sure | Says what result would show it wrong, and calls a small test a hint | Says only one of the two | Neither |

**Pass rule:** your plan passes when no part is "Not yet" and at least three parts are "Met".

The mentor then replies with one or two short questions about your plan. For example: "What would you do if the coin gives you 12 cases on one side and 8 on the other?" You answer them in writing or in a voice note. That reply is part of the gate, so look out for it after you submit.
