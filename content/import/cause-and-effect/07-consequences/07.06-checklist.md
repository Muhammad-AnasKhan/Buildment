## Learning log

1. **Stopping early:** Think of a decision you saw at work, in study or at home. Where did people stop at the first effect? What came next that they did not expect?
2. **Easy paths:** Which group do you find hardest to predict: clients, family, teammates or customers? Why do you think that is?
3. **Your numbers:** Name one number you are judged by, such as marks, ratings or hours. How could someone hit that number without doing the real work?
4. **Watching:** When you change something, what do you usually check afterwards? After this module, what would you add to that check?

These questions are about how you think, not about getting a right answer. Short, honest notes are more useful than long ones. You will read them again at the end of the course, so write what is true for you now.

## Your consequence map

This task asks you to do what Manal, Hamza, Sawsan and Yara learned to do in this module. You follow one decision forward before it happens. The decision can be yours, or one that someone near you is making. It can be small. A clear map of a small decision teaches more than a vague map of a big one.

The point of the task is the habit, not the forecast. Nobody can know exactly what will happen. Your mentor is not checking whether your predictions come true. They are checking whether you asked the right questions. Who will react? What is their easiest path? And then what? They also check that you chose what to watch before the change, not after it.

### Choosing a decision

Good choices are decisions that change what other people find easy, cheap or rewarding. Think of a new rule at home, a new price for your freelance work, or a target at a part-time job. Each of these makes people react. A decision that only affects you, such as which laptop bag to buy, gives you very little to map.

Choose a decision that has not happened yet, or one that started only recently. Then your map can still change something. If you choose a decision someone else is making, change their name and leave out private details. You are mapping the decision, not judging the person who makes it.

If you cannot think of one, the provided case below works well. So does any decision from your study group, your team or your family about a new rule, a new price or a new target.

### What a good answer contains

- The decision in one sentence, and its goal in one sentence
- The first effect: what the decision does directly
- At least two groups of people, each with the reaction you expect and why it is their easiest path
- At least one chain that reaches a third effect, where each link follows from the one before
- One good surprise, if you can find one
- The effect that is both likely and big, and one change to the plan or a small first try because of it
- One watch measure, a stop rule with a number, and the date you will check

The list is a guide, not a form to fill in. You can write it as short paragraphs, as numbered lines or as a voice note. What matters is that each part is there and that each link in your chain follows from the one before it.

### Common gaps to check before you submit

Read your map once more and look for three common gaps. The first is a chain that jumps. "Fewer customers, so the business closes" skips several steps. Ask yourself what has to happen in between, and write that link down.

The second gap is a reaction that is really a hope. "Customers will understand" is what you want them to do. Ask instead what is easiest for them, and write that.

The third gap is a watch measure that only measures the goal. If your decision is meant to raise sales, sales is your main measure. Your watch measure should be something the side effect you fear would make worse, such as returns, complaints or lost regular customers.

### If you prefer a provided case

You do not need to use your own life. You can use this case instead. Huda, from the home bakery in modules 1 and 6, plans to give 10 percent off every order placed at least two days ahead. Her goal is to plan her baking better and to stop the rush of same-day orders on Thursday nights.

Think about how her regular customers, her new customers and Huda herself will react. Ask what each group's easiest path is now. Follow at least one reaction to a third step. Then plan a small first try and the one thing she should watch.

### How your mentor will read it

Your mentor uses the table below. Each line has three levels. Spelling and grammar do not count. A voice note is welcome if writing is hard today.

| Line | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decision and goal | Both stated, one sentence each | One is missing or vague | Neither is clear |
| Who reacts | Two or more groups, each with an easiest-path reaction | Groups named, but reactions are hopes | No groups, or only yourself |
| The chain | One chain reaches a third effect; each link follows | Stops at a second effect, or one link jumps | First effects only |
| Likely and big | One effect chosen with a reason, and a change or pilot | Chosen with no reason, or no change to the plan | No effect chosen |
| Watch plan | Watch measure, stop rule with a number, and a date | One of the three is missing | No watch measure |

To pass, "The chain" and "Watch plan" must be Met, and no line can be Not yet. If your answer does not pass yet, your mentor replies with one or two short questions about the weakest line. You answer them by adding to your map and submitting it again. Most people need one round of questions, and that is normal.
