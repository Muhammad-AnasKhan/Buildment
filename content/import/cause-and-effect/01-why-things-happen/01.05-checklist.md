## Module 1 checklist

- [ ] I can write an effect exactly, with numbers where I have them
- [ ] I can draw a cause chain back from a problem, one link at a time
- [ ] I can tell a direct cause from a contributing cause
- [ ] I list several possible causes and check them before I choose one
- [ ] I ask for more evidence than "it came first"
- [ ] I answered the questions in chapters 1.2 to 1.4, and submitted my cause chain below

## Learning log

1. **The first guess:** For the problem in your chain below, what did you, or the people around you, first think the cause was? Was it the whole story, part of it, or wrong?
2. **Direct or contributing:** Which link in your chain was hardest to call direct or contributing? Why?
3. **One cause or several:** Think of a recent talk at home, at study or at work about why something happened. Did people name one cause or several? What would you ask now?
4. **After or because:** Which link in your chain rests mostly on timing? What one piece of evidence would make it stronger?

## Your cause chain

This gate asks you to draw one cause chain of your own, in words. The chain is the main habit of this module, and it is also the start of every later module. In module 4 you will dig deeper into root causes. In the capstone you will build a full map of causes. Both begin with the simple chain you write here.

Choose a small problem that you have seen yourself, at study, at work or at home. Good choices are problems whose causes are within your reach: a device that misbehaves, a task that is always late, a habit that keeps slipping. You do not need to share anything personal. If you prefer, use the provided case about Hana's phone below. It has everything you need, and a mentor will read it in the same way.

## What a strong chain looks like

Here is a worked example, so you can see the level of detail that helps. Ziad belongs to a study group of six students. They meet online every Tuesday at 8 in the evening. For the last four weeks, the meeting started 20, 15, 25 and 20 minutes late. Ziad writes the effect exactly: "Our meeting is meant to start at 8:00. In the last four weeks, it started on average 20 minutes late."

Then he asks what made it happen directly. He looks at the call history and sees that the person hosting opened the call late each week, so everyone else waited at an empty link. That is the direct cause, and he marks it "checked", because the call history shows it. Next he asks why the host opened the call late. The group takes turns to host, and each host makes a new link and sends it to the group chat. The chat shows the links arriving between 8:05 and 8:15. That link is also "checked".

He asks once more: why are the links sent so late? The group never agreed on a fixed link or on who prepares the call before 8:00. Each new host assumes someone will remind them, and nobody does. This is a cause Ziad can act on, so he stops here. He also adds a contributing cause: two members join from their phones on the way home, so they would be a few minutes late anyway. He marks it "guess", because he has not asked them yet.

Ziad's chain is short, but it is strong. The effect has numbers. Each link says how it led to the next. He separates the direct cause (the host opening the call late) from a contributing cause (members travelling). He marks what is checked and what is a guess. And he stops at something the group can change: one fixed link, and one person who opens it at 7:55 for a month. Notice also what he avoided. He did not blame the last host as "lazy", and he did not stop at "the meeting is late", which only repeats the problem.

## The provided case: Hana's phone

Hana's phone used to last a full day on one charge. For the last two weeks, the battery has been empty by about 3 in the afternoon. Two weeks ago she installed a new video app. In the same week she was working outside in the sun for a few days. So she turned the screen brightness up to full, and she never turned it down again. The phone is three years old.

Yesterday the phone's battery page said the video app had used 40 percent of the battery, although Hana watched videos for only 20 minutes. When she opened the app's settings, she found that videos keep playing quietly after she leaves the app, until she closes it fully.

If you use this case, build the chain from these facts. Decide which link is the direct cause of the empty battery, which causes are contributing, and which one Hana can act on today. Think also about timing: two things changed in the same week, so "it started after I installed the app" is not enough on its own. Which fact gives you more than timing?

## How your mentor will read it

Your mentor is not looking for one correct cause. Two careful people can draw different chains for the same problem, and both can be useful. Your mentor reads for the habits from this module. A short, honest chain with two guesses marked is better than a long chain that sounds sure about everything. The table shows what your mentor looks for in each part.

| Part | Met | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Effect | Says what happened, when, and how much | Clear, but no number or no time | Vague, such as "my phone is bad" |
| Links | Three or more links, each says how it led to the next | Links are there, but one jumps a step | One arrow from a cause straight to the problem |
| Direct and contributing | Names both, and says why the contributing cause was not enough alone | Names both, with no reason | Does not tell them apart |
| Checked or guess | Every link is marked | Some links are marked | No link is marked |
| Stopping point | Ends at a cause someone can act on | Ends at a symptom, or goes too far back | Ends at a person to blame |

Your chain passes when at least four parts are Met and no part is Not yet. After reading, your mentor replies with one or two short questions, for example: "How do you know this link is true?" You answer them in a few sentences. The gate is complete when you have answered.

If you find, while writing, that your first guess was wrong, say so in the learning log. Changing your mind because of evidence is the skill this course is built on. It is not a mistake.
