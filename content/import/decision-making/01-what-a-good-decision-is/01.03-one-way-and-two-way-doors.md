## Two kinds of doors

Imagine a door. If you walk through and do not like the room, you can walk back out. That is a **two-way door**. Some doors lock behind you. That is a **one-way door**.

Decisions work the same way. Amazon's founder, Jeff Bezos, made this idea popular inside his company: treat easy-to-undo decisions quickly, and slow down only for the ones that are hard to undo.

| Two-way door (easy to undo) | One-way door (hard to undo) |
|---|---|
| Trying a new task tracker for a week | Signing a one-year contract with a client |
| Changing the time of a team meeting | Quitting a stable job for a new startup |
| Choosing a library for a small prototype | Deleting a customer's data |
| Testing a new message template | Publicly criticizing a client online |

## Why this helps

Many people spend the same worry on every decision. They lose hours choosing a meeting tool and then rush a contract. Others decide everything fast, including the things they cannot undo.

A simple habit fixes both: before you decide, ask **"If this goes wrong, how hard is it to undo?"**

- **Easy to undo:** decide quickly, try it, and watch what happens. Waiting is often more costly than a small mistake.
- **Hard to undo:** slow down. Get more information, ask someone you trust, and write down your reasoning.

## Turning a one-way door into a two-way door

Sometimes you can make a hard-to-undo decision easier to undo. This is a powerful skill.

- Instead of a one-year contract, offer a **one-month trial** first.
- Instead of quitting your job for a startup, ask to **work part time** for them for a few weeks.
- Before deleting data, **make a backup**.

You are not avoiding the decision. You are making it safer.

<Callout type="warning" title="Watch out for hidden one-way doors">
Some choices look small but are hard to undo, such as a public message, a promise to a client, or sharing a password. Pause on anything that involves trust, money, data or your reputation.
</Callout>
