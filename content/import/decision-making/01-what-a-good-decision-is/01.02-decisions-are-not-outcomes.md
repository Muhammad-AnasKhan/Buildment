## Two developers, two stories

Omar and Sara both have a client demo at 10:00.

Omar checks the weather and the power schedule the night before. He charges his laptop and phone, downloads everything he needs, and plans to use mobile data if the internet fails. In the morning there is a long, unexpected outage across his area. The mobile network goes down too. He misses the demo.

Sara does no planning. She wakes up late, the power happens to be on, and the demo goes well.

Who made the better decision?

## Quality and result are different

A decision has two parts:

1. **The decision quality:** the information you gathered, the options you considered and your reasoning.
2. **The outcome:** what happened afterwards. This depends on your decision **and** on luck, other people and events you cannot control.

Most people judge only the outcome. That is natural, but it teaches the wrong lesson. If Sara thinks "no planning works fine", she will fail one day when the power is off. If Omar thinks "planning is useless", he will stop doing the thing that helps him most of the time.

<Callout type="tip" title="Ask two questions, not one">
After any important result, ask: "What did I know, and how did I decide?" and then "What was luck?" Change your habits only because of the first answer.
</Callout>

## Good and bad, lucky and unlucky

| | Good result | Bad result |
|---|---|---|
| **Good decision** | Deserved success | Bad luck: keep the habit |
| **Bad decision** | Lucky: change the habit | Deserved failure: learn |

The two boxes that fool us are "lucky" and "bad luck". In both, the result points you to the wrong lesson.

## Explaining a bad result to a client

This idea is also useful when you talk to others. If a result goes wrong, you can explain your reasoning calmly: what you knew, what you chose and why, and what you will do differently next time if anything. Clients and team leads usually respect a clear explanation more than an excuse.

## Common questions

**Q: Does this mean results do not matter?**
A: Results matter a lot. But one result is a weak signal. Look at your decision process, and look at results over many decisions.

**Q: How do I know if it was luck?**
A: Ask whether the thing that went wrong (or right) was something you could reasonably know or control at the time. If not, it was mostly luck.
