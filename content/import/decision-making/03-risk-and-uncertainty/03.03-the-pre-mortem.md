## "Any problems? No? Great."

Hiba is the product lead at a startup with four people: two in Gaza, one in Cairo and one in Lisbon. In three weeks they will launch a new sign-up page for their app.

At the planning call, Hiba shares the plan and asks: "Does anyone see any problems?" Nobody says anything. The call ends in ten minutes. Everyone feels good.

## Why "any problems?" does not work

Asking "Does anyone see problems?" sounds open, but it puts pressure on people. To answer yes, you must criticize a plan that your lead and your team seem happy with. Junior people, new people and people on a weak video call find this especially hard.

There is a second problem. "What might go wrong?" is a vague question. People give vague answers, or none.

## Imagine it already failed

The **pre-mortem** fixes both problems. The name comes from "post-mortem", which in tech teams means a review after something has gone wrong. A pre-mortem happens **before**: you review a failure that has not happened yet.

The psychologist Gary Klein, who studies how people make decisions in real work, made this method popular. The key move is simple. Instead of asking "What **might** go wrong?", you say: "It is six weeks from now. The project **has failed**. Why did it fail?"

Two things change. First, finding problems becomes the task, so raising a doubt is helping, not being negative. Second, when people imagine the failure as a fact, they tend to think of specific, concrete reasons instead of vague worries.

## How to run one

1. **Share the plan.** Make sure everyone knows the goal and the date.
2. **Set the scene.** "Imagine it is one month after launch. The launch was a failure. What happened?"
3. **Write alone first.** Everyone writes their reasons privately for five to ten minutes. This stops the first speaker from shaping what everyone else thinks.
4. **Share in turns.** Each person reads one reason, then the next person, until all reasons are on the list. No arguing yet.
5. **Rate and act.** Rate each reason for likelihood and impact, like in the last chapter. For the biggest ones, change the plan and give each fix an owner.

Hiba runs a pre-mortem the next day. The team writes, among other things:

| Imagined reason for failure | Change to the plan |
|---|---|
| Only Hiba could deploy, and she lost internet on launch day | Teach a second person to deploy this week |
| The page was too heavy for slow mobile data | Test the page on a slow connection before launch |
| Text messages with sign-up codes did not reach some countries | Test sign-up with numbers from each main country |

None of these came up in the first call.

<Callout type="tip" title="Remote teams and different time zones">
You do not need a live call. Share a document with the question, ask everyone to write their reasons within 24 hours without reading others first, then meet briefly to rate and decide. This also helps teammates with unreliable power or internet take part.
</Callout>

## A pre-mortem for one person

Freelancers can do this alone in ten minutes. Write the plan at the top of a page. Then write: "It is Friday. I missed the deadline. Why?" List every reason you can think of. Ibrahim, from the first chapter, would probably have written "power was off most of Tuesday" within the first minute.
