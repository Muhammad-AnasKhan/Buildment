## What it is

To **confirm** something means to show it is true. Confirmation bias is the habit of hunting for confirmation. Once you believe something, your attention works like a filter. Evidence that agrees with you passes through easily. Evidence against you gets explained away, called "a rare case", or simply not noticed.

This is not lying. It happens without you choosing it. That is what makes it dangerous.

## A work scenario

Rania is a freelance web developer. She believes a new client, a small online shop in Germany, will be a great long-term client. On the first call, the owner was friendly and talked about "lots of future work".

Over the next month:

- The client paid the first invoice 20 days late.
- He changed the project scope twice without agreeing a new price.
- He sent messages at midnight his time asking for "quick changes".
- He also sent her a kind message saying she was "the best developer we ever had".

Rania is now deciding whether to sign a six-month contract with him.

## How confirmation bias works on you

Rania did not invent anything. The kind message is real. But one friendly sentence outweighed three warning signs, which she explained away as "just busy". The same pattern shows up everywhere at work:

- A developer is sure a bug is in the server, so she only reads the server logs.
- A team lead likes a new hire, so he remembers her good days and forgets the missed deadlines.
- A founder believes users love a feature, so he quotes the three happy reviews and skips the twenty complaints.

## How to spot it

Watch for these signals in yourself:

| Signal | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| You explain away bad news quickly | "That is just a rare case." |
| You keep collecting support | "I found another article that agrees." |
| You feel annoyed by the other side | "Why is Nour always so negative?" |
| You cannot say what would change your mind | "Nothing could make this a bad idea." |

The last signal is the strongest. If you cannot name any evidence that would change your mind, you are probably not weighing evidence at all.

## One check: look for the opposite

Before an important decision, ask one question:

**"If I were wrong, what would I expect to see? Have I looked for it?"**

Then go and look for it on purpose. Some simple ways:

- Write two lists: evidence for and evidence against. Make the "against" list first.
- Ask one person who disagrees with you to explain their view, and listen without arguing.
- Search for the problem, not the praise: "framework X slow on old phones", not "framework X review".

For Rania, the check takes ten minutes. She writes down the evidence against: late payment, two scope changes, midnight requests. Seeing it on paper, she offers a one-month contract with payment in advance instead of six months. If the client is as good as she hopes, they can extend.
