## A confident mistake

Fadi leads a small team of three developers at an NGO. They are building a mobile app that helps families find aid distribution points. Two months ago, Fadi chose a new framework for the app. He liked it from the start.

Since then, he has read many articles that praise it. When a teammate, Nour, reported that the app was slow on older Android phones, Fadi said it was probably her test phone. When a second report came from a user, he said it was a rare case.

Last week the NGO's partners tested the app on the phones families actually use. It was too slow on most of them. Fadi is not careless or lazy. He is smart and he works hard. So what went wrong?

He fell into a **thinking trap**. He looked for news that agreed with his choice and explained away news that did not. He never decided to do this. It happened on its own.

## Why smart people are not immune

Our brains take shortcuts. Most of the time the shortcuts help: they let us decide fast without thinking through every detail. But some shortcuts fail in the same way, again and again, in almost everyone. Psychologists call these failures **cognitive biases**.

Three facts make biases hard to beat:

- **They feel like normal thinking.** From the inside, a bias feels like being sensible.
- **Intelligence does not switch them off.** A clever person can build a clever argument for a biased choice.
- **Stress makes them stronger.** Tight deadlines, tiredness and pressure push us toward shortcuts. Many of you work through power cuts, long commutes or study on top of a job, so these conditions are real.

The goal of this module is not to remove biases. Nobody can do that. The goal is to **notice** them and use a **simple check** at the moments that matter.

<Callout type="info" title="Biases are about situations, not character">
Having a bias does not make you a bad professional. Everyone has them. What separates good judgment is the habit of checking.
</Callout>

## The four traps in this module

| Trap | In one line |
|---|---|
| **Confirmation bias** | You look for evidence that agrees with you and ignore the rest |
| **Sunk cost trap** | You keep going because of what you already spent, not what is ahead |
| **Anchoring** | The first number you hear pulls your judgment toward it |
| **Overconfidence** | You feel more sure than your evidence allows |

Each chapter follows the same shape: a plain definition, a work scenario, how to spot the trap, and one check you can use. Then you practice naming the traps in a quiz, and you look for one in your own past decisions.
