## Part 1: Anchoring

A ship's **anchor** holds it in one place. Even when the ship moves with the waves, it cannot move far. A number can do the same thing to your thinking. The psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman described this effect in the 1970s: people's estimates stayed close to a starting number, even when that number was clearly random.

## A work scenario

Nadia is a UI designer. A startup in Canada wants a full redesign of its mobile app. On the call, before she can say anything about price, the founder says:

"Our last designer charged us 300 dollars for something similar. What would you charge?"

Nadia had planned to say about 1,500 dollars, based on three weeks of work. Now 1,500 suddenly feels too high. She starts thinking "maybe 500, or 600 at most".

## How to spot anchoring

| Signal | Example |
|---|---|
| The first number came from someone else | A client's budget, an old price, a manager's guess |
| Your own number moved after hearing it | "I was going to say 10 days, but 5 sounds possible." |
| You cannot explain your number without the anchor | "It is close to what they said, so it seems fair." |

## One check: set your own number first

**Before you hear anyone else's number, write down your own, with your reasons.**

For a price: hours of work times your rate, plus any costs. For a deadline: the steps of the task and the time each one takes. If someone gives a number first, go back to your written number and your reasons, and ask: "Has anything about the job actually changed?"

Nadia uses this check. She says calmly: "For this scope, my price is 1,500 dollars. Here is what is included." If the budget is truly small, she can offer a smaller scope, not a smaller price for the same work.

## Part 2: Overconfidence

**Overconfidence** means feeling more sure than your evidence allows. It shows up most in estimates. Most of us think tasks will take less time than they do, even when we have been late on similar tasks before.

## A work scenario

Tariq is a backend developer on a remote team. In the planning meeting, his team lead in Europe asks how long it will take to connect the app to a new payment service. Tariq has never used this service. He says: "Three days, no problem."

He does not think about the time to read the service's documentation, the test account approval, or the power cuts that usually cost him a few hours a week. The task takes nine days. The team had already promised the feature to a client.

## How to spot overconfidence

- You give **one exact number** for something you have never done before.
- You cannot name anything that could make the task take longer.
- Your past estimates for similar work were often late, but this time "feels different".
- You feel sure, but if someone asks "how do you know?", you have no real answer.

## One check: give a range

**Give your estimate as a range, and make the range wide enough that you would be surprised to land outside it.**

A range is honest. It shows both your best guess and how unsure you are. Tariq could have said:

"Between four and ten days. Most likely about six. I have not used this service before, so the first day is reading and testing. I will update you after day two."

Now the team lead can give the client a safe date.

<Callout type="tip" title="Use your own history">
Look at three tasks like this one that you finished before. How long did they really take, compared with your first guess? If you were usually 50 percent over, add that to your new estimate.
</Callout>
