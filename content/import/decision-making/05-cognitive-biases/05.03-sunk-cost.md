## What it is

A cost is **sunk** when it is already spent and cannot be recovered. Like a stone dropped into the sea, it is gone. The **sunk cost trap** (often called the sunk cost fallacy, where "fallacy" means a mistake in reasoning) is when you keep going with a plan only because you have already put so much into it.

It sounds like: "We cannot stop now, we have spent four months on this."

The four months are real, and losing them hurts. But they are gone whether you continue or stop. The only question that matters is: **from today, which choice gives the best future?**

## A work scenario

Khaled and two friends started a small startup. For five months they have built a marketplace app for second-hand textbooks. They have spent their savings on hosting and design, and many late nights on code.

Now the numbers are clear. In three months of testing, only 40 students signed up, and only 6 made a purchase. Meanwhile, several universities have asked them about a simple tool they built on the side for booking study rooms. It took two weeks to build.

Khaled says: "We have put five months into the marketplace. If we switch now, all that work is wasted."

## Why the trap is so strong

Stopping feels like admitting a mistake, and continuing lets you avoid that feeling for now. But continuing does not bring the five months back. If the plan is failing, it only adds more months to the cost.

Note that the past is not useless. What Khaled learned in five months has real value: his skills, his code, and his knowledge of what students want. The trap is not "using the past". The trap is **letting the amount you spent decide for you**.

## How to spot it

| Signal | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| You justify a plan by its past cost | "We have already spent so much on this." |
| You fear "wasting" what is gone | "If we stop, all that work was for nothing." |
| You keep adding a little more | "Just one more month and it will start to work." |
| You would not start it today | You would never choose this plan if you were starting fresh |

Sunk cost shows up in small decisions too: finishing a bad online course because you paid for it, staying in a two-hour meeting that stopped being useful, or fixing old code that would be faster to rewrite.

## One check: the fresh start question

Ask yourself:

**"If I were starting today, with what I know now and nothing spent yet, would I choose this?"**

If the answer is yes, continue with confidence. If the answer is no, the only thing keeping you there is the sunk cost.

For Khaled, the answer is clear. Starting fresh today, he would build the room booking tool. He and his friends decide to pause the marketplace, keep its code in case they need parts of it, and spend the next two months on the booking tool. They also set a date to review the numbers again.

<Callout type="warning" title="Stopping is not the only answer">
The check does not always say "stop". Sometimes the plan is still the best choice. The point is to decide on the future, so you continue for good reasons, not because of the past.
</Callout>
