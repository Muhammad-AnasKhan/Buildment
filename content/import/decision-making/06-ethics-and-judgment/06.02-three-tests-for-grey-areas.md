## Start with a small one

Rami is a freelance developer. His client in Canada pays by the hour. Each week Rami fills in a **timesheet**: a record of the hours he worked on each task.

He estimated 8 hours for a new login page. It took him 5. The client already agreed to the 8 hour estimate.

```predict
prompt: "What should Rami write on his timesheet for the login page?"
options:
  - id: estimate
    label: "8 hours, because the client already agreed to the estimate"
  - id: real-hours
    label: "5 hours, the time he actually worked"
correctOptionId: real-hours
explanation: "A timesheet records what happened, not what was planned. Writing 8 is called padding: adding hours you did not work. Finishing early is good news to share."
```

That one has a clear answer. Many situations do not. For those, you need a quick way to check your thinking before you reply or press send.

## The three tests

Each test is one question. You can ask all three in under a minute.

| Test | The question | What it catches |
|---|---|---|
| **Newspaper** | Would I be comfortable if this were public? | Things that only work if nobody finds out |
| **Mentor** | What would a person I respect say? | Excuses you make for yourself under pressure |
| **Rules** | Does it break a law, a contract or a policy? | Clear limits you may not know or may forget |

**The newspaper test.** Imagine your choice in a news story, or as a screenshot in your team's group chat. If a choice only works when nobody sees it, that is a strong warning sign.

**The mentor test.** Picture one real person whose judgment you respect: a teacher, a senior developer, a mentor in this fellowship. Under pressure we find reasons: "everyone does it", "it is only small". Imagining their face helps you hear how those reasons sound from outside.

**The rules test.** Does it break a law, your contract, or a **policy** (a written rule of a company or platform, such as a freelance platform's terms of service)? You do not need to be a lawyer. Read your own contract, know the main platform rules, and ask when you are not sure.

<Callout type="warning" title="Passing one test is not enough">
Something can be legal and still fail the newspaper test. Something can feel fine and still break your contract. If any test fails, slow down and think again.
</Callout>

## The tests in action

Huda is a designer at a small startup. The founder sends her a competitor's website and says: "Make our landing page exactly like this. Same layout, same text, just our logo."

- **Newspaper:** "Startup copies rival's website word for word." Huda would not like that headline.
- **Mentor:** Her old design teacher would say: learn from good work, but make your own.
- **Rules:** The competitor's text and photos are protected by **copyright**, the law that says creative work belongs to the person who made it. General ideas and common layouts are usually not protected. The exact words and images are.

All three tests point the same way. They also show a better path: Huda can study *why* the page works, such as a clear headline and one main button, and use those ideas in an original design.
