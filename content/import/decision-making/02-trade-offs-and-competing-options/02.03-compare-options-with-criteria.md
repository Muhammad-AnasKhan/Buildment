## Going round in circles

Yousef from chapter 2.1 has named the trade-off. Offer A gives him more money and less flexibility. Offer B gives him more learning and less pay. But every time he thinks about it, he changes his mind. In the morning he wants the money. In the evening, after a power cut, he wants the flexible hours.

When several things matter at once, it helps to put them on paper. A **weighted decision matrix** is a simple way to do that.

## Six steps

1. **Check must-haves first.** A must-have is a condition an option must meet, or it is out. For Yousef, the pay must cover his family's basic costs. Both offers do, so both stay.
2. **Pick three to five criteria.** These are the things that matter for this decision. Fewer is easier to think about.
3. **Give each criterion a weight.** Use 1 for nice to have, 2 for important, 3 for very important. Do this **before** you score.
4. **Score each option from 1 to 5** on each criterion. 5 is best.
5. **Multiply and add.** For each option, multiply each score by its weight, then add the results.
6. **Compare with your gut.** If the result surprises you, ask why before you decide.

## Yousef's criteria and weights

| Criterion | Weight | Why it matters to him |
|---|---|---|
| Pay | 3 | He helps support his family |
| Fits his power hours | 3 | He can only work reliably a few hours a day |
| Learning | 2 | He wants to grow into data dashboards |
| Future work | 2 | A repeat client means steadier income |

Now he scores each offer. Offer A pays more, so it gets 5 for pay. But the fixed daily calls and the tight deadline are hard with his power hours, so it gets only 2 there. Offer B is flexible, so it gets 4 for power hours.

## The worked example

Each cell shows **score x weight = points**.

| Criterion (weight) | Offer A | Offer B |
|---|---|---|
| Pay (3) | 5 x 3 = 15 | 3 x 3 = 9 |
| Power hours (3) | 2 x 3 = 6 | 4 x 3 = 12 |
| Learning (2) | 2 x 2 = 4 | 4 x 2 = 8 |
| Future work (2) | 3 x 2 = 6 | 3 x 2 = 6 |
| **Total** | **31** | **35** |

Offer B wins by 4 points. That is useful, but do not stop here.

## Test the result

Ask: **"What would have to change for the other option to win?"** You can test this by changing one weight at a time and adding up again.

Before you use the matrix below, make a guess: which single weight, changed on its own, could make the two offers tie? Then change the weights and see if you were right.

<InteractiveCanvas slug="weighted-matrix-two-offers" />

Here is one answer. Suppose Yousef's area gets a steady power supply, and he gives power hours a weight of 1 instead of 3. Then Offer A gets 27 and Offer B gets 27: a tie.

So the result depends heavily on his power situation. That tells Yousef what really drives this decision. If his power is unreliable, Offer B fits his life better. He can also explain this to his family or to the clients in one clear sentence.

## Where a matrix can mislead you

A matrix looks exact because it has numbers. But every weight and every score is still your judgment. It organizes your thinking. It does not replace it.

- **Changing weights after scoring.** If you change weights until your favorite wins, the matrix only repeats what you already wanted. Set weights first. Change them later only if you find a real reason, and write that reason down.
- **Hiding deal-breakers.** A high total can hide a failed must-have. That is why must-haves come first.
- **Missing criteria.** If the result feels wrong, you may have left out something that matters. Add it, then score again.
- **Tiny differences.** A gap of one or two points means both options are reasonable. Use other factors, such as how easy each one is to undo.
- **Small decisions.** For a two-way door, a matrix is too slow. Just decide and learn.

<Callout type="tip" title="Keep it small">
Three to five criteria and two or three options is enough. A matrix with twelve rows looks serious but is harder to think about, not easier.
</Callout>
