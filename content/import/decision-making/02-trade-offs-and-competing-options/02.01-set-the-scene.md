## Two offers in one inbox

Yousef is a freelance front-end developer. He has been building his profile for a year, and this week two clients want him at the same time.

**Offer A** is an online shop for a small business in Canada. The pay is good. The deadline is three weeks. The client wants a video call every morning at 9:00 their time, which is late afternoon for Yousef. He knows the tools well.

**Offer B** is a dashboard for an NGO (a non-profit group that works for a social cause) that tracks food deliveries. The pay is lower. The deadline is six weeks and the hours are flexible. It uses a charting library he has wanted to learn. The NGO says there may be more work next year.

Yousef cannot do both well. In his area, electricity comes for only a few hours each day, and he never knows exactly when. The fixed daily calls in Offer A worry him. The lower pay in Offer B worries him too.

Neither offer is free. Each one costs him something.

## Why gut feeling is not enough

Most people choose between options like these with a feeling. Feelings are useful, but they have two problems:

- They are easy to push around. The biggest number, the last message you read, or your mood that day can decide for you.
- They are hard to explain. If a client, teammate or family member asks "why this one?", "it felt right" is not a strong answer.

In this module you learn two simple habits. First, **name the trade-off**: say clearly what you get and what you give up. Second, **compare options with criteria**: decide what matters, how much, and score each option against it. You do not stop using your judgment. You make it visible.

<Callout type="info" title="This is not only for big decisions">
The same habits help with small team choices too: which tool to use, which feature to build first, or which task to drop when time runs out.
</Callout>

## Key words

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Trade-off** | Getting more of one thing means getting less of another |
| **Opportunity cost** | The value of the best option you did not choose |
| **Criteria** | The things you judge options by, such as cost or time. One is a criterion |
| **Weight** | How much one criterion matters compared with the others |
| **Must-have** | A condition an option must meet, or it is out |
| **Decision matrix** | A table that scores each option on each criterion |
