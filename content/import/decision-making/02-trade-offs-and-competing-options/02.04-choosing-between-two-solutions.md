## The problem

Rana, Daniel and Samir run a three-person startup. They build a booking app for small clinics. Rana leads the work from Gaza, Daniel writes the backend from Amman, and Samir handles design part time.

A clinic tells them that many patients miss their appointments. It asks for reminders. The team sees two solutions:

- **Option A: SMS reminders** through a paid SMS service. They work on any phone, even without internet. Every message costs money.
- **Option B: Push notifications** inside the app. They are free to send. But they only reach patients who installed the app and are online at the right time.

Daniel says: "Push is free. Easy choice." Rana is not so sure. Here is how they decide.

## Step 1: Name the problem, not the solution

The problem is not "we need SMS" or "we need push". The problem is: **fewer patients should miss their appointments.** Writing it this way keeps the team focused on the goal, not on a favorite tool.

## Step 2: Check must-haves

The clinic gives two must-haves: the reminders must be ready within a month, and the monthly cost must fit the clinic's small budget. Both options pass, although SMS uses most of the budget. Both stay in.

## Step 3: Get criteria from stakeholders

Rana lists who is affected and what each one cares about:

| Stakeholder | What they care about |
|---|---|
| Clinic | Fewer missed appointments, low cost |
| Patients | Reminders that reach their phone, even a basic one |
| The team | Build time, and little maintenance later |

From this, the team picks four criteria: **reach**, **monthly cost**, **build time** and **maintenance**.

## Step 4: Weight and score

The clinic says many of its older patients use basic phones without apps. So push notifications would miss them. The team scores with that in mind. Remember that 5 is always best: for cost, a high score means cheap.

| Criterion (weight) | SMS | Push |
|---|---|---|
| Reach (3) | 5 x 3 = 15 | 2 x 3 = 6 |
| Monthly cost (2) | 2 x 2 = 4 | 5 x 2 = 10 |
| Build time (2) | 4 x 2 = 8 | 3 x 2 = 6 |
| Maintenance (1) | 4 x 1 = 4 | 3 x 1 = 3 |
| **Total** | **31** | **25** |

## Step 5: Name the trade-off

"If we choose SMS, we get more reach and a simpler build, and we pay more every month." Now everyone can see the price of the choice, including Daniel.

## Step 6: Look for a third option

Two options are a starting point, not a limit. Samir asks: "What if we send a push notification to patients who have the app, and an SMS only to those who do not?" This keeps most of the reach and cuts the SMS cost. It takes a little more build time. A mix of two options is often better than either one alone.

## Step 7: Make it easy to undo

The SMS service charges per message with no long contract. That makes it a two-way door. The team agrees to try the mixed approach with one clinic for one month, then look at the numbers.

## Step 8: Write it down

Rana sends the clinic two sentences:

> "We recommend push notifications for patients with the app and SMS for everyone else, because this reaches almost all patients at a lower cost than SMS alone. We will try it with your clinic for one month and check whether missed appointments go down."

<Callout type="tip" title="Short is strong">
A recommendation that fits in two or three sentences is easier for a busy client to accept. Keep the matrix ready in case they ask how you got there.
</Callout>

## Your turn

Leila coordinates volunteers for an NGO that delivers school supplies. Right now she calls each volunteer to fill weekly shifts, which takes her hours. She sees two options:

- **Option A:** a shared online spreadsheet where volunteers add their own names to shifts.
- **Option B:** a messaging group where Leila posts the shifts and volunteers reply.

Some volunteers have weak internet. Some are not comfortable with spreadsheets. Everyone already uses the messaging app.
